The guide

The story

Listen first. Beneath the wind, you may hear the River Lossie running behind the old precinct walls, and jackdaws calling from the high stonework. The ground is soft grass now, where a stone floor once carried processions. The walls around you are warm red and honey sandstone, rough with lichen. Overhead there is only sky. This is Elgin Cathedral, and for most of its life it was roofed, painted, and filled with candlelight and chant.

The story begins in the early thirteenth century. Around 1224, the bishops of Moray moved their seat here, to a site beside the river at the edge of the town. Before that, the cathedral of this diocese had stood at Spynie, a short distance to the north. The new church grew in stages. It was built to show that this corner of northeast Scotland, the province of Moray, was a confident and well-organised part of the medieval church.

At its height, the cathedral earned a beautiful nickname. People called it the Lantern of the North. The name fits what you can still see. Look at the tall windows at the eastern end, set in two tiers, one above the other. Imagine them full of coloured glass, with the low northern sun coming through. In a region of small stone churches and farmland, this building must have seemed full of light.

The church you see is not the first version. A fire damaged the cathedral in 1270, and the clergy rebuilt it on a grander scale. That is one reason the eastern end feels so ambitious. The masons here worked in the Gothic style, which means pointed arches, tall slender openings, and walls that feel lighter than the heavy round-arched churches that came before. Run your eye along the mouldings around the windows. They are cut in layers, crisp even after centuries of rain.

Then comes the most dramatic chapter. In 1390, the cathedral was burned on purpose. The man responsible was Alexander Stewart, Earl of Buchan, a son of the Scottish king. History remembers him by a fierce nickname, the Wolf of Badenoch. He had a long dispute with the Bishop of Moray over land and over his own private life, and the bishop had excommunicated him. In revenge, his men set fire to the town of Elgin and to this cathedral.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Wolf did not lose everything for this act. He later did public penance and was received back into the church. The damage, however, took a very long time to repair. The bishops appealed for funds, and rebuilding went on for many decades. When you notice small changes in the stonework, slightly different colours or styles side by side, you may be looking at the work of those repair campaigns.

Now walk toward the octagonal room off the north side of the choir. This is the chapter house, where the clergy met to manage the business of the cathedral. It is one of the best-preserved parts of the site, and it still has its stone vault. Stand in the centre and look up. A single clustered pillar rises from the floor and opens out into ribs, like the branches of a stone tree, holding up the whole ceiling.

The chapter house is a good place to pause and listen. Stone rooms like this carry sound in a particular way. Even a quiet voice comes back to you. Around the walls you will see seats set into the stone, where the canons of the cathedral once sat in order of rank. Look closely at the carved heads and small details on the capitals. Some are worn smooth, but others still show faces and foliage cut by medieval hands.

The cathedral survived fire and war, but it did not survive the Reformation. In 1560, Scotland broke with the authority of the Pope, and great Catholic churches lost their purpose. Services here stopped. A few years later, the lead was stripped from the roof on official orders, to be sold. Without lead, the timbers rotted and rain came in. The building slowly became a ruin, used as a quarry for stone by people in the town.

The final blow came in 1711. On Easter Sunday that year, the great central tower collapsed. It brought down much of the nave, the long western part of the church where ordinary worshippers once stood. That is why the middle of the cathedral feels so open today. You are standing in the space the tower once filled. Look at the broken ends of the walls and try to picture the height of what fell.

The ruin owes much to one unlikely guardian. In the early nineteenth century, a local man named John Shanks, a shoemaker by trade, became the keeper of the cathedral. He spent years clearing away huge quantities of rubbish and fallen stone by hand. His work helped make the ruin readable again, and visitors began to come. Today the site is cared for by Historic Environment Scotland, which looks after many of the country's historic places.

Before you leave, walk to the west end and face the great doorway between the two towers. This is the formal entrance, framed by deep, layered arches. Above it rose a large window. The towers themselves still stand to a good height, and inside them you can see a collection of carved stones from the site. Look out too for the old grave slabs and memorials in the grass. Some are worn, some still carry clear lettering and carved skulls.

One more surprise sits among those carved stones. The collection here includes a Pictish cross slab, far older than the cathedral itself. The Picts were the people who lived in northern and eastern Scotland in the early medieval centuries, before the kingdom of Scotland took shape. The slab was found in Elgin and brought here for safety. It is a reminder that this was sacred ground in the imagination of the region long before the bishops arrived.

As you walk away, turn back once. Notice how the light falls on the red stone, and how the empty windows frame the sky. Listen again for the river and the birds. Very little here is complete. Yet the outline of the church is still clear... the long walls, the towers, the vaulted room to the north. Take that outline with you, and picture it roofed and lit, the Lantern of the North.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the chapter house first, before groups fill it.
Bring a waterproof layer; the ruin has little shelter.
Walk the outside of the precinct to see the river.
Allow extra time for the carved stones inside the towers.
Come late afternoon for warm light on the sandstone.
