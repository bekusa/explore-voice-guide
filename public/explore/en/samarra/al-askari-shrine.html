The guide

The story

Listen first. The soft scuff of socks and bare feet on polished stone. A low murmur of prayer that rises and falls like a slow tide. Somewhere a child laughs and is quickly hushed. Then look up. Above you, a great dome of gold catches the Iraqi sun and throws it back across the flat roofs of Samarra. This is the Al-Askari Shrine, one of the holiest places in the world for Shia Muslims, and you are standing in its courtyard.

The shrine holds the tombs of two men. The first is Ali al-Hadi, the tenth Imam in Twelver Shia belief. The second is his son, Hasan al-Askari, the eleventh Imam. Twelver Shia Muslims believe in a line of twelve Imams, leaders descended from the Prophet Muhammad through his cousin and son-in-law Ali. For millions of believers, these men are not distant historical figures. They are guides, intercessors, and family. That is why pilgrims travel here from Iran, Lebanon, Pakistan, the Gulf, and every corner of Iraq.

To understand why the Imams lie here, you need to know what Samarra once was. In the ninth century, the Abbasid caliphs moved their capital up the Tigris from Baghdad to this site. For a few decades, Samarra was the seat of an empire that stretched across much of the Islamic world. The city grew fast and wide, with palaces, barracks, racecourses, and enormous mosques spread along the riverbank. Today, the ruins of that imperial city are listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The name al-Askari comes from an Arabic word linked to the army, or a military camp. Samarra was a garrison city, built to house the caliph's soldiers. The Abbasid rulers saw the Imams as a political threat, because many people looked to them as rightful leaders. So the tenth Imam was brought to Samarra and kept under close watch. He and his son became known by the name of the camp where they lived, almost as a reminder of their confinement.

Both Imams died in Samarra in the second half of the ninth century. They were buried in their own house, which slowly became a place of visitation. Over the following centuries, rulers and patrons from many dynasties added to the site. They built walls and gateways, then courtyards and minarets, and finally domes. What you see now is not the work of one builder or one era. It is many layers of devotion, placed one over another across more than a thousand years.

The shrine also holds the graves of two women who are deeply honoured here. One is Hakima, the sister of the tenth Imam. According to Shia tradition, she was present at the birth of the twelfth Imam and helped raise him. The other is Narjis, the mother of that twelfth Imam. Pilgrims often stop at their resting places too, and you may notice women in particular lingering there, touching the grilles and offering quiet prayers.

That twelfth Imam brings us to the second surprise. Close to the main shrine is a smaller sanctuary built over a cellar. In Twelver Shia belief, the twelfth Imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, entered a state called occultation. This means he is believed to be alive but hidden from the world by God, and that he will return one day to bring justice. Many believers associate this cellar with his disappearance. For pilgrims, it is a place of hope as much as of mourning.

Now turn your attention back to the dome. Its covering of gold tiles was completed in the early twentieth century, and for generations it was one of the largest golden domes anywhere. On a clear day, travellers on the road could see it shining from far across the plain. Inside the shrine, the walls and ceilings are covered in mirror mosaic, thousands of small glass pieces cut and set by hand. When the lamps are lit, the light breaks into countless points and fills the room.

The recent history of this place is painful, and it matters to everyone you see here. In February 2006, bombs placed inside the shrine destroyed the golden dome. The attack shocked Iraq and the wider Muslim world. It set off a wave of sectarian violence that cost many thousands of lives across the country. In 2007, a second attack brought down the two golden minarets. For a time, the shrine stood broken, its gold scattered in the rubble.

What followed was a long and careful rebuilding. Engineers, craftsmen, and religious authorities worked together to restore the dome, the minarets, and the decorated interiors. Much of the gold and mirror work had to be made again from the beginning. The rebuilt shrine reopened in stages over the following years. So when you look at the dome today, you are looking at something both very old and quite new. The form is traditional, but many of the tiles are modern.

Watch how people move through the space. Pilgrims often pause at the threshold, touch the doorframe, and kiss their fingers before they enter. Around the tombs, the silver and gold grilles are polished bright by countless hands. Some visitors press their foreheads against the metal. Others tie small pieces of cloth or simply stand and weep. Families sit together on carpets in the courtyard, sharing tea and bread. This is a place of worship, but it is also a place of gathering.

Outside the shrine walls, Samarra continues its ordinary life. Small shops sell prayer beads, prayer stones made of pressed clay, and framed images of the Imams. The streets nearby carry the smell of grilled meat and strong sweet tea. Security is visible, and checkpoints are part of the journey here, a reminder of the years of violence. A short drive away stands the spiral minaret of the old Great Mosque, a reminder of the Abbasid city that came before.

As you stand here now, take a moment before you leave. Feel the cool stone under your feet, even on a hot day. Listen to the voices in prayer, in Arabic, Persian, Urdu, and more. Look once more at the gold above you, rebuilt after it fell. When you walk back through the gates, you may notice how the noise of the street returns slowly. And you may carry a little of this quiet with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly; women should bring an abaya or chador.
Expect several security checkpoints before reaching the shrine.
Leave shoes at the shoe depository near the entrance.
Ask staff before photographing pilgrims or the tomb chamber.
Visit outside major pilgrimage dates for a calmer courtyard.
