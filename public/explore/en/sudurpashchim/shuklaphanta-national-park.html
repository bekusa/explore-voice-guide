The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry rustle moves through grass taller than your head. Somewhere out in the open a bird calls, sharp and repeated. Then comes a soft, heavy sound, like many hooves shifting in damp ground. The light here is wide and pale. It falls on a sea of grass that runs flat toward a line of dark forest, and on a clear day, toward the faint blue shape of distant hills.

You are in Shuklaphanta National Park, in the far southwestern corner of Nepal. This is Kanchanpur District, part of Sudurpashchim Province. It is the edge of the country in every sense. To the west, the Mahakali River marks the border with India. To the south, the forests continue across the line into Indian protected land. Few international travellers reach this corner, and that is part of its character.

The name tells you what matters here. In Nepali, a phanta is an open grassland. Shukla Phanta is the name of the great central meadow, and it gave its name to the whole park. Most people imagine Nepal as mountains and snow. But this is the Terai, the low, flat belt of land along the southern border. Here the ground is hot, wet in the monsoon, and very fertile.

For much of its modern life, this land was protected as a wildlife reserve. It became a national park in 2017. The park covers roughly three hundred square kilometres. That is a modest size on a map. Yet it holds one of the largest open grasslands left anywhere along the southern foot of the Himalaya. In most of the Terai, grassland like this has been turned into farmland. Here, it survived.

And here is the first surprise. The animal that defines this park is not the tiger. It is the swamp deer, also called the barasingha. The word means twelve-tined, a reference to the many points on the male's antlers. Shuklaphanta holds what is widely considered the largest single population of swamp deer in the world. On the open meadow, they can gather in herds of hundreds. Some counts have recorded far more.

Take a moment to picture what that means. Across much of their old range in South Asia, swamp deer have disappeared. They need wet, open grassland, and that habitat has shrunk for centuries. In this park, you may see a whole herd move across the meadow at once. The stags carry wide, branching antlers. Their coats look golden brown in low sun. It is a scene that has almost vanished from the rest of the subcontinent.

The grass itself deserves attention. Some species grow taller than an adult person, even taller than a rider on an elephant's back. This tall grass is a home, not empty space. It shelters the hispid hare, a small, rare rabbit-like animal that few people ever see. It also shelters the Bengal florican, a bustard, a heavy ground bird. The florican is one of the most threatened birds in South Asia, and this park is one of its strongholds.

Here is the second surprise. The male Bengal florican is usually hard to find, because it hides in the grass. But in the breeding season, the male performs a display flight. He jumps up above the grass, flutters with white wings flashing, and drops back down. Bird watchers travel a long way for that brief sight. If you are here in the right season, watch the open meadow for a sudden flash of white.

Larger animals live here too. Bengal tigers hunt in the forest and along the grassland edges. Asian elephants move through the park, and sometimes across the border into India and back. Leopards, sloth bears, and many kinds of deer share the land. In recent decades, conservation authorities also moved a small number of greater one-horned rhinoceros here from other parks in Nepal. The aim was to spread the population, so one disaster could not wipe it out.

The forest around the meadows is mostly sal, a hardwood tree common across the Terai. Sal trees grow tall and straight, with large, broad leaves. In the dry season, those leaves drop and crunch underfoot. Along the rivers and wetlands, the forest changes. You find marshes, small lakes, and channels where crocodiles, otters, and wading birds live. The water is what keeps the grassland green after the rains end.

One of those water bodies carries a story. Locals speak of a lake called Rani Tal, which means the Queen's Lake. According to popular tradition, it is linked to an old local ruler and his queen, and some people point to old brick remains nearby as the traces of a fort. Treat this as local memory rather than settled history. Still, it is a reminder that people lived and ruled on this land long before it became a park.

People still live close to it today. The Rana Tharu are an Indigenous community of this far western Terai. Their villages sit near the park's edges, and their traditions are tied to this landscape of grass, forest, and river. Many Tharu communities in the Terai have long used tall grass for roofs and walls, and wild plants for food and medicine. Life beside a park with tigers and elephants is not always easy, and that tension is part of the story too.

Now look again at what is in front of you. Notice the colour of the grass, and how it changes as the wind moves across it. Listen for birds, for hooves, for the quiet that settles between them. When you leave, look back once at the open meadow. Few places in Nepal still look like this. For a little while, you stood in one of them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in early morning for the most animal activity.
Bring binoculars to scan the open grassland.
Wear muted colours to blend into the landscape.
Carry water and sun protection on the open meadow.
Visit after the monsoon when tracks are passable.
