The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across open grass, and somewhere a bell rings from a small shrine. The ground under your boots is soft and springy, cushioned by centuries of roots and fallen needles. Mist often drifts across this plateau in the morning, then lifts all at once to show rolling meadows ringed by dark forest. You are high in the hills of far-western Nepal, and the air is thin, cool, and smells of damp earth and pine.

This is Khaptad National Park, in Sudurpashchim Province. The park sits where four districts meet: Bajhang, Bajura, Doti, and Achham. It was protected in the 1980s, and it covers a little over two hundred square kilometres. Much of the visitor area lies on a broad plateau around three thousand metres above sea level. The plateau is not a single summit. It is a wide, rolling tableland of grass and woodland, lifted high above the deep river valleys on every side.

The open meadows have a local name: patans. Each patan is a natural clearing of grass and wildflowers, edged by trees. Local people often say there are twenty-two of them, and walking from one to the next is the main rhythm of a visit here. In summer and during the monsoon, these meadows fill with flowers in yellow, purple, and white. In late autumn, the grass turns gold and brown. In winter, snow can cover the whole plateau.

Now look toward the edges of the clearings. The forest changes as you climb. Lower down, there are pines and broadleaf trees. Higher up, you find oak, rhododendron, and fir. In spring, the rhododendrons flower in shades of red and pink. Moss and lichen hang from many branches, which shows how much moisture this ridge catches from passing clouds. Walk into the trees, and the light drops quickly to a green shade.

Here is the first thing many visitors do not expect. Khaptad is not only a nature reserve. For many Nepalis, it is a place of pilgrimage. The reason is one man, known as Khaptad Baba. He was a Hindu hermit and teacher who came to this plateau and lived here alone for about fifty years. He studied, meditated, and received visitors who climbed up to ask for his advice.

Khaptad Baba lived simply, in a small hut among the trees. Over the decades, his reputation spread far beyond these hills. Ordinary villagers, officials, and even members of the royal family made the long walk to see him. He spoke about health, spiritual practice, and living in balance with nature. He died in the 1990s. His ashram, his small hermitage, still stands, and it remains one of the most visited spots in the park.

When you reach the ashram, slow down. It is a quiet place, and people treat it with respect. Pilgrims leave offerings and sit in silence. Many believe that his long presence here is one reason the forest stayed so well protected. Whether or not you share that belief, the effect is real. The plateau feels calm, and visitors tend to lower their voices without being asked.

There are other sacred sites across the plateau. At a place called Tribeni, three small streams join, and pilgrims bathe and pray there. There is also a lake, Khaptad Daha, and a shrine known as Sahasra Linga, a name that refers to a thousand symbols of the god Shiva. Each of these places carries local stories. Some are legend rather than history, but they shape how people move through this landscape.

The second surprise comes once a year. During the full moon of the Ganga Dashahara festival, in early summer, thousands of pilgrims climb to the plateau for a great gathering. Families walk for days from villages across the far west. They camp in the meadows, bathe at Tribeni, sing, and trade. For a few days, this remote and silent place fills with smoke from cooking fires, music, and conversation. Then the crowds leave, and the quiet returns.

Now think about the wildlife. Khaptad is home to Himalayan black bears, leopards, barking deer, and wild boar. Monkeys move through the trees, and many species of birds live here. Look out for the Himalayan monal, Nepal's national bird, called danphe in Nepali. The male has shining feathers in green, blue, copper, and purple. You may hear its call before you see it, a sharp whistle from the forest edge.

The plateau is also known for its plants. Botanists have recorded many flowering species here, and a large number are used in traditional medicine. Local healers have collected herbs from these slopes for generations. Khaptad Baba himself was said to value these plants for health. As you walk, you may notice small herbs at your feet, low to the ground and easy to miss. Please leave them where they grow.

Getting here is part of the story. There is no road onto the plateau itself. Most visitors walk up for at least a day or two from towns such as Silgadhi in Doti or Chainpur in Bajhang. The trails climb steeply through villages, terraced fields, and forest. That effort is one reason the park feels so untouched. Far-western Nepal receives only a small share of the country's visitors, so you may walk for hours and meet nobody.

The weather changes fast at this height. A clear blue morning can turn into cloud and rain by the afternoon. The temperature drops quickly when the sun goes down. Facilities on the plateau are basic, and many walkers carry their own food or arrange meals with local hosts. This simplicity is part of the experience. Here, the day follows the light, the weather, and the distance between one meadow and the next.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Notice the shape of the meadow around you, and the line where the grass meets the trees. Listen for wind, water, a bird, or a distant bell. If the clouds open, look out across the ridges that fall away toward the valleys below. As you walk on, the trail will carry you through the forest again. Take the quiet with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan at least a day of hiking to reach the plateau.
Pack warm layers for cold evenings at altitude.
Carry rain gear even on a clear morning.
Keep your voice low near the ashram.
Arrange food in advance because plateau facilities are basic.
