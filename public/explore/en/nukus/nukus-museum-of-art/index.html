The guide

The story

Outside, the air is dry and pale with dust from the steppe. Inside, the light changes. The rooms are cool and quiet, and the walls are covered in colour: deep reds, burnt oranges, sudden blues. You are in Nukus, the capital of Karakalpakstan, in the far west of Uzbekistan. Few places feel more remote. And yet this building holds one of the most remarkable collections of twentieth-century art anywhere in the former Soviet Union.

Most visitors know this place by another name: the Savitsky Museum. It carries the name of Igor Savitsky, a painter and collector born in Kyiv and trained in Moscow. He first came to this region around 1950 as an artist with the Khorezm archaeological expedition. Khorezm was an ancient civilisation of oases and fortresses along the lower Amu Darya river. Savitsky came to draw ruins and artefacts. Then he stayed.

At first, his interest was local. He collected Karakalpak jewellery, embroidered robes, carpets and the felt and woven pieces of nomadic life. He worried that these objects were disappearing as Soviet life changed the villages. When the museum was founded in the 1960s, Savitsky became its director. He gave it a clear purpose... to preserve what others were throwing away.

Then his collecting took a far more dangerous turn. Under Stalin, Soviet art had one approved style: socialist realism. That means heroic workers, happy farmers and clear political messages. Many artists of the 1920s and 1930s had worked differently. They experimented with bold colour, broken forms and personal visions. Their work was called formalist, a serious accusation at the time. Some artists were arrested. Many were simply forgotten, and their canvases sat rolled up in attics and storerooms.

Savitsky went looking for those canvases. Through the 1960s and 1970s he travelled to Moscow, Leningrad and Tashkent. He visited widows, children and elderly artists, and he asked to see what they had hidden. Often the families were glad that someone cared at all. Savitsky brought paintings, drawings and sketches back to Nukus — thousands of them. Distance helped him. Nukus was a long way from the critics and officials in Moscow.

There is a popular story about how he kept the collection safe. According to the tale, Savitsky sometimes gave risky paintings harmless or patriotic titles, so inspectors would see nothing worth worrying about. Treat this as legend rather than documented fact. What is certain is that he took real risks. He bought work that the official art world did not want, and he did it with stubbornness and very little money.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The rooms around you show only a small part of what the museum owns. The full collection runs to tens of thousands of objects, and most of it lives in storage. So the walls change over time. What you see today may not be what the next visitor sees. Each display is a selection from a much larger archive of forgotten Soviet modernism.

As you walk, look for a particular kind of painting. Some of the most striking works come from artists who settled in Central Asia in the early Soviet years. They arrived from Russia and Ukraine and found something new here: hard sunlight, flat desert horizons, teahouses, camels and bazaars. Their style mixed European modernism with Central Asian subjects. Art historians sometimes call this the Turkestan avant-garde. You will see it in hot, flattened colour and simplified figures.

One name to look for is Usto Mumin. He was born Alexander Nikolaev in Russia and moved to Uzbekistan. He took a local name, which means master Mumin, and painted young men and dancers in a style that borrows from old Persian miniatures. The faces are calm and stylised, almost like icons. Another painting that visitors often seek out is a powerful bull by Vasily Lysenko, heavy and dark, filled with menace.

The second surprise is about the people who painted these works. Many never saw their paintings in a public gallery. Some died in poverty or in the Soviet labour camps. For decades, their names did not appear in art histories. This museum kept their work together and gave it a home. In recent years, exhibitions abroad and a documentary film called The Desert of Forbidden Art have brought international attention to the collection.

Do not rush past the other halls. The archaeological rooms show objects from ancient Khorezm, including pottery and fragments found by the same expeditions that first brought Savitsky here. The Karakalpak collection shows richly decorated jewellery, tent hangings and clothing. These rooms explain the world Savitsky first fell in love with. Without them, the paintings upstairs make less sense. The museum began as a love of this land.

It is also worth remembering where Nukus stands. The city lies not far from what was once the Aral Sea. In the late twentieth century, the rivers that fed it were diverted for cotton fields, and the sea shrank dramatically. Life in the region became harder. Against that background, this museum feels even more striking. It is a place of careful preservation in a landscape marked by loss.

Savitsky died in 1984, before the Soviet Union ended and before the wider world learned what he had saved. Today, his collection is a record of artists who chose their own way, and of one man who refused to let their work disappear. When you leave, step back into the bright light outside. Notice the wide streets and the flat horizon. That same sun and space once inspired the painters on the walls behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least two hours for the art halls.
Ask staff about photography rules before taking pictures.
Visit the Karakalpak applied arts rooms before the paintings.
Bring water and sun protection for Nukus streets.
Check which works are currently displayed before your visit.
