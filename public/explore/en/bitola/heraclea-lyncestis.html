The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere a cricket starts up in the dry grass. Pale limestone blocks lie scattered across the slope, warm to the touch when the sun is out. To the west, the long shoulder of Mount Pelister fills the horizon. You are standing at the edge of Bitola, in the south of North Macedonia. These quiet ruins were once a city of soldiers, merchants and bishops. Its name was Heraclea Lyncestis.

The name holds two clues. Heraclea honours Heracles, the hero Romans called Hercules. The royal house of ancient Macedon claimed him as an ancestor. Lyncestis comes from Lynkestis, the old name of this wide, fertile plain. Tradition credits the founding of the city to Philip the Second of Macedon, father of Alexander the Great, in the middle of the fourth century before Christ. Philip was joining upland regions like this one to his growing kingdom. A city here helped him hold the plain.

Very little of that early Macedonian town is visible today. What you see around you mostly comes from later centuries, when Rome ruled the Balkans. That is the first surprise for many visitors. They arrive expecting the world of Alexander, and they find a Roman and early Christian city instead. Each age built on top of the one before. Stone from older buildings was often cut and reused. So the ground here holds layers, stacked like pages in a closed book.

The reason Rome cared about Heraclea was a road. The Via Egnatia was a great Roman highway. It crossed the Balkans from the Adriatic coast towards the Aegean and, in time, Constantinople. Heraclea sat along this route. Soldiers marched through. Officials travelled with letters and orders. Traders moved goods between east and west. A town on a road like this could grow rich from the people who stopped for a night, a meal or a market day.

Now walk towards the theatre, cut into the hillside. Its curved rows of seats face a stage area below. Scholars usually date it to the second century, in the time of Emperor Hadrian, a ruler who travelled widely and spent heavily on public buildings. Sit on one of the stone steps. Notice how sound carries. A voice from the stage reaches the upper rows without much effort. Roman builders understood this well, and they shaped the curve to help it.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In later Roman times, tastes changed. Plays gave way to harder entertainment, with wild animals and armed fighters. Archaeologists believe the theatre was adapted for these shows, with changes to protect spectators from the arena floor. So the same stones that once heard comedy and tragedy also saw blood on the sand. Today the theatre has a gentler life again. In summer, concerts and performances are sometimes held here in the open air.

Beyond the theatre, the streets of the town open out. Look for the low walls of the Roman baths. Public baths were the social centre of a Roman town, a place to wash, exercise and talk business. Nearby you can trace the lines of colonnades and paved areas. Try to picture roofs, painted plaster and crowds. The empty space between the walls was once filled with noise, steam and the smell of wood smoke from the furnaces.

As Christianity spread through the empire, Heraclea changed again. The city became the seat of a bishop. Historical sources record bishops of Heraclea taking part in church councils in the early Christian centuries. Several churches rose inside the town. Archaeologists have uncovered a small basilica, a large basilica and a building identified as the bishop's residence. A basilica, in this sense, is a long hall-shaped church, divided inside by rows of columns.

The real treasure of Heraclea lies on the floor of the large basilica. It is a mosaic, made from thousands of small coloured stones, laid in the fifth or sixth century. It shows a whole world in miniature. Trees bend with fruit. Birds and animals fill the scene, among them deer, peacocks and dogs. At the centre, a vessel pours water. Many scholars read it as a picture of creation, or of paradise, framed by a border that suggests the sea.

And here is the second surprise. The mosaic is fragile, so it is not always on view. To protect it from frost and weather, it is often covered for part of the year, especially in the colder months. If you find it uncovered today, take your time. Look closely at the faces of the animals, the leaves, the shading in the stones. If it is covered, you are seeing the same careful protection that has kept it alive for so long.

No city lasts forever, and Heraclea had a hard end. In the late fifth century, Ostrogoths, a Germanic people moving through the Balkans, threatened and plundered the town. Later, Slavic groups settled across the region, and the old Roman order faded. Earthquakes and neglect did the rest. By the end of the sixth century, city life here had largely stopped. Earth and grass slowly covered the streets, and the ruins waited.

Excavations brought the city back into the light, especially through the twentieth century, and the work continues. Many finds from the site, including sculpture and inscriptions, are kept in museums, so part of Heraclea now lives in display cases rather than on this hillside. Even so, the site gives you something a museum cannot. You can walk the same ground, feel the same slope underfoot, and see the same mountain that the people of this city saw every day.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Look from the theatre seats to the broken columns, and then out to Pelister. The light shifts across the stone as clouds move. A few steps away, modern Bitola carries on with traffic, bells and coffee cups. Behind you lies a town that lasted for centuries and then went quiet. As you walk back towards the gate, listen again to the gravel under your feet.
