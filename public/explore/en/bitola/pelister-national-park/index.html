The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind moves through the pines with a long, soft rush, like distant water. Underfoot, the path changes from dry needles to granite grit, then to grass that springs back after each step. The air is cooler here than in the town below, and it carries the sharp, clean smell of resin. Above you, the mountain rises in broad, grey shoulders. This is Pelister National Park, on the slopes of Baba Mountain, just above the city of Bitola.

Pelister holds a special place in North Macedonia. It was declared a national park in 1948, which makes it the oldest national park in the country. It is also one of the oldest protected mountain areas in the Balkans. The park takes its name from its highest summit, Pelister peak, which rises to 2,601 metres. For people in Bitola, the mountain is part of daily life. They see its outline from the city streets, and they read the weather from the clouds on its crest.

The reason for the early protection is all around you, in the trees themselves. Look closely at the pine branches. Many of them belong to the Macedonian pine, which locals call molika. Its needles grow in bundles of five, soft and slender, with a faint blue-green colour. This tree is a relict, which means a survivor from a much older period of Earth's history, before the great Ice Ages. Today it grows naturally only in a few Balkan mountain ranges, and Pelister holds some of its finest forests.

Here is something many visitors miss. Botanists often treat Pelister as a classic home of the molika pine. The species is so closely linked to this mountain that the tree has become a symbol of the whole park. When you stand in a molika forest, you are among trees whose ancestors grew here when the climate of Europe was very different. The bark is grey and smooth on younger trees, and darker and cracked on the older ones. Touch it, if you can reach.

As you climb higher, the forest begins to thin. Beech and pine give way to open slopes of grass, low juniper and bare rock. In early summer these meadows fill with wildflowers. Among them grows a plant that every family in Bitola knows — mountain tea. Locals gather it, dry it, and brew it in winter against coughs and colds. The tea has a gentle, earthy, slightly lemony taste. Many households would not think of a cold season without a jar of it in the kitchen.

Then comes one of the strangest sights on the mountain. On some slopes, you will see long streams of grey boulders running downhill, with almost no soil between them. People call these the stone rivers. They are not made by flowing water. They formed during colder periods, when frost broke the granite apart and the loose blocks slowly crept down the slopes. Today they lie still, covered in pale lichen. In the silence of the high ground, they look almost like frozen waves.

Higher still, near the summit ridge, the mountain hides two glacial lakes. Locals call them Pelister's Eyes — the Big Lake and the Small Lake. They sit in hollows carved by ice long ago, and their water is cold and very clear. On a calm day, the surface holds a perfect image of the sky. The lakes are a long hike from the lower parts of the park, so many visitors never see them. Those who reach them often say the effort was worth it.

The name, Pelister's Eyes, comes from the way the people of the region see their mountain. It is not just rock and forest to them. It is almost a living presence that watches over the plain below. Folk songs and local stories mention the mountain again and again. You do not need to know the songs to feel it. Turn around on any open slope and look down at the wide plain, the roofs of Bitola, and the fields beyond. The mountain is always watching that view.

The park is also home to wildlife that keeps to itself. Brown bears and wolves live in the deeper forests, and chamois, a kind of mountain goat, move across the rocky heights. You are unlikely to see the larger animals, as they avoid people. But look for signs. Birds of prey often circle above the ridges, riding the warm air. Small lizards rest on sunny stones. In the evening, the forest grows loud with birdsong, and then very quiet.

The edges of the park are lined with old villages, and they are part of the experience too. Dihovo, Magarevo, Trnovo and Nižepole sit on the lower slopes, with stone houses, red-tiled roofs and small churches. Several of these villages were once home to Aromanian communities, also called Vlachs, a people who speak a language related to Romanian. Many families followed a life of herding, moving flocks between the mountain pastures and the plain. Today, some village houses welcome walkers with homemade food and local cheese.

On the summit itself, you may notice a tall telecommunications tower. It looks out of place among the wild rocks, but it has become a familiar landmark for people in the region. On clear days, the view from the high ridge is very wide. To the west, you can look toward the waters of Prespa Lake. To the south, the mountains of Greece run toward the horizon. The border is close, and the mountain does not end where the map lines do.

The mountain changes deeply with the seasons. In winter, snow covers the upper slopes, and skiers and snowshoers come up from the city. In spring, meltwater runs loudly in the streams, and the meadows turn bright green. Summer brings hikers, berry pickers and families escaping the heat of the plain. Autumn may be the quietest and most beautiful time, when beech leaves turn copper and gold among the dark pines. Weather can change fast here at any time of year.

Before you move on, take one more slow look around you. Notice the colour of the pine needles — that soft blue-green of the molika. Notice the granite under your feet, grey and rough, flecked with tiny crystals. Listen again to the wind in the branches. Somewhere above you, the two small lakes are holding the sky, and the stone rivers lie quiet on the slopes. As you walk back down toward Bitola, the air will grow warmer with every step... and the mountain will stay behind you, watching the plain.

Before you go

Practical tips

Carry warm layers even in summer for the high ridges.
Start early if you plan to hike to the lakes.
Check mountain weather before going above the treeline.
Visit Dihovo village for homemade food after your walk.
Wear sturdy boots for rocky and uneven granite paths.
