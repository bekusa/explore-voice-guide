The guide

The story

Start at the edge of the moat. The water lies still and dark, and the stone walls rise straight out of it. The stones are large and uneven, fitted together without mortar. Behind you, Shizuoka moves at its normal pace. There are buses, office workers, and the hum of a modern city. In front of you is a wide green park where one of the most powerful men in Japanese history chose to spend his last years.

This is Sunpu Castle. Today most of the grounds are a public park, and the old keep is gone. Do not let that disappoint you. This site is less about one grand tower and more about layers. There are layers of stone, layers of power, and layers of excavation. Under the grass, archaeologists keep finding pieces of a story that is still being written.

The central figure is Tokugawa Ieyasu. He was the warlord who unified Japan after more than a century of civil war, and he founded the Tokugawa shogunate. That government ruled the country for roughly two and a half centuries. Its capital was Edo, the city we now call Tokyo. So why does Ieyasu matter so much here, so far from Tokyo? The answer starts with his childhood.

As a boy, Ieyasu came to this area as a hostage. Sunpu was then the base of the Imagawa clan, a powerful family that controlled this region. Taking hostages was a common way for samurai families to guarantee loyalty. So Ieyasu spent formative years here, not as a ruler, but as a guest who could not leave. It is a quiet irony. The town where he was once held became the town he later chose for himself.

In the 1580s, after the Imagawa clan had fallen, Ieyasu built a castle on this ground. Later, he handed the title of shogun to his son, Hidetada. Officially, he retired. In practice, he did not step back at all. He moved to Sunpu and rebuilt the castle on a larger scale. From here he continued to guide national politics. Historians often describe this period as a kind of dual government, with one centre in Edo and another right here.

Ieyasu died in 1616, and Sunpu is where he spent his final years. Look up toward the hills to the southeast if you can. On Mount Kuno, near the sea, stands a shrine where he was first enshrined after his death. Locals often connect the two sites in one day. The castle shows where he lived. The shrine shows how he came to be honoured almost as a god.

The castle did not stay intact for long. The keep was lost to fire in the seventeenth century, and it was never rebuilt. Over the following centuries the site changed purpose many times. In the modern era, after the samurai class was abolished, the Japanese army used the grounds. Later the land became the park you are standing in. Moats were partly filled, buildings vanished, and for a long time Sunpu was more memory than structure.

Then the rebuilding began. In the late twentieth century, the city reconstructed several gates and corner towers using traditional methods. Walk to the East Gate and look closely at the heavy timber and the massive doors. Nearby stands a corner tower called Tatsumi Yagura. A yagura is a defensive tower placed at the corners of castle walls. The names of these towers come from old compass directions, which were once marked with animals of the Chinese zodiac.

On the opposite corner of the grounds, there is a newer tower called Hitsujisaru Yagura, finished in the 2010s. Here is something many visitors miss. The name means the direction between the sheep and the monkey, which is southwest. The east tower, Tatsumi, means dragon and snake, which is southeast. So as you walk, the castle itself works like a giant compass made of wood and stone.

The second surprise lies underground. Excavations at the old keep site have uncovered stone walls from more than one period. Archaeologists found evidence of an earlier castle beneath the structures linked to Ieyasu. That earlier castle dates from the years when a rival lord held this region under Toyotomi Hideyoshi, the ruler before the Tokugawa age. Among the finds were roof tiles decorated with gold leaf. In other words, two competing visions of power sit stacked on top of each other here.

If the excavation area is open, take time at the viewing spaces. You can see raw stone foundations and exposed earth, not polished reconstruction. It feels unfinished, and that is the point. History here is not sealed behind glass. It is being uncovered in real time. Nearby, look for a statue of Ieyasu holding a falcon. Falconry was one of his favourite pastimes, and he reportedly enjoyed hunting in the countryside around Sunpu during his retirement.

Now step into Momijiyama Garden, a Japanese garden built in the park in more recent times. Momiji means maple, and in autumn the leaves turn deep red and orange. The garden uses ponds, stones, and paths to suggest larger landscapes in miniature. On a clear day, the wider Shizuoka area offers views of Mount Fuji. That mountain shaped this region for centuries. Travellers on the old road between Edo and Kyoto passed through here, and Fuji was always the landmark they watched for.

Before you leave, walk once more along the moat. Notice how the city presses right up against the old stone. Joggers pass the gates. Children run across lawns where soldiers once drilled. Listen for the small sounds: water at the edge of the wall, gravel under shoes, wind in the pine trees. Ieyasu chose this place to rest after a lifetime of war. Today, it is still a place where people come to slow down.
