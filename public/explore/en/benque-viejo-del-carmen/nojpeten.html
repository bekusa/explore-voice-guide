The guide

The story

Picture still water, flat and green under a heavy sky. Somewhere across it, a small island rises in a low dome of red roofs and white walls. Fishing boats knock against wooden piers. The air is thick and warm, and it smells of wet stone and lake weeds. This is the setting of Nojpetén, the last great capital of an independent Maya kingdom.

First, a word about where you are. Benque Viejo del Carmen sits on the western edge of Belize, right against the Guatemalan border. Nojpetén itself lies across that border, to the west, on an island in Lake Petén Itzá in northern Guatemala. Today the island is the town of Flores. So this guide tells the story of a place that shaped this whole borderland, even though its stones sit in the next country.

The road west from here crosses into Guatemala at the town of Melchor de Mencos. From there it runs through low hills, cattle land and patches of forest toward the lake. Many travellers make this journey on their way to the great ruins of Tikal. Few realise that the pretty island town at the end of the road was once a Maya capital. In fact, it held out against Spanish rule longer than any other.

Nojpetén was the capital of the Itza, a Maya people who dominated the lakes of central Petén. The name is usually translated as "great island". Itza tradition linked their ancestors to the Yucatán Peninsula to the north, and to the famous city of Chichén Itzá. Historians still debate how literal those stories are. What is clear is that by the sixteenth century, the Itza ruled a strong, organised kingdom from this island.

The island was a natural fortress. Deep water surrounded it on every side. Canoes were the only way across, and the Itza controlled the canoes. Temples, shrines and houses covered the island. Spanish visitors later described many buildings crowded onto its slopes. Around the lake, towns and farms supplied food, and the forest gave the Itza both protection and space. For a long time, distance itself was their best defence.

Here is the first surprise. The conquistador Hernán Cortés, conqueror of the Aztecs, passed this way in 1525 on a long overland march. He met the Itza ruler peacefully, then moved on. But Cortés left behind a lame horse. According to Spanish accounts, the Itza later made a stone image of the horse and honoured it. Friars who arrived generations later reported seeing this statue, and one of them smashed it in anger.

That story shows how the Itza treated the outside world. They were curious, careful and independent. Spanish missionaries visited the island in the seventeenth century and tried to convert the Itza. Some visits ended peacefully. Others ended in violence, and some Spanish parties never returned. The Itza ruler held the title Kan Ek', a name passed down through a line of kings. Under these rulers, the island stayed free while the rest of the Maya world fell.

And this is the second surprise. Most people imagine the Spanish conquest of the Maya ended in the 1500s. It did not. The Itza kingdom survived until 1697, almost two centuries after Cortés first came through. That date is later than the founding of many English colonies in North America. For nearly two hundred years, this island stood as an independent Maya state surrounded by Spanish territory.

The end came in March 1697. A Spanish force under Martín de Ursúa built a road through the forest from Yucatán. They also built a large boat, a galley, on the lakeshore. With that boat, they crossed the water and attacked the island directly. The Itza defenders fought from canoes and from the shore, but Spanish guns decided the battle quickly. The ruler Kan Ek' was captured, and Nojpetén fell.

After the conquest, the Spanish destroyed the temples and built their own town on top. They raised a fort and a church on the high point of the island. Over the centuries, this town became Flores. The Maya buildings did not disappear completely. Their foundations and fill lie beneath the streets and houses. Archaeologists working on the island have found traces of the older city under the colonial layers.

So what does a visitor see today? Not grand pyramids, but a compact town of narrow, sloping streets. The houses are painted in strong colours, blue, yellow and pink. A church with white towers stands at the top of the island. A causeway now links Flores to the mainland, so canoes are no longer the only route. Walk the lakeside path, and you circle the whole island in a short, easy stroll.

The borderland around you carries this history too. Many families in western Belize and northern Guatemala have Maya roots, and Maya languages are still spoken in the region. Spanish place names, Maya faces, and the shared forest tie both sides of the border together. When you look west from Benque Viejo toward the hills, you are looking toward the heart of the old Itza world.

If you make the journey, take your time on the island. Sit on a lakeside wall in the late afternoon. Watch the boats come and go, and listen to the water against the stone. Try to picture canoes instead of motorboats, and temples instead of church towers. The shape of the island has hardly changed. Only the buildings on top are new.

For now, stand for a moment where you are. Feel the warm air and look toward the western hills. Beyond them lies the lake, and the small island that held out longer than any other Maya kingdom. As you walk on, remember that history here does not stop at the border. It runs under the roads, through the forest, and across the water.
