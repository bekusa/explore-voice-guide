The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across the grass. Somewhere below, water runs in a stream bed. Under your feet the ground is soft, earthy, and uneven, and it looks like any other hillside in these Zagros foothills. But the soil here holds the remains of one of the earliest farming villages ever found by archaeologists.

This is Jarmo, in the Chamchamal district of the Sulaymaniyah Governorate, between the plains and the high mountains of Kurdistan. There are no towers here, no columns, no carved gates. The site is a low mound on a bluff above a valley. To understand why it matters, imagine the people who lived here roughly nine thousand years ago. They were among the first humans to stay in one place and grow their own food.

Let us start with the landscape, because it is the reason for everything else. These hills are part of what scholars call the Fertile Crescent, a great arc of land that curves from the Levant through southern Turkey and down along the Zagros Mountains. Wild wheat, wild barley, wild goats and wild sheep all lived naturally on slopes like these. Rain falls here in winter. The soil holds moisture. For early people, this was a place where the raw materials of farming were already growing.

The modern story of Jarmo begins in the late 1940s. An American archaeologist named Robert Braidwood, from the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago, came to Iraq with a question. Where, and how, did farming begin? Many scholars at the time looked to the great river valleys for the answer. Braidwood had a different idea. He believed that agriculture began in the hilly flanks of the mountains, where the wild ancestors of crops and animals actually lived.

Jarmo became his test case. Braidwood and his wife, the archaeologist Linda Braidwood, led excavations here across several seasons around 1950 and the mid-1950s. And here is the first thing most visitors do not know. Their team was unusual for its time. Braidwood brought in specialists from the natural sciences, including a botanist to study ancient seeds and a geologist to read the landscape. This mix of archaeology and science helped shape how prehistoric sites are studied around the world today.

So what did they find? They found a small village. Houses with several rooms were built from packed mud, laid in layers and allowed to dry, sometimes on stone foundations. Floors were covered with reed matting. Many houses had clay ovens and storage areas. Estimates suggest a few dozen houses stood here at one time, home to perhaps one or two hundred people. It was not a city. It was a community of families who knew each other well.

Now the most important discovery... the seeds and the bones. In the excavated soil, the team found charred grains of early wheat and barley. These grains show changes that come with cultivation, the first steps from wild plant to crop. They also found the bones of goats, and the pattern of those bones suggests that people were managing herds, not only hunting. In other words, Jarmo captures a moment of transition. Its people still gathered wild foods, collected acorns and pistachios, and hunted. But they were also becoming farmers.

The objects from the village tell us about daily life. People here carved bowls from stone and polished them smooth. They made tools from flint, and also from obsidian, a sharp black volcanic glass. Here is a second surprise. There is no obsidian in these hills. Studies point to sources far to the north, in the region of Lake Van in eastern Turkey, hundreds of kilometres away. So this small village was connected to a wider world, through exchange that passed from hand to hand across the mountains.

The people of Jarmo also shaped clay. Archaeologists recovered thousands of small clay figurines. Some show animals. Others show human figures, including seated women. Nobody knows exactly what these figurines meant. They may have been toys, tokens, or objects of belief. It is honest to say that their purpose remains an open question. Pottery appears only in the upper layers of the site, which means the earliest villagers lived without clay pots at all, and learned to make them later.

When did all this happen? Radiocarbon dates place the village roughly in the seventh millennium before the common era, around nine thousand years ago. Scholars have debated the exact range for decades, because early dating methods were less precise. What is clear is that people lived here for many generations. They built new houses on top of old ones. Over time, these layers slowly raised the ground into the mound in front of you.

As you look across the site, notice the edge of the bluff and the valley below. The stream has cut into the hill over thousands of years, and part of the ancient village has been lost to erosion. What remains is only a portion of the original settlement. Notice too how the land opens to the south, toward the plains, while the mountains rise behind. The villagers chose a place with water, good soil, and a wide view.

Today, the original finds from Jarmo are held in museum collections, divided between institutions in Iraq and the United States. The site itself is quiet. You may share it with grazing sheep, the descendants of animals whose wild ancestors once lived on these same slopes. In spring, the hills turn green and wildflowers appear. In summer, the grass dries to gold and the heat is strong. Each season shows you the same cycle that shaped the lives of the first farmers here.

Before you leave, take a few seconds to stand still. Look at the soil under your shoes, the grasses moving in the wind, the line of the hills against the sky. Somewhere nearby, nine thousand years ago, someone pressed a seed into this ground and waited to see if it would grow. The bread on tables across the world today traces part of its story back to places like this one. As you walk away, listen again to the wind and the water. They are the same sounds the villagers of Jarmo heard every day.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit with a local guide who knows the access route.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, grassy hillside ground.
Come in spring when the surrounding hills are green.
Bring water and sun protection; there is little shade.
Check current local access conditions before driving out.
