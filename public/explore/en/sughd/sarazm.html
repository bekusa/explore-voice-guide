The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across the low, open ground, and somewhere nearby a car passes on the road to Penjikent. Under your feet the soil is pale and dusty, the colour of dry clay. Around you, the Zeravshan valley spreads out wide and green, with mountains standing at its edges. At first glance, there is not much to see here. And yet you are standing on one of the oldest settled places in Central Asia.

This is Sarazm, in the far west of Tajikistan, not far from the town of Penjikent and close to the border with Uzbekistan. People lived here more than five thousand years ago. The settlement grew during the fourth millennium before Christ and lasted until near the end of the third millennium. That makes it older than the famous cities of the Silk Road, such as Samarkand, by thousands of years. When people built here, writing barely existed anywhere on Earth.

The name itself carries a small poetic idea. In the Tajik language, which is a form of Persian, Sarazm is often explained as coming from the words for head and land. Together they suggest something like the place where the land begins. Locals say it fits. To the east, the valley narrows toward the high mountains. To the west, it opens toward the plains and the great oases. Sarazm sits right at that threshold.

Here is the first surprise. This place was not found by a team of archaeologists with a grand plan. It was found by chance, in the 1970s, by a local man working the land. He turned up an ancient copper axe and reported it. That one object led specialists to the site. Excavations began soon after, and they continued for decades under the Tajik archaeologist Abdullojon Isakov, whose name is closely tied to Sarazm.

What did they find? Not palaces of stone, and not towering temples. The people of Sarazm built with earth. They used mud brick and packed clay, the same humble materials that people in this region still use for walls and courtyards today. Archaeologists uncovered the outlines of houses, storage rooms, courtyards, and workshops. Some buildings were rectangular. Some were round. Together they formed a real community, spread across a large area.

As you walk, you will notice protective roofs standing over some of the excavated areas. These shelters are important. Mud brick is fragile once it is exposed to rain, wind, and the hard summer sun. Without cover, these ancient walls would slowly melt back into the earth. So when you look under those roofs, you are looking at walls that have survived for five thousand years, and that now need constant care to survive any longer.

Now comes the detail that turns Sarazm from a village into something remarkable. The people here worked metal. They smelted copper and made tools, weapons, and ornaments. The valley around you is rich in minerals, and the Zeravshan River carries them down from the mountains. Sarazm seems to have been an early centre of metalworking — a place where raw materials from the hills became finished objects that could travel far.

And travel they did. This is the second surprise. Objects found at Sarazm show contact with distant lands. There are beads of turquoise, carnelian, and lapis lazuli, a deep blue stone that came mainly from the mountains of what is now Afghanistan. There are seashells that must have travelled from far-off coasts. Pottery styles link Sarazm to cultures in present-day Turkmenistan, Iran, and the regions toward the Indus valley, as well as to the herding peoples of the northern steppe.

Think about what that means. Long before camels carried silk along the famous caravan routes, people here were already part of a web of exchange. Goods, ideas, and probably marriages moved between farmers, herders, and craftspeople across huge distances. This is why UNESCO added Sarazm to the World Heritage List in 2010. It was the first site in Tajikistan to receive that recognition, as early evidence of settlement and trade in this part of the world.

One burial in particular has captured people's imagination. Archaeologists found the grave of a woman buried with rich ornaments — bracelets, and many beads of precious stones and other materials. Today she is widely known as the Princess of Sarazm. That name is a modern nickname, not an ancient title. We do not know who she was, or what role she held. But the care taken with her burial tells us she mattered deeply to the people who laid her to rest.

Archaeologists also found structures that seem to have had a ritual purpose, including places connected with fire. Fire held deep meaning across ancient Iran and Central Asia, long before later religions formalised it. The exact beliefs of the people of Sarazm are unknown. They left no written words. What remains are hearths, platforms, and careful arrangements of space. From these, we can only guess at their prayers and ceremonies.

There is a small museum at the site, and it is worth your time. Inside, you can see finds and reconstructions that help the bare ground make sense. Many of the most precious objects, however, are kept in national museums elsewhere in Tajikistan. So treat the field and the museum as two halves of the same story. The objects show what people made. The ground shows where they lived and worked.

As you walk between the trenches, try to picture the settlement full of life. Smoke rises from cooking fires. A smith works over hot copper. Children run between clay walls, and traders arrive from the west with blue stones wrapped in cloth. The river, the fields, and the mountains around you looked much the same then as they do now. That continuity is part of what makes standing here so powerful.

Before you leave, pause for a moment and look down at the soil again. It looks ordinary — pale, dry, and quiet. But beneath it, and beneath much of the land around you, more of Sarazm still waits, unexcavated. Listen once more to the wind across the valley. Then, as you walk back toward the road, notice how the land begins to rise toward the mountains. You are leaving the place where, five thousand years ago, the land began.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the small site museum before walking the excavations.
Bring a hat and water; shade is scarce outdoors.
Combine your visit with nearby ancient Penjikent.
Come in morning or late afternoon to avoid midday heat.
See major Sarazm finds in national museums afterward.
