The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the ground under your feet. It rises. You are not on a natural hill but on a mound built up by people, layer upon layer, over thousands of years. Pale limestone walls curve above you. The air carries the sharp, warm smell of roasting pistachios and the faint ring of hammers on copper from the lanes below. This is Gaziantep Castle, and it sits at the very heart of the old city.

That mound matters more than the walls on top of it. Archaeologists call this kind of site a höyük — a settlement mound. Each generation built on the ruins of the last. Houses fell, new houses rose, and the ground slowly climbed. So when you look at the castle, remember that the oldest part of the story is not above you. It is beneath you, sealed inside the earth.

The castle as a fortress has deep roots. It is generally believed to have begun as a Roman watchtower, a lookout over the roads and farmland of the region. Later, in the sixth century, the Byzantine Empire — the eastern, Greek-speaking half of the Roman world — expanded it into a real fortress. That work is usually linked to the reign of the emperor Justinian, who strengthened many frontier towns in this part of Anatolia.

After the Byzantines, the castle passed through many hands. Seljuk Turks, Ayyubids, and Mamluks all held this region at different times, and each repaired and reshaped the walls. Later the Ottomans took control. Look closely at the stonework and you can often see it. Some blocks are large and carefully cut. Others are smaller and set in a rougher way. The wall is a patchwork of many repairs, made by many hands.

Now notice the shape. Many castles are square or follow the line of a cliff. This one is round. The walls follow the curve of the mound, and a ring of towers stands along them. A deep ditch, or moat, once circled the base. Walk part of the way around and you feel how the fortress turns slowly ahead of you, always bending, never offering a long straight line.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. The most important chapter for local people is not ancient at all. It belongs to the twentieth century. After the First World War, French forces occupied the city. From 1920 into early 1921, the people of Antep — the old name of the city — resisted them in a long and hard siege. Families, craftsmen, and local fighters held out for months.

The cost was terrible. Food ran out, and many people died. One local fighter, Şahin Bey, became a symbol of the resistance after he was killed in 1920. You will see his name on streets and monuments across the city. In 1921, the new Turkish national assembly gave the city the title Gazi, which means veteran or victorious warrior. That is why Antep became Gaziantep. The name itself carries the memory of that siege.

Inside the castle, a museum tells this story. It is known as the Defence and Heroism Panoramic Museum. Instead of glass cases full of objects, it uses large painted scenes, figures, and sound to recreate moments from the siege. It can feel theatrical. But for many Turkish visitors it is deeply emotional, because the people shown here are often their own great-grandparents. Watch the faces of the families around you as they move through it.

There is a second, more recent layer to this place, and it is a painful one. In February 2023, powerful earthquakes struck southern Turkey and northern Syria. Gaziantep was hit hard. Parts of the castle walls and some of its towers collapsed, and stones fell down the slope of the mound. Images of the damaged fortress travelled around the world. For a building that had stood through empires and sieges, it was a shock.

So what you see today may be a castle in recovery. Scaffolding, fences, and fresh stone are part of the scene. Some areas may be closed while specialists work. It is worth looking at the repairs with curiosity rather than disappointment. You are watching the latest chapter of the same process that shaped these walls for centuries — damage, then rebuilding, by the people of the city.

From the castle, the old town spreads out around you. Just below lie the historic markets, where coppersmiths still shape trays, pots, and coffee sets by hand. Listen for the steady tapping. Nearby you will also find small workshops, spice sellers, and stalls piled with dried peppers. Gaziantep is famous across Turkey for its food — especially pistachios and baklava — and the streets around the castle are a good place to taste both.

Think about why a fortress was built exactly here. The mound gave height in a fairly open landscape. From the top, guards could watch the roads that crossed this region between the Mediterranean coast and Mesopotamia, the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. Traders, armies, and pilgrims all passed this way. The castle grew because the city grew, and the city grew because so many roads met here.

As you stand here now, let your eyes move slowly. Follow the curve of the wall. Find a stone that looks old and one that looks new. Notice the colour of the limestone in this light, and the way the sound of the market drifts up the slope. When you walk away, look back once. The castle is still the centre of the city — damaged, repaired, and still standing on the hill its people built.
