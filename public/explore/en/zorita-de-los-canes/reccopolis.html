The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here on the hill, the wind moves through dry grass and wild thyme, and somewhere below you the Tagus river runs green between its banks. The ground underfoot is pale and stony. Low walls rise only knee-high around you, the colour of old bread in the sun. It does not look like much at first glance... and yet you are standing inside one of the rarest places in Western Europe.

This is Reccopolis, a city built by the Visigoths. The Visigoths were a Germanic people who ruled most of the Iberian Peninsula after the collapse of Roman power in the West. They were not wanderers camping in Roman ruins. By the late sixth century, they ran a kingdom with laws, coins, bishops and royal courts. And here, on this hill above the river, one of their kings decided to build a city from nothing.

That king was Leovigild. According to a chronicle written in his own lifetime, by a churchman called John of Biclaro, Leovigild founded this city around the year 578. He named it after his son, Reccared. The name simply means the city of Reccared, in the same way that Constantinople meant the city of Constantine. That comparison is not an accident. Leovigild was openly copying the style of the Roman emperors in the East.

Here is the first surprise. Between the fall of Rome and the age of Charlemagne, almost nobody in Western Europe built new cities. People repaired old ones, or let them shrink. Founding a city from scratch was a statement of power that very few rulers could make. Reccopolis is one of only a handful of such foundations, which is why archaeologists treat this quiet hillside with such care.

As you walk, try to picture the city as it was meant to be seen. A wall ran around the top of the hill, with towers along its line. Inside, the most important buildings stood together on the highest ground: a large palace complex and a church. Power and faith shared the same view. From up there, the king's officials could look down over the river valley and the road that ran beside it.

The palace area is one of the clearest parts of the site. You can see the outlines of long halls, laid out in straight lines. These were big, formal buildings, with upper floors that have long since gone. Experts believe they served the royal court and the administration of the region. Stand at their edge and think of the people who once climbed these steps, carrying petitions, taxes and news.

Next to the palace stands the church, and its plan is still easy to read on the ground. Look for the shape of the nave and the rounded end at the east. The church outlived much of the city around it. In later centuries, people kept returning to this spot to worship, long after the palace halls had fallen. Sacred places often keep their meaning when everything else is forgotten.

Now look downhill from the palace. A main street runs between rows of rooms. These rooms were shops and workshops, opening directly onto the street. Excavations found traces of craft work here, including glass and metal working. So this was not only a royal stage set. It was a working town, with the noise of hammers, the smoke of small furnaces, and traders calling to people walking past.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a small one that you can hold in your hand. Reccopolis had its own mint. Gold coins were struck here, carrying the name of the city. A hoard of Visigothic gold coins was also discovered at the site. For a kingdom that is often imagined as dark and poor, these little gold pieces tell a very different story about wealth, trade and royal confidence.

The city did not stay great for long. After the Islamic conquest of the peninsula in the early eighth century, Reccopolis slowly declined. Arabic writers knew it under a new name, Rakkubal. Over time, the population drifted away. Much of the stone was carried off and reused, most likely in the castle you can see nearby at Zorita de los Canes. In a sense, part of this city still stands... just in a different place.

That castle has its own long story. In the Middle Ages, it became a stronghold of the Order of Calatrava, a military religious order of knights. Look across the landscape and you can follow the line from the Visigothic hill to the castle rock above the village. Two very different worlds, built partly from the same stone, facing each other across a short stretch of valley.

For centuries, the ruins here lay under soil and scrub. Excavations began in the twentieth century, and modern research has continued for decades, uncovering the palace, the church and the commercial street step by step. Every season adds detail, and much of the hill has still not been fully explored. What you see is a city still being read, one trench at a time.

Before you leave the summit, take a moment to look at the view itself. The river curves below. The fields change colour with the season. The hills fold away towards the horizon. This was the view that Leovigild chose, the view his son's city was named for. It has hardly changed, even though the kingdom that built these walls vanished more than thirteen centuries ago.

As you walk back down, notice the small things. The way the stones are laid in rough courses. The bits of mortar still holding them together. The lizards that dart into the cracks when your shadow falls across them. A king once imagined a grand capital here. Today it is quiet, open to the sky, and shared with the wind... and with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the interpretation centre to understand the site layout.
Bring a hat and water; the hilltop has little shade.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose stones and uneven paths.
Visit early morning or late afternoon for softer light.
Combine your visit with the castle in Zorita de los Canes.
