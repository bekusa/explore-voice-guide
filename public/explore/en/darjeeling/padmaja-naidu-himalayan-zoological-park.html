The guide

The story

You climb a path lined with tall trees. The air is cool and smells of pine and damp earth. Somewhere ahead, hidden in the green, an animal moves through the branches. This is no ordinary zoo. It sits high in the mountains, and it is home to some of the rarest animals of the Himalayas. Your footsteps are soft on the damp path, and the forest feels close.

The park opened in 1958. It is named after Padmaja Naidu, who served as the governor of West Bengal. Over the years it has grown into the largest high-altitude zoo in India. It sits at about 2,134 meters above the sea, so the animals here live in the cool mountain climate they know. Mist often drifts through the trees here, and the cold deepens as the day fades.

That high, cool setting is the whole idea. Many Himalayan animals cannot live in the heat of the lowlands. Here, the air is thin and cold, much like their true mountain homes. This lets the park care for creatures that few other zoos in the world are able to keep. Few zoos anywhere sit this high, and fewer still keep their animals in such cool, clean mountain air all year round.

The park is best known for one small animal. The red panda. It is about the size of a large cat, with reddish brown fur and a long striped tail. It lives in the trees and eats mostly bamboo. The park has worked for many years to breed red pandas and protect them. This work has made the park known among those who study these animals.

Watch the trees in the red panda area closely. These animals are quiet and shy. They often rest high on a branch, curled up, with the tail wrapped around the body for warmth. When one moves, it steps slowly and carefully, gripping the bark with small clawed feet. If you are patient, you may catch the slow blink of its dark eyes.

The park is also a home for the snow leopard. This is one of the hardest animals in the world to see in the wild. It lives high in the mountains and hides well, with pale gray fur marked by dark rings. Here, in large enclosures, the park breeds and cares for them.

Snow leopards are built for the cold. They have thick fur and very wide paws that work like snowshoes on deep snow. Their long, heavy tails help them balance on steep rock, and they wrap those tails around themselves to keep warm. Watch for one resting quietly on a high ledge. Even at rest, it watches everything with calm, pale eyes.

The enclosures here are large and planted with trees, so the animals live among branches and rocks much like their wild homes. This makes them harder to spot. You may stand at a railing for several minutes, scanning the green, before you notice a shape resting quietly above you. The search is part of the visit. When you finally find the animal, half hidden in the leaves, the small discovery feels earned.

The work here is about more than showing animals to visitors. The park runs careful breeding programs for species that are growing rare. Some young animals raised here are part of larger efforts to keep these species alive. The goal is not only to display the animals, but to help them survive. It is patient, careful work that goes on year after year, mostly out of sight.

Beyond the red panda and the snow leopard, the park cares for other mountain animals. There are wolves of the high Himalayas. There are wild goats and sheep that live on steep slopes. There are pheasants and other birds with bright feathers, kept in tall enclosures among the trees. Each enclosure is set among the natural forest, so a walk here feels partly like a walk in the woods. Leaves crunch underfoot, and somewhere a bird calls and falls silent.

Take your time on the paths. The park is spread across a hillside, and the walk between enclosures is part of the visit. Tall trees shade the way. The ground rises and falls. The mountain air keeps you cool even as you climb, and the views open up between the branches.

As you walk back down the path, think of the animals you saw. The shy red panda in its tree. The quiet snow leopard on its ledge. These are creatures most people will never meet in the wild. Here, high in the hills, they are cared for, studied, and protected. That care is the real work of this place. And it goes on quietly, every day, high above the plains. You leave a little quieter than you came.
