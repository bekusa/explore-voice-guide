The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind moves across low scrub and dry earth, and somewhere a dog barks from a farm. The ground here is pale and hard, broken by thorny bushes and the odd olive tree. Then your eye catches something that does not belong: a squat block of grey concrete, half sunk into the land, with a dark slit facing south. This is the Mareth Line, and it was built for a war that came here later than anyone planned.

You are in southern Tunisia, between the coastal city of Gabès to the north and Medenine to the south. On a map, this stretch of land forms a natural corridor. The Mediterranean lies on one side and the Matmata Hills rise on the other. Anyone moving an army along the coast between Libya and the rest of Tunisia had to pass through this gap. That simple fact of geography explains almost everything you are about to hear.

In the 1930s, Tunisia was a French protectorate. Next door, Libya was an Italian colony, and relations between France and Mussolini's Italy were growing tense. French military planners feared an Italian push westward along the coast. Their answer was a chain of fortifications across this corridor, stretching roughly from the sea to the edge of the hills. People often compare it to the Maginot Line, the much larger French defensive system on the border with Germany. The idea was similar: concrete, steel and firepower placed where an attacker had no choice but to come.

The builders did not rely on concrete alone. They used the land itself. A seasonal riverbed, the Wadi Zigzaou, runs across the front of the line. A wadi is a valley that stays dry for much of the year and fills with water after heavy rain. Its steep banks made a natural ditch against tanks, and the French deepened and strengthened parts of it. Behind it, they placed blockhouses, gun positions, trenches and barbed wire.

Here is the first twist in the story. The line was built to stop the Italians, yet the French never fought for it. In 1940, France fell to Germany and signed armistices with both Germany and Italy. Under those terms, the fortifications in southern Tunisia were disarmed and left largely idle. For more than two years, these bunkers sat quietly in the sun while the war in North Africa swung back and forth across Libya and Egypt.

That changed in the winter of 1942 and 1943. After their defeat at El Alamein in Egypt, German and Italian forces retreated west across Libya, pursued by the British Eighth Army under General Bernard Montgomery. As they reached Tunisia, the Axis commanders saw the old French line and recognised its value. The defences built against Italy would now shelter Italian and German troops. They repaired positions, laid mines and added new weapons to face an enemy coming from the east.

In early March 1943, Rommel's forces struck out from these positions against the British near Medenine, just to the south. The attack failed, and it was the last offensive Rommel led in Africa. Shortly afterwards, he left the continent for good. The defence of the line passed to other commanders, and the soldiers here waited for the British blow they knew would come.

It came later that month. The British launched a frontal assault across the Wadi Zigzaou, and it proved brutally hard. Soldiers had to cross the steep riverbed under fire, through mines and wire. Vehicles struggled on the soft, wet ground, and bridgeheads on the far bank came under heavy counterattack. For a time, the attack stalled. If you look at the wadi today, try to imagine those banks at night, lit by flares and gunfire.

The second surprise is that the line was not broken here, at its strongest point. It was turned. While the frontal fight went on, a force led largely by New Zealand troops made a long march inland, around the far side of the Matmata Hills. They pushed through a pass known as the Tebaga Gap, aiming to get behind the defenders. When the British shifted more strength to this desert flank, the whole position became impossible to hold. The Axis troops withdrew north toward Wadi Akarit, and the fighting moved on.

This pattern appears again and again in the history of fixed defences. Concrete is strong, but it cannot move. Planners in the 1930s expected an enemy to come straight up the coast. They believed the hills protected the inland flank. In 1943, motor vehicles, careful scouting and sheer determination proved that assumption wrong. Within a few weeks, the remaining Axis forces in North Africa were pushed into a shrinking pocket in northern Tunisia, where they surrendered in May.

Today, the town of Mareth has a small military museum dedicated to the line. Inside, you can see maps, photographs, weapons and models that explain how the fortifications worked. Outside, a few bunkers sit within reach, and walking around them helps the story make sense. The concrete is rough and weathered. Rusted steel shows through in places, and the firing slits are narrower than most people expect.

As you walk, notice how low these structures sit in the landscape. They were designed to be hard to see and hard to hit, not to impress anyone. Notice also the view south from a firing slit. It frames a slice of open ground, which is exactly what a defender wanted to watch. Many people who fought here came from very far away, from Britain, New Zealand, India, Germany, Italy and beyond, and many of them did not go home.

Before you leave, look once more at the land around you. Farmers still work these fields. Traffic moves along the coastal road that armies once fought to control. The bunkers have become part of the scenery, like old stones in a field. As you walk back, listen again to the wind in the scrub. It is the same sound the soldiers heard in the quiet hours here, between the noise of the war.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the Mareth military museum for maps and context.
Bring water and sun protection for walking between bunkers.
Wear sturdy shoes for rough, uneven ground near the fortifications.
Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid midday heat.
Combine your visit with nearby Matmata or Medenine.
