The guide

The story

Put your palm flat against one of the pillars. The granite is rough and cool, and it is speckled grey and gold like old bread crust. Now look up. Arch after arch climbs above you, stacked in two tiers, and the sky shows through each one like a window. Pigeons settle on the ledges. Below, voices echo off the stone of the square. You are standing beside one of the most complete Roman structures anywhere in the world.

This is the Aqueduct of Segovia. Its only job was to carry water. It brought water from the mountains of the Sierra de Guadarrama, the range you can often see on the horizon, into the hilltop town. Most historians date it to the late first or early second century, during the reigns of the emperors Domitian or Trajan. Nobody has found a signed contract or a builder's name. What remains is the work itself, and it is enough.

Here is the first surprise. There is no mortar holding these stones together. None at all. The Romans cut each granite block so carefully that weight and balance alone keep the arches standing. Every block presses down on the one below. Every arch pushes against its neighbour. Nearly two thousand years of winters, wind, and earth tremors have not undone the calculation. Look closely at the joints and you will see how tightly the faces meet.

The structure you see is only the most visible part of a much longer system. Out beyond the town, the water ran mostly at ground level or in channels cut into the land. It also passed through a settling tank, where sand and grit dropped out before the water moved on. Only here, where the land dips sharply before the old town, did the engineers need to lift the channel high into the air. That dip is the square where you are standing now.

At this point the arches reach their greatest height, nearly thirty metres above the ground. The lower tier carries the upper tier, and the upper tier carries a narrow channel along the very top. You cannot see that channel from here. But the whole purpose of this enormous mass of stone was to support a small stream of water no wider than a gutter. The gradient was so gentle that the water flowed steadily without rushing.

Now look at the faces of the blocks. Many of them have small holes in them, set in pairs. Locals tell a story about these holes. Legend has it that a young girl who carried water up the hill each day grew tired of the climb. She made a bargain with the devil. If he built her a channel for water in a single night, he could have her soul. The devil worked through the darkness, carrying stones and setting arches.

According to the tale, the girl regretted the bargain and prayed. A rooster crowed just before the devil placed the final stone, so the deal was broken and her soul was saved. The holes, people say, are the marks of the devil's fingers. The real explanation is more practical, but still clever. Roman builders gripped the heavy blocks with iron pincers and lifted them with cranes. The holes show where those pincers held the stone.

High in the centre of the upper tier, look for a small niche set into the stone. Today it holds a figure of the Virgin Mary and the Child. That figure is a later Christian addition. Some writers suggest that a pagan figure, possibly Hercules, stood there in Roman times, but that remains uncertain. Just below, there was once a large inscription in bronze letters. The letters are long gone. Only the fixing holes for them remain in the stone.

Those empty holes became a puzzle for scholars. In the twentieth century, the German historian Géza Alföldy studied the pattern of the fixing points. From their positions, he reconstructed what the missing letters probably said. His reading connected the dedication to the age of the emperor Domitian and later to Trajan. It is a good example of how much a careful eye can recover from what looks like nothing but marks in rock.

The aqueduct has not survived untouched. In 1072, during fighting in medieval Iberia, attackers from the south damaged a section of the arches. For centuries the gap remained, and the water supply suffered. In the late fifteenth century, during the reign of the Catholic Monarchs, Isabella and Ferdinand, the monks and builders of the time rebuilt dozens of arches. They copied the Roman design closely, so most visitors never notice the repairs.

The second surprise is how long this structure kept working. The aqueduct brought water to Segovia for many centuries after the Romans left. It served Visigoths, Muslim rulers, medieval Castilian kings, and the modern town. Its water reached homes and fountains well into modern times. Few Roman buildings anywhere can say they did the job they were built for for so long. Segovia also chose the aqueduct as the main image on its coat of arms.

For much of the twentieth century, cars and buses drove directly under these arches. Vibration and exhaust fumes damaged the granite. In recent decades the town moved the traffic away and cleaned and protected the stone. In 1985, UNESCO listed the aqueduct together with the old town of Segovia as World Heritage. Today the square around the pillars is open mostly to people on foot, and the base feels calm again.

If you have time, walk up the stone steps beside the aqueduct, on the side toward the old town. As you climb, you rise level with the upper tier. From the top you can look along the line of arches as they shrink into the distance. Then follow the line of the aqueduct across the lower town. The arches become shorter and shorter, until the channel meets the ground and disappears into the streets.

Before you leave, stand back near the edge of the square for a moment. Notice how the afternoon light moves across the granite and turns the grey blocks warmer. Listen to the footsteps and the echo under the arches. Every stone above you was lifted by hand, by people whose names nobody wrote down. And as you walk away, look back once more. The arches will frame the sky exactly as they have for nearly two thousand years.
