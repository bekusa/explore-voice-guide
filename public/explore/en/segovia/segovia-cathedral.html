The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the Plaza Mayor and look up. The stone in front of you is a pale, honey-coloured limestone. In the late afternoon it turns almost gold. Café chairs scrape on the paving, pigeons lift off the rooftops, and above it all rises a forest of pinnacles, buttresses and small spires. This is Segovia Cathedral, standing on the highest ground of the old city. From almost anywhere in town, you can see it.

Locals and admirers give it an affectionate nickname — the Lady of Cathedrals. Walk around it once and you will understand why. The building does not feel heavy. Its outline is layered and delicate, rising step by step like the folds of a long dress. The many pinnacles make the roofline look lighter than it really is. It is one of the last great Gothic cathedrals built in Spain. By the time it was started, much of Europe was already moving on to other styles.

Here is the first surprise. This is not Segovia's original cathedral. An older one stood near the Alcázar, the royal fortress at the western tip of the city. In the early sixteenth century, Castile was shaken by the Revolt of the Comuneros. This was an uprising of Castilian towns against the new king, Charles the Fifth. Segovia was at the heart of it. During the fighting, the old cathedral was badly damaged, and the city decided to build again.

They chose a new site right here, in the middle of town. Work began in the 1520s. The design came from the master builder Juan Gil de Hontañón. After his death, his son Rodrigo continued the project. Building a cathedral was a task of generations, not years. The work went on for well over two centuries. The church was finally consecrated in 1768, long after its first builders had gone.

That long timeline creates something unusual. The cathedral was planned in the Gothic tradition and stayed loyal to it, even as fashions changed. Gothic is the medieval style of pointed arches, ribbed vaults and tall windows. By the later sixteenth century, most new Spanish churches looked to Renaissance ideas from Italy instead. Segovia kept its Gothic plan. So you are looking at a late echo of the Middle Ages, built in the age of explorers.

Walk around to the outside walls and look closely. The flying buttresses — the stone arms that brace the walls from outside — rise in tiers. Each one ends in a carved pinnacle. These are not only decoration. The extra weight on top helps push the force of the vault down into the ground. Engineering and beauty work together here. Let your eye follow one buttress from bottom to top, and you can trace that force.

Now look at the bell tower. It is tall today, but it was once taller. Over the centuries, lightning struck it more than once. After one serious fire, the top was rebuilt in a different form. For a city on a high ridge, with dry summer storms, a stone tower was a natural target. On some days, guided visits climb inside the tower. From the top, you can see the rooftops, the Alcázar and the open plains of Castile.

Step inside, and the temperature drops. Your footsteps sound different on the stone floor. The space opens upward into tall columns that branch into star-shaped vaults. Light comes in through stained glass, much of it from the sixteenth century. On a sunny day, coloured patches move slowly across the pillars and the floor. Stand in the centre of the nave and simply look up. The ribs of the ceiling form a pattern like a woven net.

In the middle of the church sits the choir, enclosed by walls. Here is the second surprise. The carved wooden choir stalls are older than the cathedral itself. They came from the old cathedral near the Alcázar. They were saved and carried across the city to their new home. The dark wood, the tiny carved figures and the worn armrests carry the marks of centuries of use. Look for the two large organs above the choir, added in the eighteenth century.

Around the edges of the church, a ring of side chapels holds paintings, altarpieces and tombs. Take your time here. One chapel holds a dramatic sculpture of the burial of Christ by Juan de Juni. He was a sculptor famous for intense, emotional figures. The faces show real grief. Many visitors walk past these chapels quickly. Pause at each iron grille, and you will find something different behind it.

The cloister is another piece that travelled. It was built for the old cathedral by the architect Juan Guas. When the new cathedral rose, the cloister was taken apart and rebuilt here, stone by stone. Think about that for a moment — numbering each block, carrying it uphill, and setting it all up again. Today it is a quiet square of arches around a garden. The noise of the plaza disappears completely.

The cloister also holds stories that sit between history and legend. Locals tell the tale of a young woman thrown from the cliffs near the city. According to the story, she called on the Virgin Mary and survived the fall. Another popular tale concerns a royal child who fell from a window of the Alcázar. Legend has it that his nurse threw herself after him in despair. Treat these as stories the city has told for centuries, not documented facts.

The cathedral also has a small museum with religious art, silverwork and old manuscripts. It is a good place to see the craftsmanship that filled this building over the centuries. Take a moment, too, to notice how the cathedral connects to the town around it. The Jewish quarter lies just behind it. The narrow streets lead down toward the Roman aqueduct. The cathedral marks the high, central point of Segovia.

When you step back outside, return to the edge of the Plaza Mayor. Look up at the cathedral once more. Notice how the light has changed since you arrived. The stone may look warmer now, or a little greyer. Listen to the café chatter and the church bells marking the hour. This building took more than two centuries to finish. As you walk away, turn back once more and watch the pinnacles against the Castilian sky.

Practical tips
