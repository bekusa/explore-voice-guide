The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. At this altitude, the silence has a texture of its own. Wind moves over bare slopes and loose scree. A stream runs somewhere below, cold and fast, over grey stones. The air is thin and dry, and each breath feels a little shorter than it should. The light is sharp, almost metallic, and every ridge stands out clearly against a deep blue sky. This is Hemis National Park, a high and open land of rock, ice, and very few people.

The park lies in Ladakh, in the far north of India, south of the Indus River and close to the town of Leh. It was set aside as a protected area in 1981. It covers several thousand square kilometres of mountains, gorges, and high valleys, and it is usually described as the largest national park in India. Most of that land is cold desert. The great Himalayan wall to the south blocks much of the monsoon rain, so very little falls here.

That dryness shapes everything you see. The slopes are mostly bare, in shades of ochre, rust, purple, and grey. Plants grow low and tight to the ground, close to the warmth of the soil. In the valley floors, wherever water flows, the picture changes. Thin lines of willow and poplar follow the streams. Small barley fields sit in green patches beside stone houses. These green strips are often the only sign that people live here at all.

The park takes its name from Hemis Monastery, one of the best-known Buddhist monasteries in Ladakh, which sits near its eastern edge. Buddhism is not a background detail here. It is part of daily life inside the park. As you walk, you will pass long walls of carved stones, called mani walls, and white domed shrines called chortens. Prayer flags in blue, white, red, green, and yellow move constantly in the wind.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. This is a national park with villages inside it. Small communities have farmed and herded in these valleys for many generations, long before any park boundary was drawn. Families keep yaks, goats, sheep, and dzo — a cross between a yak and a cow. Their way of life is part of the protected landscape, not separate from it. Rumbak, Markha, and other small settlements still sit along the old walking routes.

Now to the animal that brings many people here. Hemis is known around the world for the snow leopard. This cat lives in steep, broken country high above the valleys. Its fur is smoky grey with dark rosettes, and its long, thick tail helps it balance on narrow ledges. It is also a warm wrap when the animal rests. The park is often described as one of the best places on Earth to try to see one in the wild.

Even here, a sighting is never certain. Snow leopards are very well camouflaged against rock. A cat can lie on a ridge in full view and still remain invisible to most people. Experienced local spotters scan the slopes with telescopes for hours. They look for a movement, a tail, or the shape of a head. Many watchers travel in the depth of winter, when the cats come lower and fresh snow shows their tracks.

The snow leopard depends on its prey, and the most important prey here is the bharal, also called the blue sheep. Look for small herds grazing on impossibly steep slopes. Their coats are a soft blue-grey and blend into the rock. The park is also home to the Asiatic ibex, with long curved horns, and the Ladakh urial, a wild sheep. Tibetan wolves and red foxes also hunt in these valleys.

Look up as well. The sky over Hemis is home to large birds of prey. The lammergeier, or bearded vulture, glides along the ridges on very long, narrow wings. It is known for an unusual habit — it drops bones onto rocks to break them, then eats the marrow. Himalayan griffon vultures and golden eagles also ride the warm air above the valleys. Closer to the ground, small birds called choughs call from the cliffs.

For a long time, snow leopards and herders were in conflict. A cat that entered a livestock pen at night could kill many animals at once, and families sometimes lost much of their income. In recent decades, villagers and conservation groups have worked together on this problem. They built stronger, covered livestock pens. They also started homestay programmes. Visitors now sleep in village homes, and the income helps families see a living snow leopard as an asset.

If you stay in one of these homes, you will likely sit in a warm kitchen with a stove in the centre. Rows of polished copper and brass pots line the shelves. You may be offered butter tea, which is salty and rich, or sweet milky tea. Meals are simple and filling. You might eat thick noodle soups, flatbread, and dishes made with barley flour. These evenings often stay with visitors longer than the mountain views.

Many people walk through the park on the Markha Valley trek, one of the most popular hiking routes in Ladakh. The trail crosses high passes, fords cold rivers, and follows narrow gorges between walls of folded rock. Look closely at those rock walls. Bands of colour curve and twist where layers of stone were bent by enormous pressure. This happened as the Indian landmass pushed into Asia and raised the Himalaya.

Altitude is the quiet force behind every visit. Even the lower valleys sit well above 3,000 metres, and the high passes climb much further. Your body needs several days in Leh to adjust before you walk here. Move slowly. Drink plenty of water. Rest when your head feels heavy. The mountains do not reward hurry, and many locals will tell you that patience is the real skill in this landscape.

So take a moment now. Look at the slope in front of you and let your eyes move slowly across it. Every rock could hide a sheep, a fox, or a cat that has already seen you. Listen again to the wind and the water. As you walk on, notice the prayer flags and the stone walls. People have lived quietly beside wild animals here for a very long time... and the land still carries that balance.

Before you go

Practical tips

Spend several days acclimatising in Leh before entering the park.
Hire a local spotter with a telescope for wildlife.
Visit in winter for the best snow leopard chances.
Pack warm layers, sunglasses, and strong sunscreen year-round.
Walk clockwise around mani walls and chortens out of respect.
