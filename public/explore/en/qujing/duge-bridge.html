The guide

The story

Listen first. Far below, out of sight, the Beipan River moves through its gorge. Up here the sound barely reaches you. What you hear instead is wind against steel cables, and the hum of tyres on a road deck that hangs in open air. On a cool morning, cloud can sit beneath the bridge, so the roadway seems to float above a white sea.

This is the Duge Bridge, also called the Beipanjiang Bridge Duge. It crosses the deep gorge of the Beipan River, whose name means the Northern Pan River. It links Xuanwei in Yunnan Province, a city administered by Qujing, with Liupanshui in neighbouring Guizhou Province. Stand near either end and you are close to a provincial border. The western half belongs to Yunnan. The eastern half belongs to Guizhou.

Now the number that brings most visitors here. The road deck runs about 565 metres above the river below. Picture it this way. You could stand the Eiffel Tower on the riverbed, and its tip would still fall far short of the roadway. When the bridge opened to traffic at the end of 2016, it became the highest bridge in the world, measured from deck to water.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Height above the valley and the height of a structure are two different records. The towers of Duge are tall, but they are not the world's tallest bridge towers. The record comes from the depth of the gorge itself. The engineers did not build upward to reach the sky. They reached across a chasm that the river had cut down into the earth over a very long time.

Look at the shape of the bridge. It is a cable-stayed design. Two concrete towers rise from the slopes, one on each side of the gorge. From each tower, fans of steel cables run down and hold the deck directly. The main span between the two towers is about 720 metres. Below the road, a steel truss frame gives the deck its stiffness against the wind that funnels through the canyon.

Why build something so extreme here? The answer lies in the land. This corner of southwest China is karst country. Karst is a landscape of limestone, slowly dissolved by water into sharp peaks, sinkholes and deep gorges. For travellers, that meant long, winding mountain roads. Crossing from one side of this valley to the other once took several hours. The bridge cut that trip to a small fraction of the time.

The bridge carries an expressway, part of a long east-west route that runs across southern China toward the border with Myanmar. So the traffic you see is mostly ordinary. Trucks carry goods. Families drive to visit relatives. Buses move between towns. For the people who live here, Duge is not a monument. It is simply the road home, made faster.

In fact, Guizhou is often described as a province of bridges. Its mountains and rivers forced engineers to span one valley after another. Many of the highest bridges on Earth stand within this region. In 2025 a newer crossing elsewhere in Guizhou, the Huajiang Grand Canyon Bridge, opened even higher above its river. Duge held the title for nearly a decade, and it remains one of the most dramatic crossings in the world.

Building at this height brought hard problems. Workers had to raise towers on steep, uneven slopes. The deck sections were assembled out over empty space, piece by piece, extending from each tower until the two halves met in the middle. Wind is a constant force in a gorge like this. The design had to keep the deck steady while thousands of vehicles crossed each day, in rain, fog and summer heat.

Let your eyes drop toward the valley. The slopes are steep and green, with patches of farmland on any ground that is flat enough to plant. Small villages cling to the hillsides. Their tiled roofs look tiny from the deck. The river itself is often hard to see, a thin line far below. That contrast, between a modern expressway and an old farming landscape, is part of what makes this place feel unusual.

The light changes the bridge through the day. Early morning often brings mist that fills the gorge. On those days the towers rise out of the cloud, and the cables appear to vanish into it. At midday the steel looks pale and sharp against the sky. Late in the afternoon, low sun lights the cables one by one, so they shine like thin strings across the valley.

Most people experience Duge from a car, crossing in less than a minute. The view comes and goes quickly. If you can, find a safe viewpoint on the hillsides near either end. From there, the full span becomes clear. You see the two towers, the long deck and the sheer drop below. Only from the side do you understand how much empty space the bridge holds up.

Think, too, about the scale of time in this view. The gorge took an enormous span of years to form, as water slowly cut through limestone. The bridge took only a few years to build. The two now sit together. One is the work of a river. The other is the work of people who wanted to cross it.

Before you leave, pause and listen once more. The wind still moves through the cables. A truck rumbles past, and the deck carries on as if nothing has happened. Somewhere far below, the river keeps running, as it did long before anyone built a road here. As you walk away, look back once. Notice how small the vehicles look against that long, thin line of steel.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning for a chance of cloud filling the gorge.
Seek a hillside viewpoint to see the full span.
Never stop on the expressway deck to take photos.
Bring a warm layer; wind through the gorge feels cold.
Check fog forecasts, since mist can hide the whole valley.
