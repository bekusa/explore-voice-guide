The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Oak leaves move overhead, a bus brakes somewhere on the edge of the square, and footsteps tap across the paths. In front of you is a building of grey stone. Up close, the blocks are rough and speckled, with faint bands of darker mineral running through them. Touch one if you can. It feels cool and gritty. This is the North Carolina State Capitol, and that stone was quarried only a short distance from where you are standing.

The building sits at the very centre of Raleigh, and that is no accident. Raleigh is a planned capital. In the late eighteenth century, the state chose this spot and laid out a grid of streets around a central square. That square is Union Square, where you are now. The main streets point toward it. If you look south down Fayetteville Street, you can see how the city was designed to lead your eye straight to this building.

This was not the first statehouse on the square. An earlier, simpler building stood here first. In 1831, a fire destroyed it. The loss was painful in another way, too. Inside that older building stood a marble statue of George Washington by the Italian sculptor Antonio Canova, one of the most celebrated artists of his age. The fire damaged it beyond repair. Keep that statue in mind... it comes back later in this story.

After the fire, North Carolina decided to build something lasting. Construction began in the 1830s, and the building was completed in 1840. The design came from the New York firm of Ithiel Town and Alexander Jackson Davis, two of the leading architects in America at that time. Later, a Scottish architect named David Paton took charge on site. He refined many of the details you see today, especially inside.

Now look at the style. This is Greek Revival architecture. In simple terms, that means the builders copied the forms of ancient Greek temples — columns, triangular roof fronts, and clean, heavy lines. In the young United States, this style was popular for public buildings. Many Americans saw ancient Athens as the birthplace of democracy, so a Greek temple felt like the right home for a government. Notice the porticoes, the porches held up by columns, on the east and west sides.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. The stone had to travel from the quarry to this square, and moving huge granite blocks by wagon was slow and hard. So the builders laid a short rail line to carry them. It is often described as one of the first railways in the state. Horses pulled the cars along the tracks. Locals reportedly paid to ride on it for fun when it was not hauling stone, a small taste of the railway age before it truly arrived.

Step back and take in the shape of the whole building. From above, it forms a cross, with four wings meeting in the middle. Over that centre rises the dome. Beneath it is the rotunda, a round hall that rises through the full height of the building. The plan is simple and balanced. Each side looks almost the same as the one opposite, and that symmetry gives the building its calm, steady feeling.

If you go inside, the rotunda is where the Washington story returns. A copy of the Canova statue now stands here, made from casts based on the lost original. It shows Washington in the clothing of an ancient Roman general, writing on a tablet. To modern eyes, it can look strange to see an American president dressed like a Roman. But in the early nineteenth century, this was a way to give him the dignity of the classical world.

Climb to the second floor and you reach the old legislative chambers — one for the House of Representatives, one for the Senate. For more than a century, the state's laws were debated in these rooms. Look at the desks, the galleries above, and the columns. The legislature worked here until the 1960s, when it moved into a new building a few blocks away. The chambers have been preserved, so the rooms still feel ready for the next debate.

The building also lived through hard years. During the Civil War, North Carolina joined the Confederacy, and decisions made in these rooms shaped that period. In the spring of 1865, Union troops under General William Tecumseh Sherman entered Raleigh. Unlike some Southern cities, Raleigh was not burned. The capitol survived intact. That is one reason the building you see today is so close to its original form, inside and out.

Here is a second surprise. This is not only a museum. The governor of North Carolina still has an office in this building. So while visitors walk through restored rooms, real government work continues on the first floor. Few state capitols in America keep this kind of link between past and present. The building was also named a National Historic Landmark, a national recognition of its importance in American architecture and history.

Before you leave, walk the grounds of Union Square. Monuments and statues stand among the trees, and they are worth reading slowly. One well-known group honours three presidents connected to North Carolina — Andrew Jackson, James Knox Polk, and Andrew Johnson. The monuments here have also changed over time. A large Confederate monument once stood on these grounds. It was removed in 2020, part of a wider national debate about which histories public spaces should celebrate.

Now look up at the building one more time. Notice how the grey stone changes colour as the light moves, warmer in the late afternoon, cooler under cloud. Listen to the city around you, the traffic, the voices, the wind in the oaks. Raleigh grew outward from this one square. As you walk away down any of these streets, glance back now and then. You will see the dome sitting quietly at the centre, just where the planners intended it to be.
