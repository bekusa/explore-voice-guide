The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your feet as you walk the last stretch toward the church. Then look up. Three pointed roofs rise above the trees like a cluster of tents, grey and dark against the sky. Around them, stone buttresses lean outward in rows. Each buttress ends in a thin spire. From here the building looks less like a church and more like a camp of stone, set on the edge of a hill.

This is the Church of Saint Barbara, and to understand it, you need to know one word: silver. In the Middle Ages, the ground under Kutná Hora held some of the richest silver deposits in Europe. Miners dug deep shafts beneath this town. The silver they found filled the treasury of the Bohemian kings. It also turned a mining settlement into one of the wealthiest towns in Central Europe.

Saint Barbara is the patron saint of miners. According to her legend, she was an early Christian martyr. Her own father locked her in a tower, and she died for her faith. Over the centuries, people who worked in danger — miners, gunners, those who handled explosives — came to pray to her for protection. For the men who climbed down into the dark shafts of Kutná Hora, she was a natural choice. This church is their church.

Work began in 1388. That detail matters. The church was not built by a king or a bishop. The miners and the townspeople paid for it. And they had a clear ambition. They wanted a cathedral that could stand beside Saint Vitus Cathedral in Prague. A town of miners wanted to show the capital what silver could build.

The early design is linked to the Parler workshop. Peter Parler was the master builder behind Saint Vitus in Prague, and his family workshop shaped Gothic building across Bohemia. Then history stopped the work. In the fifteenth century, the Hussite Wars swept through the region. The Hussites were followers of the reformer Jan Hus, and their struggle with the Church and the crown lasted for years. Building here stopped for decades.

Work started again in the 1480s. Two master builders gave the church much of what you see today. Matěj Rejsek worked on the choir and its vaulting. Later, Benedikt Ried, a royal architect who also worked at Prague Castle, designed the great vault over the main nave. Then the silver began to run out. Money became short again. The church was closed off with a temporary wall, and for a long time it stood unfinished.

Here is the first surprise. The western front you see today is not medieval. The church was only completed around the turn of the twentieth century, in a neo-Gothic style. Neo-Gothic means a later revival that copied the medieval forms. So this building took more than five hundred years to reach its final shape. Look at the stone, and you may see slight differences in colour and wear between the older and newer parts.

Now step inside. The first thing most people notice is the light. Tall windows fill the walls, and the space feels high and open. Then look up at the ceiling. Benedikt Ried's vault is made of stone ribs that curve and cross each other. They form a pattern like flowers or stars spread across the ceiling. Among the ribs, you will find painted coats of arms. These belong to local families and to the guilds of the town, the trade groups that helped pay for the work.

The second surprise is in the side chapels. Some of the old wall paintings do not show only saints. They show working people. In one chapel, painters showed the men who struck coins at the royal mint. You can see them at their tables, working with hammers and metal. These are some of the most direct images we have of everyday labour in medieval Bohemia. The church was a place of prayer, but it was also a record of the town's work.

Look also for the figure of a miner. Inside the church, a statue shows a medieval miner in his working clothes. He wears a white hooded garment and an apron of leather. He carries a small lamp. Miners in Kutná Hora wore white so that they could see each other in the darkness of the shafts. It is a quiet reminder of the real people under this ground.

Walk back outside and follow the path that leads to the church. On one side stands a long building. This is the former Jesuit college. The Jesuits, a Catholic religious order, came to Kutná Hora in the seventeenth century. Along the terrace in front of their college, a line of Baroque statues of saints faces the valley. Many visitors compare this walk to the Charles Bridge in Prague. Here, though, it is usually much quieter.

From this terrace, the view opens over the old town and the valley below. You can see red roofs, church towers, and green slopes. Under all of it, the old mine shafts still run through the hill. Since 1995, the historic centre of Kutná Hora, together with this church and the Cathedral of Our Lady at Sedlec, has been part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site. It is recognised for what the silver built here.

When you have time, walk the full way around the outside of the church. The east end is the best place to see the flying buttresses. These are stone arms that reach out from the walls and hold up the high vault inside. Without them, the walls could not be so thin and so full of glass. On a sunny day, the shadows of the buttresses fall across the grass in long, even stripes.

Before you leave, stop for a moment and look back at those three tent-shaped roofs. Think of the miners who climbed down into the dark each morning, and of the town that put its silver into this stone. The silver is gone now. The church is still here. Listen to the wind around the spires, and the voices on the terrace, as you walk slowly back toward the town.
