The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Outside the gate, the old city is loud — motorbike horns, the tap of a jeweller's hammer, a vendor calling out prices for tea. Then you step through, and the noise drops away. Your feet meet cool stone. In front of you, a wide courtyard opens to the sky, and a pool of still water holds the reflection of a white facade. This is the Mahabat Khan Mosque, in the heart of Peshawar's walled city.

The mosque belongs to the Mughal era. The Mughals were a dynasty that ruled much of South Asia for roughly three centuries, and they left behind the Taj Mahal, the Red Fort, and gardens across the region. This building dates to around the 1630s, during the reign of the emperor Shah Jahan. It takes its name from Mahabat Khan, a Mughal nobleman who served as governor of Peshawar. Paying for a grand mosque was one way a governor showed both faith and power.

Think about where Peshawar sits. The city lies near the eastern end of the Khyber Pass, one of the oldest routes between Central Asia and the plains of the subcontinent. Armies, merchants, pilgrims, and poets all passed this way. For the Mughals, Peshawar was a frontier city and a gateway. A mosque here was not only a place of prayer. It was also a statement, placed on the road where every traveller would see it.

Now look at the prayer hall across the courtyard. The facade is bright and pale, framed by two tall minarets — the slender towers from which the call to prayer was once given. Above the hall sit domes, low and rounded, typical of Mughal design. The central arch is larger than the others. It draws your eye straight to the entrance, and that is intentional. Mughal builders loved symmetry, and almost everything here mirrors itself from left to right.

The courtyard is the part most visitors remember. It is broad and open, paved in stone, with the ablution pool at its centre. Worshippers wash their hands, faces, and feet here before prayer. Watch for a while and you may see this quiet ritual. Someone rolls up a sleeve, splashes water, and moves on toward the hall. The pool also does a second job. On a clear day, it doubles the facade, so the mosque seems to stand twice.

Here is something many visitors miss. Step closer to the prayer hall and look up at the interior surfaces. They carry painted decoration — floral patterns, vines, and geometric designs in soft colours. Islamic art in mosques usually avoids images of people and animals. Instead, artists filled walls with plants, flowers, and repeating shapes. Up close, you can see the brushwork. Further back, the patterns blend into a single texture, like embroidered cloth.

The mosque has lived through many rulers since the Mughals. In the early nineteenth century, Peshawar came under the control of the Sikh Empire, based in Lahore. During this period, the city was governed for some years in the 1830s by an Italian soldier of fortune named Paolo Avitabile. He was known for harsh justice. Accounts from the time, often repeated by locals, say that the minarets of this mosque were used for executions. It is a dark story, and it is worth hearing with care.

After the Sikhs came the British, and later, in 1947, the new state of Pakistan. Through all of these changes, the mosque kept working as a mosque. That is perhaps the second surprise. This is not a museum or a ruin. It is a living place of worship. People still come here for daily prayers, and on Fridays the courtyard can fill with worshippers. The building has been repaired and restored at different times, and some surfaces show newer work beside older stone.

Take a moment to notice the edges of the courtyard. Arcades and rooms line the sides, creating shade and a sense of enclosure. Mughal mosques often used this plan. The open centre gives light and air. The covered edges give shelter from Peshawar's strong summer sun. Stand in the shade and look back across the courtyard. The bright stone, the dark arches, and the open sky make a strong contrast, especially in the middle of the day.

Now think about the streets outside. The mosque stands close to the old bazaars of the walled city. The lanes nearby are known for jewellery shops, with gold and silver work displayed behind glass. Further out are markets for spices, cloth, copper pots, and dried fruit. For centuries, trade and prayer have lived side by side here. A merchant could close a deal in the morning and step into the courtyard for prayer at noon.

If you are visiting, a few points of courtesy help. Dress modestly, with arms and legs covered. Women usually cover their hair. Shoes come off before you enter the prayer areas, so wear something easy to slip on and off. Avoid prayer times if you want to walk around freely, and ask before photographing anyone. Most people here are welcoming, and a polite greeting goes a long way.

Before you leave, walk once more to the edge of the pool. Look at the water, then up at the real facade, then back at the reflection. Notice how the minarets stretch down into the water. Notice the small details at the top of each tower, and the way the domes sit just behind the central arch. These are the same forms that a traveller from the Khyber road would have seen nearly four centuries ago.

And now, simply stand still for a moment. Feel the stone under your feet, cooler here than in the street. Listen to the soft sounds — water, footsteps, perhaps a low voice reciting prayers. When you step back through the gate, the noise of the bazaar will rush in again. Carry a little of this quiet with you as you walk back into the old city.
