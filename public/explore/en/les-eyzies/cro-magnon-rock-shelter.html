The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look up. Above you, a pale overhang of limestone leans outward like the brim of a stone hat. The rock is cool, slightly damp, streaked with grey and honey-coloured stains where water has moved through it for thousands of years. Somewhere nearby, a train may rattle past. Keep that sound in mind. It is part of this story.

You are standing at the Cro-Magnon rock shelter, in the village of Les Eyzies, in the Dordogne region of southwest France. In French it is called an abri, which simply means a shelter. It is not a deep cave. It is a hollow under a cliff, open to the light, the kind of place where people could stay dry, keep a fire going, and still watch the valley below. And from this modest hollow came a name that is now known around the world.

Here is the first surprise. Nobody came here looking for ancient humans. In 1868, workers were building a railway line through the valley. They needed fill for the embankment, so they began clearing rubble and earth from the foot of this cliff. As they dug, they uncovered the back of the shelter. Inside were bones, stone tools, and objects that had clearly been placed or left by people. That railway is still here today, which is why the sound of a passing train belongs to this place.

News of the discovery reached the scholars who were already working in the area. The valley had become a centre for a new science, the study of human prehistory. A young geologist named Louis Lartet led the excavation. He was the son of Édouard Lartet, one of the pioneers of this field. Louis carefully recorded what lay in the deposits, and what he found changed how people understood their own past.

The shelter held the remains of several individuals, including adults and a child. One skeleton, an older man, became especially famous. Researchers later called him simply the Old Man of Cro-Magnon. His skull showed a tall forehead, a rounded braincase, and a face much like our own. These were not a different species. They were anatomically modern humans, people of our own kind, who lived in Europe during the last Ice Age.

That is the second surprise. The word Cro-Magnon is now used in books and films to describe early modern Europeans in general. But it began as the name of this one small spot. In the old regional language, Occitan, a cro is a hollow or cavity. Magnon is generally explained as the name of a local family or landowner. So the famous term really means something close to Magnon's hollow, a farmer's corner of the cliff.

How old are these people? Modern dating places them roughly twenty-eight thousand years ago, in the period archaeologists call the Upper Palaeolithic, the later part of the Old Stone Age. Picture the valley then. The climate was much colder. Reindeer, horses, and other large animals moved through open landscapes. People here hunted, made tools from flint and bone, and returned to sheltered spots like this one season after season.

Alongside the bones, the excavators found small marine shells with holes drilled through them. These were almost certainly beads, sewn onto clothing or strung as ornaments. Think about what that means. This valley lies far from the sea. The shells travelled a long way, by trade or by people walking great distances. And someone took the time to pierce them and wear them. These were people who cared how they looked, and perhaps who they belonged to.

The original bones are not displayed here. They left the valley long ago and are kept in national collections in Paris. What you see on site is the shelter itself, along with interpretation that helps you picture its past. For the objects and tools of this era, the National Museum of Prehistory in Les Eyzies is a short walk away. Its building is set into the cliff above the village, and it gives the wider context for everything found here.

Now look at the setting around you. Les Eyzies sits where the Vézère River runs between tall limestone cliffs. These cliffs are full of overhangs, ledges, and caves. The same geology that created this shelter created dozens of others along the valley. Together they form a group of prehistoric sites and decorated caves listed by UNESCO as World Heritage. Few places on Earth hold so many traces of Ice Age life in such a small area.

Notice how the village itself still uses the cliff. Houses are built right against the rock. Some walls are partly natural stone, partly masonry. Roofs tuck under overhangs that are older than any human memory. In a way, the people of Les Eyzies continue a very old habit. They let the cliff carry part of the weight and give part of the shelter, just as the hunters did here so long ago.

There is something quietly moving about the scale of this place. It is not grand. There are no towers or carved columns. It is a hollow in the rock, the size of a large room. Yet the people who rested here are among the first of our own kind ever identified by science. When you hear the name Cro-Magnon, it points back to this exact patch of ground, and to a few workers with shovels on a railway job.

Before you go, take one more look at the overhang. Notice the colour of the stone, the dark lines of moisture, the way the light changes as it reaches the back wall. Listen for the river, and perhaps for another train. As you walk back into the village, look at the cliffs above the rooftops. Every ledge and shadow up there is a possible shelter, and many still keep their stories to themselves.

Practical tips
