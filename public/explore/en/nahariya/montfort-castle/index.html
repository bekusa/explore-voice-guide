The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, water runs over stones in the bottom of a deep green valley. Around you, oak and terebinth trees rustle on the slopes, and somewhere a jay calls. Under your feet, the ground is rough limestone, pale and broken. You are standing on a narrow ridge in the hills of the western Galilee, and the ruins in front of you belong to Montfort, a castle built by crusading knights in the thirteenth century.

The coast and the town of Nahariya lie to the west, but here the world feels far away. The ridge drops steeply on three sides. The stream below is the Kziv, and the valley is now a protected nature reserve. This setting is the first thing to understand about Montfort. Its builders did not choose a crossroads or a harbour. They chose a quiet spine of rock that rises out of the forest, where an enemy would struggle to climb.

So who built it? The castle belonged to the Teutonic Knights, a military religious order that grew out of the Crusades. Like the better-known Templars and Hospitallers, they were monks who also fought. They took vows of poverty and obedience, and they also carried swords. The order was mainly German, and its members gave the place a German name — Starkenberg, which means something like strong mountain. The French name, Montfort, carries the same idea.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Montfort was not first a fortress. In the early thirteenth century, the knights acquired this land as an estate, with fields and villages around it. Only later did they turn the hilltop into a stronghold. The Crusader states were shrinking at this time. Jerusalem had been lost, and the main Christian centre was the port of Acre, down on the coast. The knights wanted a place inland that belonged only to them.

And Montfort became exactly that. Other orders shared power in Acre with kings, nobles, and merchants. Here, the Teutonic Knights answered to no one but their own leaders. The castle became the centre of the order in the Holy Land. It held their treasury and their archive — the documents, charters, and records that proved what they owned. For a religious order, those papers mattered as much as gold. This quiet ridge was, in a sense, their headquarters and their safe.

Now look at the shape of the ruins. The castle runs along the ridge in a long line, because the rock gives it no room to spread. At the eastern end, where the ridge joins the higher hills, the builders cut a deep ditch into the stone. This was the weakest side, the only approach on fairly level ground. The ditch and a massive tower behind it closed that gap. On the other sides, the steep slopes did the work of walls.

As you walk among the stones, notice the cut blocks. Some are large and carefully squared, with smooth faces. Crusader masons often shaped their stone with a raised centre and a flat border around the edges. You may see these finished blocks lying beside rough rubble and fallen fragments. Inside the castle there were halls, storerooms, and cisterns to collect rainwater. A fortress on a ridge has no well, so every drop from winter rain had to be saved.

The end came with Baybars, the Mamluk sultan of Egypt and Syria. The Mamluks were a dynasty of soldier rulers, and in the 1260s Baybars began to take Crusader castles one after another. His forces attacked Montfort in 1266, but the castle held. In 1271 they returned. This time the siege worked. Miners dug under the walls, and the defenders saw that they could not last. They agreed to surrender.

The terms of that surrender are the second surprise. According to the accounts, the knights were allowed to leave and go to Acre. They took their archive with them. Because those records were carried out, many of them survived the fall of the Crusader states and later reached Europe. So the castle fell, but the memory of the order's property and rights did not. Baybars then ordered the walls pulled down, so that no army could use the fortress again.

That is why Montfort looks the way it does today. It was not simply worn away by time. It was deliberately broken. Fallen blocks lie where they tumbled down the slopes, and trees now grow between them. In the twentieth century, an expedition from the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York excavated part of the site. Some of the objects found here, including carved stone and pieces of armour and glass, went to that museum.

Take a moment to look down into the valley. Near the stream, below the castle, stands another stone building with arches. Scholars believe it served the knights' estate, perhaps as a mill or a guesthouse, or both over time. A castle like this needed bread, water, and work in the fields. The soldiers on the ridge depended on farmers, millers, and servants in the valley. The ruins show both halves of that life.

Getting here is part of the experience. Most visitors reach the castle by trail, walking down through the forest and across rocky paths. The way is steep in places, and the climb back is the hard part. Good shoes, water, and a hat make a real difference, especially in the warm months. In spring, the slopes are green and flowers grow between the rocks. In summer, the shade under the trees is very welcome.

As you stand here, think about distance. From the high points, you look toward the west, where the land falls toward the sea. The knights could not see Acre from every corner, but they knew it was there, a day's ride away. It was their lifeline and their last refuge. When they left Montfort in 1271, they walked toward that coast. Twenty years later, Acre itself fell.

So before you turn back, stay still for a minute. Hear the water again in the valley below. Feel the uneven stone under your feet, cut by hands more than seven hundred years ago. Look at a single fallen block and imagine the mason who squared it. Then, as you walk away along the ridge, glance back once. The broken towers sit quietly among the trees, and the forest keeps growing around them.
