The guide

The story

Listen first. Water moves in and out of the flat rock shelves at your feet, filling shallow pools and draining them again with a soft, steady hiss. The stone is rough and pitted, warm when the sun is high, slick with green weed near the waterline. Small crabs slide into cracks as your shadow passes. Out beyond the reef, the Mediterranean is a deep, even blue. This is Achziv, a few kilometres north of Nahariya, close to Israel's border with Lebanon.

The name is very old. A town called Achzib, or Achziv, appears in the Hebrew Bible. In the Book of Joshua it is listed among the places given to the tribe of Asher. The Book of Judges adds an honest admission: Asher did not drive out the people who lived there. So, in the Bible's own telling, Achziv stayed a Canaanite and Phoenician place, a coastal town with its own people and its own trade.

That coastal setting explains almost everything here. Look at how the land meets the sea. Low headlands and rock reefs create sheltered coves, and small streams once reached the shore nearby. For ancient sailors working in small wooden boats, a protected landing and fresh water were valuable. Achziv sat on the sea route between the Phoenician cities to the north, such as Tyre and Sidon, and the ports further south. This was a working harbour town.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The most important finds from Achziv are not the walls you can see, but the graves you cannot. Archaeologists have excavated cemeteries around the ancient town from the Iron Age, roughly the early first millennium before the common era. They found pottery, small figurines, jewellery and burial goods with strong Phoenician character. Some of these objects are now in museum collections, so the story of Achziv is partly told far from this beach.

Achziv also appears in a famous Assyrian record. Assyria was the great empire based in what is now northern Iraq. Its king Sennacherib campaigned along this coast at the end of the eighth century before the common era. His royal inscriptions list a town called Akzibu among the coastal cities that fell under his control. It is a small entry in a long list, but it shows that Achziv was known to the powers of the ancient world.

The town did not end with the ancient empires. Through the Roman and later periods, people kept living on this mound beside the sea. In the Middle Ages, when European Crusaders held parts of this coast, the place had a different name in their records. Later, an Arab village called az-Zeeb grew up here, its name an echo of the ancient one. The layers of settlement sit on top of each other, like pages pressed together.

The village of az-Zeeb was emptied during the war of 1948, and most of its houses no longer stand. As you walk the park, you will see a few old stone structures that survived. Take a moment with them. They are a quiet reminder that this beach has meant home to many different communities, and that the recent past here is as real as the ancient one.

Now, a story that sounds like legend, but is true. After 1948, a man named Eli Avivi settled in one of the remaining buildings near the shore. Over the years he declared his small patch of beach an independent place he called Achzivland. It was never recognised as a country, of course. But for decades travellers came to stay, to talk, and to see his collection of objects found along this coast. Avivi died in 2018, and his story is still part of local conversation.

Today most of the area is protected as Achziv National Park. Green lawns roll down toward the water, shaded in places by trees, and families spread out blankets on weekends. The contrast is striking. On one side, quiet grass and picnic baskets. On the other, raw black and grey rock beaten by the waves. Between them, the slightly raised ground of the ancient mound, where three thousand years of history lie under your feet.

The rock shelves deserve a closer look. They are flat platforms cut by the sea itself, as waves wear down the soft coastal stone over long periods. At low water, the pools fill with life. You may see small fish, sea anemones, snails, and darting crabs. Walk slowly and watch where you step. The surface is sharp in some places and slippery in others, and the edges can drop suddenly into deeper water.

This stretch of coast also matters to sea turtles. Loggerhead and green turtles, two species found in the Mediterranean, come ashore on Israeli beaches in summer to lay their eggs. Conservation teams work along this coastline to protect the nests and help the young turtles reach the sea. If you see marked or fenced areas on the sand, they may be part of this work. Please give them space.

Lift your eyes northward along the shore. The white cliffs of Rosh HaNikra rise near the border, bright against the sea on a clear day. South, the town of Nahariya stretches along the coast. Achziv sits between them, a pause in the built-up shoreline. In the late afternoon, the light turns gold over the water, and the rock pools catch the colour of the sky like scattered mirrors.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the edge of the rock. Feel the breeze off the water, salty and cool. Listen again to the pools filling and emptying. Sailors, farmers, villagers, and dreamers have all stood near this spot and looked at the same horizon. As you walk back across the grass, notice the slight rise of the ground. That gentle slope is the old town, still here, still holding its secrets.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear shoes with grip for the sharp rock shelves.
Come in late afternoon for warm light over the pools.
Bring a towel for swimming in the sheltered coves.
Pair your visit with nearby Rosh HaNikra to the north.
Keep clear of any fenced sea turtle nesting areas.
