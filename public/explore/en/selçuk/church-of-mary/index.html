The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, on the quieter northern edge of ancient Ephesus, the crowds of the main marble street feel far away. Wind moves through the dry grass. Lizards flick across warm stone. Underfoot, the ground is uneven, a mix of packed earth, broken brick and pale marble blocks. In front of you stretches a long, narrow ruin, much longer than it is wide. At first glance it looks like a row of walls with no clear shape. Yet this is one of the most important places in the story of Christianity.

This is the Church of Mary, sometimes called the Council Church. It stands near the old harbour side of the city, close to the great Roman baths and gymnasium complex. In ancient times, ships came into Ephesus through a harbour channel not far from here. Today the sea lies several kilometres away. Centuries of silt from the river filled the harbour and pushed the coastline west. So try to picture water, masts and warehouses where you now see fields and trees.

Here is the first surprise. This building did not begin as a church at all. The long shape you see comes from an earlier Roman structure. Archaeologists believe it was a vast hall or colonnade, built during the Roman imperial period, probably around the second century. It was part of a large sacred precinct. Later, Christians took over the ruined hall and fitted a church inside it. That is why the church is so strangely long and thin. The builders reused the walls they already had.

Why does this place matter so much? In the year 431, a major council of the Christian church met in Ephesus. It is known as the Council of Ephesus, the third of the great early church councils. Bishops travelled from across the Roman world to attend. Tradition and most historians place the meeting in this church. The debates were heated, political and, at times, bitter. Rival bishops even held separate sessions. This was not a calm gathering of quiet scholars.

At the centre of the argument was a single word. The word was Theotokos, a Greek title that means God-bearer, or Mother of God. A bishop of Constantinople named Nestorius had objected to calling Mary by this title. His opponents, led by Cyril, the bishop of Alexandria, insisted on it. The council sided with Cyril. Nestorius was condemned. The decision shaped how many Christians would speak about Mary, and about Christ, for the next fifteen centuries.

Think about that for a moment. Words decided here, among these walls, still echo in prayers today. In Orthodox and Catholic churches around the world, the title Mother of God is spoken every week. Much of that language traces back to arguments held in this hall. The church is also often described as one of the earliest churches dedicated to Mary. That dedication fits the city well. Ephesus has strong traditions linked to her and to the apostle John.

Now walk slowly along the length of the ruin. Notice how the walls change. Some sections are built of large, neatly cut stone blocks. Others are made of thin red bricks set in thick bands of mortar. This mix shows different phases of building. The church was damaged, repaired and rebuilt over time. In a later period, when the city was shrinking, people built a smaller church inside part of the larger one. They did not need the full space anymore. Ephesus was slowly emptying.

Look for the brick structures near the western part of the site. Here you can see the remains of that later, smaller church. Brick piers and arches once supported a roof, possibly with a dome. The work feels heavier and more practical than the elegant Roman stone around it. It tells a quiet story. The great port city was declining. Its harbour was silting up. Fewer people lived here, and they built churches to match their smaller community.

Now find the baptistery. It lies on the northern side of the church, near the area that once formed an open courtyard at the entrance. Here is the second surprise for many visitors. You can still see the shape of the baptismal pool, sunk into the floor. New Christians stepped down into the water, were baptised, and stepped out the other side. The idea was a passage from an old life into a new one. Stand at its edge and the ritual becomes easy to imagine.

As you look around, notice the pieces of marble scattered across the site. Some carry carved crosses. Others show Roman mouldings and decorations from the earlier building. Christian builders often reused older stone, so a single wall can hold pieces from different centuries. Look closely and you may spot fragments with lettering. Ephesus produced a huge number of inscriptions, in Greek and Latin. Many of the stones here have travelled from their original places and found new uses.

This part of Ephesus often feels different from the rest of the site. Many visitors walk the main route past the Library of Celsus and the Great Theatre and never come this far. That gives the Church of Mary a calmer atmosphere. You may have long stretches of time almost alone here. Birds nest in the trees. In spring, wildflowers grow between the stones. In summer, the heat rises from the ground and the cicadas are loud.

It helps to keep the wider landscape in mind. Above the town of Selçuk, on the hill, stands the Basilica of Saint John, linked by tradition to the apostle's tomb. In the hills south of Ephesus is the House of the Virgin Mary, a place of pilgrimage for both Christians and Muslims. In Islam, Mary is honoured as Maryam, the mother of the prophet Jesus. Together these places show how deeply the memory of Mary is rooted in this corner of Anatolia.

Before you leave, stand still at one end of the long nave and look toward the other. Let your eyes follow the line of the walls. This was once a roofed and painted space. It was filled with lamplight, incense and the sound of chanting voices. Now there is open sky, warm stone and the wind in the grass. As you walk back toward the main city, listen to your own footsteps on the path. Bishops, sailors and pilgrims walked this same ground long before you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the side path toward the old harbour area.
Visit early morning for cooler air and fewer people.
Bring water and a hat; there is little shade.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose stones and uneven ground.
Pair your visit with the Basilica of Saint John.
