The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere below you, a ladle clinks against a copper pot. The air smells of crushed fruit, warm bread, and dry herbs. Sunlight lands on white plastered walls and dark timber beams. Cobblestones shift slightly under your feet. Şirince sits in a bowl of green hills, about eight kilometres from the town of Selçuk. Vineyards and peach orchards climb the slopes on every side. This village introduces itself through the nose and the ears first — the eyes catch up a moment later.

For centuries, this was a Greek Orthodox village, and its old name was a joke at its own expense. It was called Çirkince, which means roughly the ugly one in Turkish. Legend has it that early settlers chose the name on purpose. They wanted to keep outsiders away from their fertile valley, so they told everyone the place was ugly. The story may or may not be true. The name, however, is well documented, and it stuck to this valley for hundreds of years.

Then came 1923. After the war between Greece and Turkey, the two countries agreed to exchange their minority populations. The Greek Christian families of this village packed what they could carry and left for Greece. Muslim families, uprooted from the region around Thessaloniki in northern Greece, arrived to take their place. They inherited houses, chapels, and orchards built by people they had never met. In 1926 the village received its current name, Şirince, which means pleasant. The old joke was finally corrected.

Look at the houses as you walk. Most follow the same pattern. The ground floor is rough stone, built for animals, tools, and barrels. The upper floor is timber-framed, plastered white, and often leans out over the lane on wooden brackets. Windows are framed in dark wood, and some upper rooms form enclosed bays that catch the light. These are Greek houses of the nineteenth century, adapted by a hundred years of Turkish family life. Few villages in the region kept this fabric so completely.

Şirince is best known today for its fruit wines. Cellars and tasting rooms occupy the ground floors of the old houses. The wines are made from peach, blackberry, mulberry, quince, melon, and more. They are sweet, low in alcohol, and poured in small glasses for tasting. Grape growing here goes back to the Greek villagers, who made wine on these same slopes. The fruit versions came later, and they helped turn a quiet farming village into a destination.

Two churches survive from the Greek era. The Church of Saint John the Baptist stands in the lower village, a stone basilica from the nineteenth century that has been restored and sometimes hosts exhibitions. Higher up the hill sit the remains of a second church, more ruined and open to the sky. Inside the restored church, look for surviving patches of painted plaster on the walls. These rooms held the village congregation until the day the whole community left.

The main lane doubles as a market. Stalls sell handmade lace, olive oil soap, dried figs, strings of red peppers, pomegranate syrup, and jars of local honey. Village women cook gözleme, a thin flatbread heated on a domed iron sheet and folded around cheese or greens. Olive oil is serious business here — the surrounding groves feed small local presses, and tasting is encouraged. Prices are negotiable, politely. The sellers have heard every opening offer many times before.

In December 2012, this quiet village briefly became one of the most watched places on Earth. Believers in the so-called Mayan calendar prophecy claimed the world would end on 21 December of that year. Şirince, they said, had a positive energy that would spare it. Thousands of visitors and journalists poured in for the date. Hotels filled, wine sold out, and the world did not end. Locals still tell the story with a straight face and a small smile.

Just over the hill sits one of the most unusual schools in Turkey. The Nesin Mathematics Village, founded by the mathematician Ali Nesin, teaches pure mathematics to students who come voluntarily, in summer and winter, simply for the pleasure of it. Classes meet in open stone amphitheatres among olive trees. There are no exams and no diplomas. In 2018, Nesin received a major international prize for creating the school. It lies within walking distance of the village.

Beyond the shops, the rhythm here is still agricultural. Autumn brings the grape and olive harvests, when trailers of fruit rattle down the lanes. Spring covers the hills with wild flowers and blossom from the peach orchards. Many families still press their own oil and dry their own herbs on rooftops. Step off the main lane and you find chickens, woodpiles, and courtyards where daily life continues much as it did before the visitors arrived.

Climb, if you can, toward the upper edge of the village. The lanes narrow, the crowds thin, and the paving turns to rough stone. From up there, the view takes in the whole bowl of the valley — tiled roofs stacked down the slope, white walls between cypress and fruit trees, and vineyards running to the ridgeline. This view costs only a short, steep walk. Wear shoes with grip, because generations of feet have polished these stones smooth.

Before you leave, find a wall in the shade and stand still for a minute. Listen again — the same ladle, the same birds, a scooter climbing the hill. Two communities built this place, one after the other, and neither would call it ugly today. The valley keeps its old habits of fruit, oil, wine, and conversation. You are inside that continuity now, one more voice passing along the lane. Take a slow breath of it before the road carries you back down.
