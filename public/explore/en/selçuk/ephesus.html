The guide

The story

The marble under your feet is worn smooth by two thousand years of footsteps. Stand still for a moment and feel the groove in the stone — chariot wheels carved these ruts into the Curetes Street, and your shoes rest in the same tracks Roman merchants wore down hauling goods from the harbor.

Ephesus was one of the largest cities in the ancient world, home to a quarter of a million people at its peak in the second century. The ruins you see today are only fifteen percent of what lies beneath the soil. Archaeologists estimate that most of the city still waits underground, preserved by centuries of silt from the Cayster River. What stands exposed now — the Library of Celsus, the Great Theatre, the Terrace Houses — represents the monumental core, the public face of a city that once rivaled Athens and Alexandria.

The Library of Celsus rises ahead, its two-story facade reconstructed in the nineteen-seventies from fragments scattered across the site. Built around 110 AD as a tomb for the Roman senator Tiberius Julius Celsus Polemaeanus, it held twelve thousand scrolls in niches cut into the inner walls. The four statues in the facade niches represent Wisdom, Knowledge, Intelligence, and Valor — Roman virtues rendered in marble. The building faces east so the reading rooms caught morning light, and the inner walls stand a meter away from the outer shell to protect the scrolls from humidity.

Walk past the library toward the Great Theatre, carved into the western slope of Mount Pion. It seats twenty-five thousand people. The acoustics are so precise that a coin dropped on the stage floor can be heard in the upper rows. This is where Saint Paul preached in the mid-first century, sparking a riot among silversmiths who feared his message would ruin their trade in miniature statues of Artemis. The theatre you see today is largely a Roman expansion of an earlier Greek structure — the Romans added the upper tiers and the elaborate stage building.

The Temple of Artemis, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, stood a kilometer northeast of here. Almost nothing remains — just a single reconstructed column and a marshy depression where the foundations once sat. The temple was destroyed and rebuilt multiple times, burned by arsonists, sacked by invading Goths, finally dismantled for building stone after Christianity became the empire's official religion. Ephesus was Artemis's city for centuries, and her cult statue — a many-breasted fertility figure — drew pilgrims from across the Mediterranean.

Climb the stepped path to the Terrace Houses if they are open. These are the preserved homes of Ephesus's wealthy elite, tucked into the hillside with heated floors, frescoed walls, and intricate mosaics. The houses were occupied from the first century BC until the seventh century AD, and each layer of renovation tells a story of changing tastes and fortunes. The frescoes show theatrical masks, mythological scenes, and geometric patterns in red, yellow, and blue pigments that still hold their color. Walking through these rooms, you see the private side of Roman life — the intimate scale of a bedroom, the luxury of a private bath, the care taken to display wealth through art.

The city began to decline in the third century as the harbor silted up and malaria spread through the coastal wetlands. By the time the Byzantines fortified the hill above the ruins in the sixth century, most of Ephesus had been abandoned. Earthquakes toppled what remained, and the marble was carted off to build later settlements. The site was forgotten until British archaeologist John Turtle Wood began excavations in 1863, searching for the Temple of Artemis. Systematic digs have continued ever since, peeling back layers of soil and revealing streets, fountains, brothels, public latrines, and temples.

Look for the marble sidewalks along Curetes Street, still fitted with drainage channels that carried rainwater downhill. Notice the carved reliefs on the gate arches — gods, emperors, and mythological figures watching over the flow of daily life. In the Great Theatre, find the inscriptions in Greek and Latin carved into the seat backs, marking reserved sections for guilds and civic groups. The stone here is pale gray Aegean marble, quarried from nearby mountains and polished to a reflective sheen that glows white in the afternoon sun.

Ephesus was a city of trade, religion, and spectacle — a crossroads where Greek philosophy met Roman law and Eastern mystery cults. It was also a city of ordinary people: bakers, weavers, dockworkers, scribes. The graffiti scratched into the marble — crude drawings, names, jokes — reminds you that these ruins were once loud with conversation, thick with the smell of cooking oil and incense, crowded with bodies moving through a living urban landscape.

As you walk back toward the exit, the late afternoon light catches the fluted columns and casts long shadows across the agora. The site empties as the day cools. You are standing in a place that was inhabited for more than a thousand years, abandoned for another thousand, and now walks again under the feet of visitors from every corner of the world. The marble remembers.
