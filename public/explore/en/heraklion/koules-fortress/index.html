The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps against stone at your feet, and halyards tap against the masts of small boats in the harbour. Gulls call overhead. The breakwater under your shoes is warm, worn smooth by centuries of salt and footsteps. Ahead of you, a heavy block of honey-coloured stone sits right at the mouth of the old port. Its walls rise straight out of the sea. This is Koules, the sea fortress of Heraklion, and it has guarded this harbour entrance for about five hundred years.

The name you hear today is not the name its builders used. The Venetians, who ruled Crete for more than four centuries, called it the Rocca al Mare, which means the Fortress on the Sea. The word Koules came later, from the Ottoman Turks. It comes from the Turkish word for a tower. So one building carries two empires in its two names. Locals simply say Koules, and so will we.

Why is a Venetian fortress here at all? Venice was a city of merchants and sailors, and Crete sat in the middle of its trade routes. Ships moved between the Adriatic Sea and the ports of the eastern Mediterranean, and Crete was a safe place to stop. The Venetians called the island Candia, the same name they gave to this city. Heraklion became one of the most important harbours in their overseas empire. A harbour like that needed protection.

The fortress you see now was built in the early sixteenth century. It replaced an older defensive structure on the same spot. By that time, warfare had changed. Cannon could break the tall, thin walls of medieval castles. So the Venetian engineers built low and thick. Look at the walls closely. They are massive, and they slope slightly outward at the base. This shape helped the stone absorb the shock of cannon fire instead of cracking.

Now look up at the outer walls. Carved into the stone you will find the winged lion of Saint Mark, the symbol of Venice. Saint Mark is the patron saint of that city, and his lion appeared on Venetian buildings across the whole Mediterranean. Here, sea air has softened the carvings over the centuries. The edges are blurred, but the shape of the lion is still clear. For a Venetian sailor entering the port, this lion meant one thing. You are in Venetian waters now.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The fortress is not only a wall facing the sea. It is a working building with rooms on two levels. Inside, there are vaulted chambers that once held soldiers, food, gunpowder, and weapons. Some rooms were used as prisons. The thick walls kept the interior cool, even in the heat of a Cretan summer. When you step inside, you will probably feel the temperature drop. That coolness is part of the design.

Inside, notice the ramps. Instead of steep staircases, wide sloping passages connect the lower level to the roof. These ramps had a clear purpose. Heavy cannon had to move up to the firing positions on top. Men and animals could push or pull them up a gentle slope far more easily than up steps. It is a small piece of military logistics, built in stone. Walk up slowly and imagine the weight of iron rolling beside you.

The greatest test for this fortress came in the seventeenth century. In 1648, the Ottoman army began a siege of Candia. It became one of the longest sieges in recorded history. The city held out for more than two decades. Supplies and soldiers arrived by sea, through this very harbour. Finally, in 1669, Venice surrendered the city. The defenders were allowed to leave by ship. After that, Crete passed into Ottoman hands for more than two centuries.

Under Ottoman rule, the fortress stayed in military use. The new rulers repaired it and made changes of their own. This is when it became known as Koules. If you look carefully at the upper parts of the walls, you may notice that the stonework is not all the same. Some sections look different in colour or in the size of the blocks. These differences show repairs and additions from different periods. The building is a record of everyone who held it.

In modern times, the fortress needed help. Salt water, wind, and age had weakened the stone. A major restoration took place in the twenty-first century, and the building reopened to visitors afterwards. Today the rooms hold exhibitions about the fortress and the harbour. You may also find objects recovered from the sea nearby. The restoration tried to keep the old character of the place. So you still see rough stone, old vaults, and simple light through narrow openings.

Before you leave, go up to the roof terrace. From here, the whole story of the harbour opens out around you. Look back toward the city. Along the waterfront, you can see a row of large stone arches. These are the remains of the Venetian arsenals, the shipyards where galleys were built and repaired. Ships once rolled out of those arches straight into the water. Today cars pass in front of them, but the shape is still easy to read.

Turn the other way, and you face the open sea. On a clear day, the water changes from deep blue to pale green near the rocks. Fishing boats come and go along the breakwater. People walk out to the end of the mole, the long stone pier, to watch the sunset or simply to feel the wind. This walk has been part of daily life in Heraklion for generations. You are sharing it with the city now.

As you walk back along the breakwater, turn around once more. See how low and solid the fortress sits against the sky. It was not built to be beautiful. It was built to survive. And it did. Notice the lion on the wall one last time, the salt stains on the stone, the boats rocking in the harbour it once protected. Then listen again to the water against the rocks. That sound has not changed in five hundred years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full breakwater for the best view of the walls.
Climb to the roof terrace for views over the harbour.
Come late afternoon when the stone turns warm and golden.
Bring a hat because the breakwater has little shade.
Combine your visit with the Venetian arsenals along the waterfront.
