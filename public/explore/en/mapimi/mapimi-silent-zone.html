The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. There is wind moving across the creosote bushes, a faint dry rattle, and then almost nothing at all. The ground under your boots is pale and stony, scattered with dark pebbles that crunch with every step. Heat lifts off the flats in slow, wavering sheets. Out here, silence does not feel empty. It feels heavy, like something you could lean against.

You are standing in the Bolsón de Mapimí, a wide desert basin in northern Mexico. A bolsón is a closed basin, a low bowl of land with no river that drains out to the sea. Rain that falls here stays here, pooling briefly in shallow flats before the sun takes it back. This basin is part of the Chihuahuan Desert, and it sits near the meeting point of three states: Durango, Chihuahua, and Coahuila. The town of Mapimí, an old mining settlement, lies to the south.

Most people come because of a name. La Zona del Silencio... the Zone of Silence. Popular stories claim that radios fall quiet here, that compasses spin, that signals simply vanish into the sand. These stories spread widely in the 1970s, through magazines, books, and word of mouth. And they turned a remote stretch of desert into a place of pilgrimage for curious travellers, believers, and sceptics alike.

Here is the first surprise. Scientists who have worked in this basin for decades say the radio blackout is a myth. Radios work. Phones work where there is a tower in range. The real reason for the silence is far simpler. There are very few people, very few roads, and almost no machines. What you hear, or do not hear, is the natural sound of a large and empty desert.

So where did the legend begin? Part of the answer is a real event. In July 1970, an American test rocket launched from a range in Utah went off course and came down in this desert. It carried small radioactive sources, and the United States sent a recovery team to find the wreckage. They worked for weeks, and they removed contaminated soil from the crash site. The recovery was real. The mystery grew around it.

A crashed rocket, foreign crews, and trucks hauling away earth... it is easy to see how rumours took hold. Over the years, people added new layers. Locals say that strange lights appear in the sky at night. According to some popular tales, tall visitors once walked out of the desert and asked ranchers for water. Others talk of meteorites drawn to this ground by some hidden force. These are stories, not documented history, but they are now part of the place.

Meteorites do fall in northern Mexico, as they fall everywhere on Earth. But no study has shown that this basin attracts them. Some researchers suggest that the dark, iron-rich rocks scattered across the ground helped feed the idea of strange magnetism. Look down at your feet. Those black stones on the pale soil are worth a closer look. They are ordinary desert rock, shaped and darkened by long years of sun and wind.

Now the second surprise. The most remarkable thing here is not hidden in the sky. It is moving slowly through the scrub. This basin is home to the Bolsón tortoise, the largest land tortoise in North America. It digs deep burrows to escape the heat and comes out mostly in the cooler hours. It is rare and protected. If you are lucky enough to see one, keep your distance and let it go about its day.

Because of animals like this tortoise, much of the area is protected as the Mapimí Biosphere Reserve. It is part of a UNESCO programme that links nature protection with research and the lives of local people. For decades, scientists have studied this desert from a field station here, sometimes called the Desert Laboratory. Their work on soil, plants, rain, and wildlife has made this one of the better studied desert landscapes in Mexico.

Take a slow look around you. The plants here are built for drought. The creosote bush gives the air its sharp, resinous smell, especially after rain. Prickly pear cactus grows in low clumps with flat green pads. Lechuguilla, a tough plant with stiff, pointed leaves, grows close to the ground. Ocotillo sends up long, thin stems that stay bare for much of the year, then turn green after rain.

The light changes everything in this desert. At midday, the colours wash out to bone and grey, and the horizon shimmers. In the late afternoon, the hills turn gold and copper, and every bush throws a long shadow. After sunset, the sky becomes very dark, because there are no city lights for many kilometres. On a clear night, the stars come out in numbers that many visitors have never seen before.

A word about respect. Over the years, some visitors have taken rocks, plants, and even tortoises as souvenirs. Others have left rubbish behind, or driven off the tracks and crushed fragile ground. Desert soil can take a very long time to recover. The best way to honour this place is simple. Stay on the tracks, take nothing, leave nothing, and travel with a local guide who knows the land.

The people of Mapimí and the nearby ranches have lived beside this desert for generations. Mining shaped the town's past, and ranching and tourism shape its present. Many local guides grew up hearing the old stories, and they tell them with a smile. Ask them what they believe. You may hear a little science, a little legend, and a lot of affection for the land.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Feel the hot wind on your face and the hard ground beneath you. Notice how far your eyes can travel before they meet a hill. Whatever you believe about the Zone of Silence, the quiet here is real. As you walk back to the road, listen to your own footsteps. In this basin, they may be the loudest sound for a very long way.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit with a local guide who knows the desert tracks.
Carry far more water than you think you need.
Avoid the midday heat; come early morning or late afternoon.
Stay overnight nearby if you want a dark, starry sky.
Keep your vehicle on existing tracks to protect fragile soil.
