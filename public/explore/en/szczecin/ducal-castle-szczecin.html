The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment in the great courtyard. Underfoot, the paving is smooth and pale, worn by centuries of footsteps. Around you rise plain, cream-coloured walls, broken by rows of windows and steep roofs. Sounds bounce off the stone, so a child's laugh or a footstep carries further than you expect. Even on a busy day, this enclosed square feels calmer than the city streets just beyond the gate. This is the Ducal Castle of Szczecin, once home to the dukes of Pomerania.

Those dukes belonged to the House of Griffins. The griffin, a mythical beast with the body of a lion and the head and wings of an eagle, was their emblem. You will see it again and again in this city, on flags, on carvings and on signs. For several centuries, the Griffins ruled Pomerania, the long stretch of land along the southern Baltic coast. Their power was never as large as that of the great kingdoms around them. But here, on this hill above the river, they built a court to match their ambitions.

The story of the castle begins in the Middle Ages, in the fourteenth century. A duke named Barnim the Third built a fortified stone house on this site. The hill made sense. It watched over the Oder River, the great waterway that carried trade from the interior of Europe to the Baltic Sea. Whoever held this spot could see ships, control traffic and display their authority to every merchant passing below. Over the following generations, later dukes added new wings, towers and halls.

The castle you see today, however, owes its shape mostly to the sixteenth century. In those decades, the dukes rebuilt the old Gothic stronghold in the Renaissance style. Renaissance, in simple terms, means the taste for order, symmetry and classical ideas that spread north from Italy. Look at the courtyard walls and you can see this. The lines are calm and regular. Instead of a dark fortress, the dukes wanted a residence that looked modern, refined and fit for receiving guests from across Europe.

Now for a detail many visitors miss. Look up at the clock on the courtyard side. Above the clock face sits a carved head, a curious, slightly comic face. Its eyes move as the clock ticks, and it seems to follow the passing of time with a mischievous look. Children often spot it first. The present clock is a reconstruction, but it continues a playful tradition. In a building made for serious politics, this little face is a reminder that the court also enjoyed wit and spectacle.

The Griffin line ended in the seventeenth century. The last duke, Bogislaw the Fourteenth, died in 1637 without an heir. His death came in the middle of the Thirty Years' War, the long and brutal conflict that tore Central Europe apart. After the war, Szczecin passed to Sweden. Later, in the eighteenth century, it became part of Prussia. The castle changed with each new ruler. It served as an administrative seat, as offices, and as a symbol of whoever governed the city at the time.

Here is the second surprise. In 1729, a girl named Sophie was born in this city. Her father was a Prussian officer who served in the local garrison and later as governor. The family is closely linked to the castle, and tradition says she spent part of her childhood within these walls. Decades later, far to the east, that girl became Catherine the Great, Empress of Russia. It is strange to imagine one of the most powerful women of her age first learning to walk on stones like these.

The twentieth century brought the hardest chapter. During the Second World War, bombing raids in 1944 left much of the castle in ruins. Roofs collapsed and walls were burned out. After the war, Szczecin became part of Poland, and its population changed almost completely. The new city chose to rebuild the castle rather than clear it away. Reconstruction took decades, stretching through the second half of the century. What you see is therefore both old and new, a careful rebuilding based on historical forms.

That rebuilding gives the castle a particular meaning. For the people who came to live in Szczecin after 1945, it became a way to connect with the deep past of the place. The Griffin dukes were a Slavic dynasty in origin, and later they were closely tied to the German world. Their castle now stands as a shared piece of regional history. Many people in the city see it simply as their castle, the place they visit for a concert, a fair or a quiet walk.

Inside, one of the most moving spaces lies below ground. In the crypt, you can see the sarcophagi of members of the Griffin family. These coffins are made of metal and decorated with engraved details. They give a direct, physical sense of the dynasty that built this place. Standing among them, the castle stops being an abstract monument. It becomes the home, and the final resting place, of real people with names, families and ambitions.

If you have the energy, climb the bell tower. The stairs are steep, so take your time. From the top, the city opens out below you. You can follow the line of the Oder River, see the cranes of the port, and pick out church spires and green squares. From up here, it becomes clear why the dukes chose this hill. The river, the port and the city all turn around this single high point.

Today the castle is a cultural centre more than a residence. Its halls host exhibitions, and its courtyards fill with concerts, markets and open-air events, especially in the warmer months. The city's opera company even takes its name from the castle. So depending on when you come, you may hear rehearsals, see a stage being built, or find a crowd gathered for a festival. The building that once served a small court now serves an entire city.

Before you leave, pause once more in the courtyard. Look at the pale walls, the steep roofs and the little clock face watching from above. Listen to the footsteps around you, and to the faint sound of traffic beyond the gate. As you walk out toward the river or the old town, notice how often the griffin appears on the streets around you. The dukes are long gone, but their emblem still marks the city they shaped.
