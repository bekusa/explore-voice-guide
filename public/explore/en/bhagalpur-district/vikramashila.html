The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, on the flat plains of eastern Bihar, the loudest sounds are usually birds, a distant tractor, and the soft scrape of your own shoes on old brick. The ground is warm and dusty underfoot. Around you, low walls of red-brown brick rise from neat lawns. In the centre stands a broad, stepped mound of brick. It looks quiet now. But for roughly four centuries, this was one of the great centres of learning in the Buddhist world.

You are standing at Vikramashila, near the village of Antichak in Bhagalpur district. The Ganges flows not far to the north. Its waters once carried pilgrims, traders and students along this stretch of the plain. Today the site is a protected archaeological area. Grass grows between the excavated walls, and the bricks have faded to soft shades of rust and ochre. The scale only becomes clear when you start to walk around it.

The story begins with the Pala dynasty, a line of Buddhist kings who ruled much of Bengal and Bihar from the eighth century onward. Tradition credits King Dharmapala with founding this monastery, around the late eighth or early ninth century. The Palas were generous patrons of Buddhism. They supported several large monasteries across their lands. Vikramashila was among the most important, and royal support helped it grow into an institution that drew teachers and students from far beyond India.

Many visitors know the name Nalanda, the older monastic university to the west. Here is something people often miss. Vikramashila was not simply a smaller copy of Nalanda. It became especially known for Vajrayana Buddhism — a tradition that uses ritual, meditation and esoteric teachings as paths to awakening. Scholars here wrote, translated and debated texts that would shape Buddhism across the Himalayas for centuries to come.

Now look at the great central structure. This is a cruciform stupa — a sacred monument built in the shape of a cross when seen from above. It rises in terraces, one stepping back from the other. On each of the four sides, there were chambers that once held images of the Buddha. Some broken sculptures were found during excavation. Walk slowly around the base, and notice how the plan repeats on every side, like a carefully balanced diagram in brick.

Next, bring your eyes down to the lower walls of the stupa. Here you will find rows of terracotta plaques — small panels of fired clay, set into the brickwork. Each one carries a figure. Some show Buddhist deities. Others show Hindu gods, animals, musicians, warriors, or scenes from everyday life. This is the second surprise of the site. A deeply Buddhist monument was decorated with a wide, mixed world of images. It shows how many traditions lived side by side in this region.

Around the stupa, the monastery forms a vast square. Its outer walls once enclosed rows of small cells where monks lived and studied. Each cell was a simple room, built for sleep, meditation and reading. Picture oil lamps flickering in hundreds of doorways at night. Picture the low murmur of chanting, and the rustle of palm-leaf manuscripts being turned. The walls you see now are the lower parts of that great residential block.

Tibetan sources preserve much of what we know about life here. According to these accounts, the monastery had senior scholars who acted as gatekeepers at its entrances. A newcomer, the tradition says, had to answer their questions before being admitted. We should treat the details carefully, because these stories were written down long after the events. But the image is a lovely one — learning so valued that even the doorway was a test.

The most famous name tied to Vikramashila is Atisha Dipankara Srijnana. He was a Bengali-born master who taught here in the eleventh century. Later, he travelled across the mountains to Tibet, at the invitation of Tibetan rulers. There he taught for many years and helped renew Buddhist practice. To this day, Atisha is deeply honoured in Tibetan Buddhism. So when Tibetan pilgrims visit this site, they are coming to a place that shaped their own history.

The great age of Vikramashila ended around the start of the thirteenth century. The monastery was destroyed during the military campaigns that swept across this region at that time. Historians commonly link its fall to the forces of Bakhtiyar Khilji, a general who also attacked other monasteries in Bihar. The monks scattered. Some fled toward Nepal and Tibet, carrying knowledge with them. Over the following centuries, the buildings collapsed, and earth slowly covered the ruins.

For a long time, the exact location was debated. Then, in the twentieth century, excavation finally brought the site back into the light. Patna University began digging here in the 1960s. Later, the Archaeological Survey of India carried out larger excavations in the 1970s and early 1980s. Layer by layer, they exposed the stupa, the monastic quadrangle, the terracotta plaques, and many smaller finds. What you are walking through today is the result of that patient work.

Before you leave, consider visiting the small site museum nearby. It holds sculptures, terracotta pieces and objects recovered from the excavations. Seeing a carved face up close, at eye level, helps you imagine the decoration that once covered these walls. Then step back outside. Try standing at a corner of the monastery square and looking toward the stupa. From there, you can sense the order and the planning behind the whole complex.

Now pause for a moment where you are. Feel the sun on the old brick, and the faint breeze crossing the open plain. Monks once walked these same paths, carrying books, questions and doubts. Their ideas travelled further than they ever did, over mountains and into other languages. As you walk away, look back once at the stepped outline of the stupa against the sky... and remember that a quiet field can hold a very loud history.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning to avoid the strong afternoon heat.
Wear a hat and carry water for open ground.
Visit the site museum to see recovered sculptures.
Walk the full stupa perimeter to see every side.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven brick and grass.
