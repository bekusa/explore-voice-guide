The guide

The story

Stand near the water's edge and listen first. The Zambezi moves wide and brown here, and the air smells of river mud and diesel. Somewhere behind you, a heavy truck shifts gears. Above the reeds, a long concrete deck curves gently away toward Zambia, held up by tall pale towers and fans of steel cables. Birds cross beneath it. Fishermen in small boats still work the current, much as they did long before any bridge stood here.

This is the Kazungula Bridge. It links Kazungula in Botswana with a town of the same name on the Zambian bank. It opened in 2021, and it is one of the newest major crossings in southern Africa. For most of the twentieth century, travellers and traders crossed this stretch of river another way, by ferry. In practical terms, the bridge turned a slow and uncertain crossing into a few minutes on solid ground.

First, look at the shape. The bridge does not run straight. It bends in a long, deliberate arc. Most visitors take this for a stylistic choice, but the reason is geography. Near this point, four countries come remarkably close together: Botswana, Zambia, Namibia and Zimbabwe. The borders here are so tight that a straight line could have crossed into a neighbour's territory. So the engineers curved the deck, and the bridge stays between Botswana and Zambia alone.

That convergence of borders is worth a moment. On a map, the meeting point looks almost like a single dot where four nations touch. Surveyors and lawyers have argued over the exact details for decades. Some describe it as two separate border points only a short distance apart. What matters to you, standing here, is simpler. Within a short view of this riverbank, you can look toward four different countries, with four different flags, laws and languages.

Before the bridge, the crossing depended on pontoon ferries. These were flat vessels that carried cars, buses and heavy trucks across the river a few at a time. Drivers sometimes waited many days in long queues on both banks. Their lorries carried copper, food, fuel and building materials. Small informal markets grew up around the waiting drivers. People sold grilled meat, cold drinks and phone credit. A whole roadside economy lived on delay.

The engineering style has a name: extradosed. That word describes a hybrid between two familiar bridge types. A cable-stayed bridge uses tall towers and long cables to hold up the deck. A girder bridge uses a deep, stiff beam instead. An extradosed bridge combines both. Its towers are shorter, and its cables run at a flatter angle. Look up at the towers, and notice how low and broad the cable fans appear compared with a classic suspension bridge.

Now look down the centre of the deck. This is not only a road. Between the traffic lanes, the designers set space for a single railway track. Road and rail share one structure. The idea was to give the region a future rail link as well as a highway. For now, trucks and cars are the main traffic. Still, the rail line shows a long-term plan: to move goods across this river by train one day.

The project was a true international effort. Botswana and Zambia shared it, and international lenders helped to fund it, including the African Development Bank and Japanese development agencies. A South Korean construction company led the building work. Concrete for the piers had to be placed deep in a powerful river with seasonal floods. Engineers worked around the rains, the rising water and the heat of the Zambezi valley.

At each end, you will see more than a road. The bridge was built with a one-stop border post. In the old system, travellers stopped at two separate border posts, one for each country, and repeated every check. Here, the two governments coordinate their procedures more closely. The goal is fewer stamps, shorter waits and faster trade. For a long-distance truck driver, saved hours turn directly into lower costs and quicker deliveries.

This crossing matters well beyond these two towns. It sits on a key trade route called the North–South Corridor. That route connects ports in South Africa with the copper mines of Zambia and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Before the bridge, much of that traffic travelled around through other countries. Now it has a shorter, direct option through Botswana. In that sense, a quiet curve of concrete changes the map of regional trade.

The setting is anything but industrial. Just downstream, the Chobe River joins the Zambezi, and the town of Kasane is close by. The Chobe area is known for very large elephant herds, and the river here supports hippos, crocodiles and fish eagles. Travellers often pass this way on the road to Victoria Falls. So the same bridge carries copper trucks, safari vehicles and families visiting relatives on the other bank.

If you come in the late afternoon, watch the light. The pale concrete turns warm gold, and the cables catch the sun one by one. The river shifts from brown to copper to grey as evening arrives. Remember that this is an active border zone. Photography rules near border posts can be strict, so ask an official before you raise a camera. A respectful question usually saves a difficult conversation later.

Stand still a moment longer. Watch a truck roll slowly over the curve and shrink toward Zambia. Below it, a small boat pushes against the current, the same way people have crossed this water for generations. The bridge did not erase that older river life; it simply added a new layer above it. As you walk away, listen for the river under the traffic... it is still the oldest road here.
