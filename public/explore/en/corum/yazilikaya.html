The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The wind moves through a gap in the grey limestone, and the air turns cooler as the rock closes in around you. Underfoot, the ground is uneven and pale. Ahead, the stone walls rise on either side. If you look closely at their surface, shapes begin to appear. They are figures in a long line, worn soft by more than three thousand years of rain and frost.

This is Yazılıkaya. In Turkish, the name simply means inscribed rock. It is an open-air sanctuary of the Hittites, a Bronze Age people who ruled much of central Anatolia. Their capital, Hattusa, lies a short distance away near the modern village of Boğazkale. Yazılıkaya belonged to that capital. Today both places share one listing on the UNESCO World Heritage List. Most visitors see the great city walls first. Many leave without realising that the most intimate space in the Hittite world is right here.

Who were the Hittites? For a long time, the wider world knew them mostly as a name in the Hebrew Bible. Their empire was a major power of the ancient Near East. It stood alongside Egypt, Babylon and Assyria. Hittite kings signed treaties, fought wars and wrote letters to pharaohs. Then, around the end of the Bronze Age, the empire collapsed. Hattusa was abandoned. The carvings here stayed in the rock, quietly waiting.

Notice that nobody built these walls. The sanctuary uses natural chambers in the limestone outcrop. The Hittites treated sacred rock and springs as places where gods lived. Here they did not need to raise a temple. The mountain already gave them one. In front of the rock, you can still see low stone foundations. These were the remains of gate buildings that once controlled who could enter. A worshipper passed through them and then stepped from daylight into this narrow, shaded world.

The larger space is usually called Chamber A. Walk slowly along its walls. On the left side, a procession of male gods marches in one direction. Most wear tall pointed hats and short kilts, and their shoes curl up at the toes. On the right side, a procession of goddesses walks the other way. They wear long pleated robes and tall rounded headdresses. The two lines move towards each other, step by step, across the rock.

Now follow both lines to the far end. There, the two processions meet in the central scene. The great storm god stands on the bowed shoulders of two mountain gods. Facing him is the sun goddess, his wife, standing on the back of a large cat, often read as a panther or a lioness. Behind her, a smaller male figure also rides an animal. He is understood to be their son. This meeting of the gods is the heart of the whole sanctuary.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The names of the gods follow Hurrian traditions, not only native Hittite ones. The Hurrians were a neighbouring people from northern Mesopotamia and Syria. Their religion deeply influenced the Hittite royal family in the empire's later centuries. So the storm god appears here as Teshub, and his wife as Hebat. Small signs carved beside many figures label them. These are Anatolian hieroglyphs, a picture-based script that scholars have learned to read.

Most of the reliefs are dated to the thirteenth century BCE. They are linked especially to King Tudhaliya the Fourth, one of the last great Hittite rulers. On one wall of Chamber A, a large figure of a king stands apart from the gods. He wears a long robe and a skullcap, and he holds a curved staff. Above him floats a winged sun symbol. The king placed himself among the gods, but slightly to one side — close, yet still a mortal.

Why did people come here? Honestly, nobody knows for certain. One popular scholarly idea suggests that Chamber A hosted a spring or New Year festival. In this view, the king led rituals for the whole pantheon. It is an attractive theory, but it remains an interpretation, not a written record. What is clear is the care put into the work. Carving such long, even processions into hard natural rock took planning, skill and many hands.

Now look for the narrow passage leading to the smaller space, Chamber B. It feels more closed and more secret. Many researchers think it was a memorial chapel for Tudhaliya the Fourth, perhaps built by his son. On one wall, twelve gods run in close step, all identical, carrying curved swords. They are often linked to the underworld. Their bodies overlap so tightly that they almost become a single moving figure. It is a strange and powerful image.

Opposite them is the most surprising carving at Yazılıkaya. It is a giant sword or dagger driven point-first into the ground. Look at the hilt. It is made of lions — two facing outwards, two hanging down. At the very top, a human head wears a tall god's hat. Scholars call this the Sword God. Nearby, another relief shows the king embraced by a protective god, who wraps one arm around his shoulders. Here, the gods do not only receive the king. They hold him.

For centuries, local shepherds and villagers knew these rocks. The wider world learned of them in the 1830s, when the French traveller Charles Texier described the ruins in this area. Later excavations, mostly by German archaeologists working at Hattusa, uncovered much more. Over time, the hieroglyph labels were deciphered. Slowly, the silent figures received their names again. Remember that weather is still the main enemy here. Rain and freezing winters continue to soften the edges of every carving.

Before you leave, stand once more where the two processions meet. Let your eyes travel back along the line of gods, then the line of goddesses. Notice how the light changes the stone. Bright sun flattens the figures, and a low angle brings them forward. Listen to the wind in the gap, and to the birds on the rock above. As you walk out through the old foundations, picture the worshippers who took this same path, stepping from the shade back into the open hills.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early or late when low light sharpens the reliefs.
Combine the visit with nearby Hattusa on the same day.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven rocky ground.
Bring a hat and water in summer heat.
Walk the processions slowly toward the central meeting scene.
