The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across the grass, and somewhere below a sheep calls. The stones under your feet are cold, grey, and rough, and they have been here for nearly two thousand years. Around you, the fells of the Lake District rise in every direction. You are standing on a narrow shelf of land high above Eskdale, on one of the most exposed places the Roman army ever chose to build.

This is Hardknott Roman Fort. The Romans knew it by a different name, Mediobogdum. Scholars still debate exactly what that name meant. What is clear is the purpose of the place. The fort guarded a Roman road that ran from the coast at Ravenglass, over the mountain passes, and on towards the fort at Ambleside. It was a link in a chain of control across difficult country.

Start by turning slowly in a full circle. To the west, the valley of Eskdale falls away towards the sea. To the east and north, the high fells close in, including the Scafell range, the highest ground in England. The Romans picked this spur for its views. From these walls, a sentry could watch the road climb from the valley and see movement long before it arrived. On a clear day, the logic of the site is easy to understand.

The fort was built in the early second century, during the reign of the emperor Hadrian. This is the same emperor whose famous wall crosses northern England not far from here. We know about the date partly because of an inscription found at the site. It names Hadrian, and it names the unit that built and manned the fort, the Fourth Cohort of Dalmatians.

That detail surprises many visitors. Dalmatia was a Roman province on the eastern shore of the Adriatic Sea, roughly where the coast of Croatia lies today. So the soldiers who stood guard here came from a land of warm limestone hills and olive trees. Imagine their first winter on this mountainside — the rain, the low cloud, the dark coming early in the afternoon. The Roman army moved people across its whole empire, and this windswept corner of Britain was one of the results.

Now look at the shape of the fort itself. It is roughly square, with rounded corners, the classic playing-card plan used by Roman forts across Europe. There was a gate in the middle of each side. The walls you see are low today, but they once stood much higher, with a walkway along the top and towers at the corners and gates. Walk the line of the walls and you can still feel how compact and orderly this place was.

Here is a detail that most people walk straight past. Look closely at the walls and you will find a thin line of slate set into the stonework. Everything below that line is original Roman masonry. Everything above it was rebuilt in modern times, when the ruins were consolidated for visitors. It is an honest piece of conservation. It lets you see exactly where the ancient work stops and the new work begins.

Inside the walls, the foundations of the main buildings are laid out in stone. At the centre stood the headquarters building, the administrative and religious heart of the fort. Here the unit kept its standards, the sacred emblems of the cohort, and here the commanding officer conducted business. Nearby was the commandant's house, a private residence for the officer and his household. And close by were the granaries, built with raised floors to keep stored grain dry and away from damp and rats.

Notice what you do not see. The ordinary soldiers lived in barrack blocks, and these were probably built of timber rather than stone. Wood rots, so those buildings have left far less trace. The stone remains give a picture of the fort's official core — but the daily life of most of its men happened in structures that have largely gone. Much of the grass around you once held their quarters, their cooking fires, and their noise.

Now walk outside the walls, down towards the slope by the road. Here you will find the remains of the bath house. For Roman soldiers, bathing was not a luxury. It was a central part of daily routine and social life. The building had a sequence of rooms, from cold to warm to hot, heated by fires beneath the floors. Beside it stands a small circular room that most experts interpret as a dry hot room, much like a modern sauna. On a raw Cumbrian day, it must have been the most welcome room in the whole valley.

Then look uphill, away from the fort, and find a broad, flattened area of ground. This is the parade ground. The soldiers cut and levelled the hillside to create it, which was no small task on a slope like this. Here the cohort drilled, trained, and gathered for ceremonies. Standing on it now, you can picture lines of men in formation, with the mountains as their only audience. It is one of the best surviving examples of its kind in Britain.

The fort was not occupied for very long. Archaeologists think the garrison was withdrawn some decades after it was built, probably when Roman frontier policy shifted further north into Scotland. There may have been later use of the site, but its busiest years were short. That brief life is part of why it feels so clear today. Few later buildings were added on top, so the Roman plan has survived with unusual simplicity.

Think, too, about how you arrived. The road over Hardknott Pass is famous as one of the steepest in England, with tight bends and sharp gradients. Many visitors arrive slightly shaken from the drive. The modern road roughly follows the route the Romans used, though their road took its own line across the slopes. Two thousand years apart, travellers have faced the same problem — how to cross these mountains safely.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment on the walls. Feel the wind and watch the light move across the fells as clouds pass. Look down the valley towards the sea, the same view the Dalmatian soldiers watched every day. As you walk back to the road, look once more at the slate line in the stones, the place where the ancient world meets our own. Then take that quiet with you down into Eskdale.

Before you go

Practical tips

Drive Hardknott Pass slowly; expect steep gradients and tight bends.
Bring waterproofs and warm layers, even in summer.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, often wet grass.
Visit on a clear day for full views to the sea.
Combine with the Ravenglass and Eskdale Railway nearby.
