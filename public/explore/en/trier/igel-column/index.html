The guide

The story

Rough red sandstone rises above you, warm to the eye even on a grey day. Cars pass on the village road behind you. Somewhere a church bell may be ringing over the rooftops. And here, between ordinary houses in a small village on the Moselle, stands one of the most complete Roman monuments north of the Alps. This is the Igel Column. It is not really a column at all. It is a tomb marker, a family billboard in stone, and it has stood on this spot for around seventeen centuries.

The village of Igel lies a short drive upriver from Trier. In Roman times, Trier was Augusta Treverorum, one of the great cities of the western empire. For a while it even served as an imperial residence. The roads and rivers around it carried goods in every direction. This monument belongs to that world of trade. It was raised in the third century, most likely around the middle of that century. It honours a family of wealthy local merchants called the Secundinii.

Take a moment to judge its height. The column rises more than twenty metres, roughly the height of a six or seven storey building. It is built from blocks of local red sandstone, stacked and carved in place. The shape is tall and slim, like a tower with a stepped base, a main shaft, a decorated upper zone, and a pointed roof. At the very top, the Romans placed a sculpture. It has weathered badly, but it is usually read as an eagle carrying a young man up into the sky.

That young man is Ganymede. In Greek and Roman myth, the god Jupiter took the form of an eagle and carried this beautiful youth up to the heavens. For a Roman family, the image had a clear meaning. It spoke of a soul rising from earth to the world of the gods. So the very peak of this monument carries a hope about life after death. Most visitors walk around the base and never look up long enough to find it.

Now look at the faces of the column. Every surface was once covered in carved scenes. Some show figures from myth, and others show heroes and gods. But the most unusual panels show daily life. You can find people at work, goods being packed and moved, and what appears to be a family sharing a meal. These scenes are the real treasure of the monument. Very few Roman monuments show ordinary business in such detail.

The Secundinii made their money in cloth. The Treveri region was known for woollen textiles, and merchants here sold fabric across the provinces. On the column, scholars have identified scenes of cloth being inspected, bundles being carried, and loads travelling by road and by river. Think of it as a portrait of the family business, carved for all to see. Every traveller on the road past this spot would have understood the message. Here lies a successful family, and this is how they earned their place.

There is also an inscription. It names members of the family, among them two brothers who share the name Secundinius. It records that the monument was set up for themselves and for their relatives, both living and dead. The lettering has suffered from wind, rain and frost, so it is hard to read today. But its purpose is simple. Like a modern family gravestone, it gathers several generations under one name and keeps that name in public view.

Here is the first real surprise. Almost every Roman tomb monument of this size in the region vanished long ago. People broke them apart and reused the cut stone for churches, walls and houses. So why did this one survive? The usual explanation is a fortunate misunderstanding. In the Middle Ages, local people came to believe that one of the carved scenes showed the wedding of Saint Helena and her husband Constantius, the parents of the emperor Constantine.

Helena holds a special place in Trier's story. Tradition links her closely to the city, and she was venerated as a saint. A monument thought to honour her marriage was not something you would knock down for building stone. So a pagan tomb, decorated with Roman gods and myths, was protected because people thought it was Christian. That error kept it standing through centuries when far more important buildings were taken apart.

The second surprise involves a famous visitor. In 1792, the German writer Johann Wolfgang von Goethe passed through this region during a military campaign. He stopped here and studied the column, and he later wrote about it with real admiration. For Goethe, it was a living link to the ancient world, standing in the open air rather than in a museum. His attention helped make the monument known to educated travellers across Europe.

Today, the column is part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site. It is listed together with the Roman monuments of Trier, the cathedral and the Church of Our Lady. That listing places this village tomb alongside the great Roman gate, the baths and the imperial basilica in the city. It is a reminder that Roman Trier did not end at its walls. Villas, farms, workshops and family tombs filled the countryside along the river.

As you walk around, notice how the weather has shaped the stone. Sandstone is fairly soft, so the sharpest edges have rounded away. Some faces have worn more than others, depending on how they meet the wind and rain. In flat midday light, many carvings almost disappear. When the sun is low, shadows return, and figures you could not see a moment ago come back into view. It is worth circling slowly and stopping on each side.

Before you leave, step back toward the road and look at the column against the village around it. Notice the roofs, the gardens and the ordinary traffic of a modern weekday. This family wanted to be remembered by everyone who passed by, and they still are. The road has changed, and the travellers now arrive by car and bicycle. But people still stop here, look up at the red stone, and ask who these merchants were.
