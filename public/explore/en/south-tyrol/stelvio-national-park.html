The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere above you, a sharp, high whistle cuts across the slope... and then silence. That sound is a marmot, warning its family that something has moved below. The air here is thin and cool, even in summer. It smells of resin from the larch trees, wet stone, and the faint sweetness of alpine grass. Under your feet, the ground is a mix of pine needles, gravel, and rock that has been broken apart by centuries of frost.

Welcome to Stelvio National Park. It is one of the oldest national parks in Italy, created in 1935. It is also one of the largest protected areas in the Alps, covering well over a thousand square kilometres. That area crosses three regions. Part of the park lies in Lombardy, part in Trentino, and part here in South Tyrol. So the same mountains carry Italian names on one side and German names on the other. For example, the Stelvio Pass is also called the Stilfser Joch.

The centre of this landscape is the Ortler massif. The Ortler, called Ortles in Italian, is the highest mountain in South Tyrol, at 3,905 metres. On a clear day you can see its summit wrapped in ice above the village of Sulden. Its north face is steep and pale. For a long time, local people treated the peak as unreachable. The first recorded ascent came at the very end of the eighteenth century, when a local hunter guided a small party to the top.

This is a park of valleys as much as peaks. On the South Tyrolean side, several long valleys lead up into the high country. The Martell Valley runs deep into the mountains, with strawberry fields on its lower slopes and glaciers at its head. The Sulden Valley climbs toward the Ortler. And near Trafoi, a small cluster of houses and a church sit below the road to the pass. Each valley has its own pace and its own character.

Now, the road. If you have driven or cycled up from the Vinschgau side, you already know it. The Stelvio Pass road rises to about 2,757 metres. It is the second-highest paved mountain pass in the Alps. On this side, the road climbs through 48 hairpin bends, one after another, up the face of the mountain. Many of the turns carry small numbered stones, so drivers and cyclists can count down as they climb.

Here is something many visitors miss. This road was not built for tourists, and it was not built by Italy. It was built in the 1820s by the Austrian Empire. At that time, Austria controlled both Lombardy and the Tyrol. Vienna wanted a direct route over the mountains to connect them. The engineer Carlo Donegani designed the road, and workers built it across steep slopes, avalanche paths, and loose scree. For its time, it was a remarkable piece of engineering.

A century later, these same mountains became a front line. During the First World War, the border between Austria-Hungary and Italy ran across this high country. Soldiers fought here at altitudes above three thousand metres. They dug positions into rock and ice, hauled guns up glaciers, and lived through winters of extreme cold. Many died from avalanches and exposure, not only from combat. As the glaciers have melted in recent decades, remains of shelters, equipment, and sometimes soldiers have come back to the surface.

That melting ice tells another story. The glaciers of the Ortler group are shrinking, and you can often see the difference with your own eyes. Look for a band of bare grey rock between the ice and the green slopes below. That pale, empty ground was covered by ice not very long ago. On some trails, small signs mark where a glacier edge stood in past decades. The distance between the sign and the ice today can be surprising.

The animals here are easier to see than in many parts of the Alps. Red deer are common, especially in the evening at the edges of forests and meadows. Chamois move across the steep slopes with ease. Higher up, you may spot an ibex, with its heavy curved horns, resting calmly on a ledge. And keep looking at the sky. The bearded vulture, a huge bird that disappeared from the Alps, has been brought back to this region and now breeds again in the park.

The plants change as you climb. In the lower valleys there are forests of spruce and larch. Larch is unusual — it is a conifer that loses its needles every year. In autumn, entire slopes turn gold and orange before the needles fall. Above the trees, the ground opens into meadows with gentians, alpine roses, and edelweiss in summer. Higher still, there is only lichen on rock, in patches of grey, orange, and green.

People have lived and worked in these valleys for many centuries. You can still see the traces. Look for old stone walls dividing meadows, wooden hay barns with dark, sun-burned planks, and narrow irrigation channels cut into the hillsides. These channels carried glacier water to dry fields for generations. Many farms still bring cattle up to high pastures in summer, and you may hear cowbells long before you see the herd.

The park is a place for slow travel. There are short, gentle paths around the villages and long, demanding routes to mountain huts and glaciers. Weather can change very quickly at this altitude. A bright morning can turn into cloud, wind, and cold rain by early afternoon. The local mountain huts are part of the experience, too. Many of them serve simple, warming food after a long climb, and their terraces look straight out at the ice.

There is also a special kind of light here. In the early morning, the sun touches the highest snow first, while the valleys are still in shadow. For a few minutes the summit glows pink and gold above dark forests. In the evening the process reverses. The shadows climb the slopes, and the last light stays on the ice. If you can, stay outside for that moment. The air goes quiet, and the temperature drops fast.

So before you move on, take one more look around you. Find the line where the trees stop and the rock begins. Find the highest patch of white you can see. Listen for water — there is almost always a stream somewhere nearby, carrying meltwater down toward the valley. This landscape has seen empires, armies, and generations of farmers. Today it is simply here, cold and quiet, and you are standing in the middle of it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start high walks early, before afternoon clouds and storms arrive.
Pack a warm layer even on hot summer days.
Bring binoculars for spotting ibex and chamois on ledges.
Watch for deer at forest edges around dusk.
Drive the hairpins slowly and give cyclists plenty of room.
