The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. In the Negev highlands around Yeruham, the quiet has weight. A dry wind moves across the ground, and small stones shift under your shoes with a sound like breaking pottery. The light here is hard and white at midday. By late afternoon it turns the colour of honey. Before we talk about names and stories, simply notice where you are standing: on the high desert of southern Israel, far from the sea.

The name Kadesh is worth a moment of attention. In Hebrew, the root K-D-SH carries the meaning of holiness, of something set apart. It is the same root found in words for sanctity and blessing. So the name itself suggests a place marked as special. Whoever chose it was reaching back into a very old vocabulary. In this landscape, that vocabulary has deep roots.

Many listeners will think of Kadesh Barnea, a place named in the Hebrew Bible during the story of the Israelites in the wilderness. Most scholars place that site further south and west, near the border with Sinai, not here in Yeruham. So it is best to hold that connection loosely. The name here echoes that biblical memory. Detailed records about this particular spot are thin, and rather than fill the gaps with guesses, we will look closely at what is actually in front of you.

Begin with the ground. The Negev highlands are made of layered sedimentary rock — limestone, chalk, and dark flint. Look down and you may see pale stones mixed with sharp brown or black pieces. That darker stone is flint. For thousands of years, people in this region shaped flint into tools. Today it simply lies scattered across the slopes, catching the sun with a dull shine.

Now lift your eyes to the horizon. The hills around Yeruham are low and rounded, worn down by wind and rare, violent rains. There are few trees on the open slopes. When rain does fall here, it often comes all at once. Dry streambeds, called wadis, fill with fast brown water for a few hours and then empty again. Look for shallow channels in the land. Those are the paths that water takes.

Yeruham itself is a young town in an old landscape. It was founded in the early 1950s, in the first years of the State of Israel. Its first residents were mostly new immigrants, many from North Africa, and later from other parts of the world. Life here was difficult. The town was far from the large cities, the summers were fierce, and work was hard to find. Over the decades, the community built schools, synagogues, and gardens out of very little.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Close to the town sits Yeruham Lake, a reservoir surrounded by a park of eucalyptus and other trees. In the middle of the desert, families come to picnic in the shade, and birds gather near the water. Migrating birds pass through this region in spring and autumn. If you have time after this stop, the contrast between the dry hills and that green patch is worth seeing for yourself.

A second surprise lies just beyond the town. Yeruham sits near HaMakhtesh HaGadol, the Big Crater. It is not a crater made by a meteor or a volcano. It is an erosion cirque, a large basin formed when softer rock was slowly carried away from beneath a harder outer shell. The Negev holds several of these formations, and they are rare elsewhere in the world. Inside, the rock appears in bands of red, yellow, purple, and white sand.

As you stand at Kadesh, think about how people have always moved through this land. Nomadic herders, traders with camels, and travellers between the coast and the south all crossed these highlands. Water decided where they stopped. An ancient well near Yeruham is linked by local tradition to biblical figures, though such stories are told as legend, not proven history. Still, the pattern is real: in the desert, every spring and every well became a meeting point.

Bedouin communities still live in the wider Negev today. You may see their homes, herds of goats or sheep, or a pickup truck kicking up dust on a distant track. Their presence is part of the living texture of this region. Be respectful when taking photographs of people or homes, and always ask first. The desert here is not empty land. It is shared by many communities with long memories.

Take a moment to feel the temperature. In this part of the Negev, days can be very hot, while nights often turn surprisingly cold, even outside winter. The air is dry, so sweat disappears quickly and you may not notice how much water you lose. The stone around you holds the heat of the afternoon and releases it slowly after sunset. Touch a rock in the shade, and then one in direct sun, and feel the difference.

Notice the plants, too. Desert shrubs grow low and spaced apart, each one claiming its own patch of moisture. Many have small grey-green leaves that reduce water loss. After winter rain, the hills can briefly turn green and even flower, and then fade back to brown within weeks. If you come in late winter, you may see a soft green layer here that most summer visitors never imagine is possible.

The sounds of this place are few, and that is part of its character. You may hear a raven call, a distant engine, the wind against your ears. In the evening, the sky above the Negev grows very dark, with little light pollution, and stars appear in large numbers. Many people come to the desert for exactly this: a chance to hear their own footsteps and to see a sky they had forgotten.

Before you leave, look once more at the land around Kadesh. Notice the pale chalk, the dark flint, the low line of the hills, and the way the light falls across them right now. A name that means holy, a young town built by newcomers, a desert shaped by rare water — all of it meets where you stand. As you walk back, listen to the stones under your feet, and carry a little of this quiet with you.

Practical tips
