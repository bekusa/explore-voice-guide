The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement for a moment and let your eyes climb. Two towers rise in front of you, capped with small copper domes. Below them, bands of pale stone alternate with warm red tones. The street noise of Plzeň runs past behind you: trams, footsteps, a bicycle bell. But the building itself is quiet. It has the stillness of a place that has seen far more than its walls can show.

This is the Great Synagogue of Plzeň. It is one of the largest synagogues in Europe. It is usually counted second in size only to the Dohány Street Synagogue in Budapest. That surprises many visitors. Plzeň is known worldwide for its beer, not for its Jewish heritage. Yet in the late nineteenth century, this city held a confident and growing Jewish community. This building was their statement of belonging.

The synagogue was built in the late 1880s and early 1890s. Bohemia was then part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. Under imperial reforms in the decades before, Jewish citizens had gained new civil rights. Many Jewish families in Plzeň moved into trade, industry and the professions. The community needed more room than its older prayer house could offer. So they planned something on a grand scale, close to the green ring of parks that circles the old town.

Here is the first surprise. The building you see is not the first design. An earlier plan, by the Viennese architect Max Fleischer, imagined a Gothic-style synagogue with very tall towers. According to the usual account, the city authorities rejected it. One reason often given is that the towers would have competed with the spire of the Cathedral of Saint Bartholomew in the main square. The design was reworked by the builder Emanuel Klotz. The towers became shorter, and the style shifted.

That new style is often called Moorish Revival. It borrows shapes and patterns from Islamic Spain, from places like the Alhambra in Granada. In the nineteenth century, many Central European Jewish communities chose this look. It marked their buildings as different from churches. It also pointed to a romantic idea of Jewish history in medieval Spain. Here, those Moorish touches sit on a solid, rounded, almost Romanesque body. Romanesque means the heavy, round-arched style of early medieval Europe.

Look at the front again, slowly. Notice the horizontal stripes of colour running across the façade. Notice the arched windows, grouped in rhythms of two and three. Above the main entrance sits a large round window. Small decorative details repeat across the stonework, like a pattern on fabric. The towers frame the whole composition. From the right distance, the building feels both heavy and light at the same time.

Inside, the scale becomes clear. The main hall is tall and open, with galleries running along the sides. In traditional synagogues, these upper galleries were for women, while men prayed below. The ceiling and walls carry painted ornament in deep blues, reds and golds. Light falls in through the coloured glass and moves across the floor through the day. On a quiet afternoon, even a whisper seems to travel far.

Now the harder part of the story. In 1939, German forces occupied Bohemia and Moravia. The Jewish community of Plzeň was persecuted, stripped of property, and then deported. Most were sent first to the ghetto at Terezín, north of Prague, in 1942. From there, many were taken to extermination camps further east. Only a small number survived the war. The community that built this hall was almost completely destroyed.

And here is the second surprise. Many synagogues across German-controlled Europe were burned or blown up. This one was not. During the war it was used as a storehouse. Locals often say it survived because it stood so close to other buildings that a fire would have spread through the neighbourhood. That explanation is widely repeated, but it is hard to confirm. What is certain is that the building stood, damaged and empty, when the war ended.

After 1945, very few Jewish residents returned to Plzeň. Then came decades of Communist rule. The building was too large for the tiny surviving community, and it slowly fell into decay. Damp got into the walls. Paint faded and flaked. For a long time, the future of the synagogue was uncertain. Some people feared it would simply be lost.

Change came after the Velvet Revolution of 1989, when Communist rule in Czechoslovakia ended peacefully. A long restoration began in the 1990s. Craftspeople repaired the stonework, the roof and the painted interior. The synagogue reopened at the end of the decade. Today it serves as a place of memory and culture. It hosts exhibitions and concerts, and the acoustics of the great hall suit music very well.

As you walk around the building, notice how it sits in the city. It faces the line of parks that replaced the old town walls. Trams pass close by. Office workers cut across the paths with coffee in hand. The synagogue is not hidden away in a separate quarter. It stands openly on a main route, just as its builders intended. That openness was part of its original message.

Before you leave, take one more look at the two towers. Think of the families who walked through these doors on a Friday evening, more than a century ago. Then turn and listen to the ordinary sounds of Plzeň around you. The city carried on, and so did this building. As you walk away, glance back once at the round window above the door. It catches the light differently from every angle.
