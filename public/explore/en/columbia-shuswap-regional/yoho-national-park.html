The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the hum of the highway there is a deeper sound, a steady roar of meltwater moving fast over stone. The air is cool, even in summer, and it smells of spruce resin and wet rock. Look up, and the mountains do not slope gently away. They rise in sheer grey walls, banded like layers of a cake, with snow still caught in their folds. This is Yoho National Park, on the western side of the Canadian Rockies, in British Columbia.

The name itself is a reaction to this landscape. Yoho is usually traced to a Cree expression of awe and wonder, something close to a gasp of amazement. It is a fitting word. Within a fairly small area, the park holds towering waterfalls, lakes of an unreal green, natural rock arches, and some of the most important fossils on Earth. It is compact, steep, and dramatic. Many visitors arrive expecting a quick stop between larger parks. They often stay much longer than they planned.

Yoho is old by the standards of Canadian conservation. It began as a small mountain reserve in 1886, which makes it one of the very first protected areas in the country. Its birth was tied directly to the railway. The Canadian Pacific line had just pushed through these mountains, and the company saw the scenery as a reason for people to travel. Hotels and lodges followed. Tourism and the train grew up together here, and you can still feel that partnership in the landscape today.

That railway history begins with a horse. In 1858, a geologist named James Hector was exploring this region with the Palliser Expedition, a British survey of western Canada. According to the well-known account, one of his pack horses kicked him hard in the chest, and he was knocked unconscious. His companions reportedly feared he was dead. Hector recovered, and the river and the pass nearby took a name from the incident. Today they are called the Kicking Horse River and Kicking Horse Pass.

The pass caused serious trouble for the railway builders. The original track descended from the Continental Divide on a slope so steep that it became known as the Big Hill. Trains struggled to climb it, and runaway trains were a real danger on the way down. The solution, completed in 1909, was the Spiral Tunnels. These are two looping tunnels cut into the mountains, which let the track turn back on itself and lose height more gradually. It is engineering you can watch in action.

Here is the surprise. From the viewpoint along the highway, you can sometimes see a long freight train entering the mountain at one level and emerging below, while its last cars are still above. For a few moments, the train appears to pass over itself. Kids and adults alike tend to stand very still, counting cars. Trains run on no fixed public timetable, so it is a matter of luck. But when it happens, it feels like watching a puzzle solve itself.

Now to the water. Much of Yoho's colour comes from glaciers. As ice grinds slowly over bedrock, it crushes the stone into a powder so fine it stays suspended in the water. This is often called glacial flour. When sunlight hits those tiny particles, the lakes and rivers glow turquoise, jade, or milky blue. The shade changes with the season and even with the hour. A cloud passing overhead can shift Emerald Lake from bright green to deep slate in seconds.

Emerald Lake is the largest lake in the park, and its name needs no explanation once you see it. It is ringed by forest and framed by steep peaks, with a wooden lodge near the water. A level trail circles the shore, and in summer canoes move slowly across the surface. Early in the morning, before the wind rises, the lake often lies almost perfectly still. Then the mountains appear twice — once in the sky, and once upside down in the water.

Further up the Yoho Valley is Takakkaw Falls, one of the tallest waterfalls in Canada. The name comes from a Cree word often translated as magnificent. The water pours from a glacier high above, drops over a cliff edge, and crashes into the valley in a white column of spray. You will feel it before you reach the base. The mist drifts across the path, cool on your face, and the sound fills the whole valley. On warm afternoons, the flow grows heavier as more ice melts.

The second great surprise in Yoho is hidden in the rock itself. In 1909, a scientist named Charles Walcott found extraordinary fossils high on a ridge above the town of Field. This site, known as the Burgess Shale, preserves sea creatures from the Cambrian period, more than 500 million years ago. That was a time when complex animal life was first spreading through the oceans. What makes these fossils rare is that soft bodies were preserved, not only shells and bones.

Some of those animals look almost invented. There are creatures with five eyes, animals with spines along their backs, and early predators with grasping front limbs. They lived in a shallow sea, and today their remains lie high in the mountains. That alone tells you how much this land has moved and lifted over time. The fossil beds are protected, and the only way to reach them is on a guided hike. The climb is long and steep, but many people call it unforgettable.

The small town of Field sits in the middle of the park, beside the railway and the river. It is a quiet place, with simple wooden houses and the mountains rising sharply behind them. Near Field, the Kicking Horse River has carved a natural bridge through solid rock, where the water forces its way through a narrow gap. Not far away, Lake O'Hara is one of the most protected corners of the park. Access is limited to keep it from being overused.

Yoho also sits within a much larger protected landscape. Together with Banff, Jasper, Kootenay, and several provincial parks, it forms part of the Canadian Rocky Mountain Parks World Heritage Site, recognised by UNESCO. The park's eastern edge follows the Continental Divide. On one side, rivers eventually flow toward the Pacific. On the other, they run east toward Hudson Bay. A single raindrop on that ridge can end up in two different oceans.

So before you move on, pause and look around you once more. Notice the bands in the cliffs, each one a layer of ancient sea floor. Listen for the river, and perhaps for the low horn of a distant train. Feel the cold air coming down off the ice. Yoho is a place where deep time and human effort sit side by side. As you walk away, take that sense of wonder with you — the same feeling that gave this valley its name.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a guided hike to reach the Burgess Shale fossil beds.
Reserve the Lake O'Hara shuttle well ahead of your visit.
Visit Emerald Lake early for calm water and reflections.
Bring a waterproof layer for the spray near Takakkaw Falls.
Wait at the Spiral Tunnels viewpoint for a passing freight train.
