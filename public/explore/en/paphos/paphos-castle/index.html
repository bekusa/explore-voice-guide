The guide

The story

Listen first. Halyards tap against aluminium masts in the harbour. Gulls argue over the fishing boats. Small waves slap against the stone of the pier under your feet. Then look ahead, to the end of the quay. A squat, square fort of pale honey-coloured stone sits right at the water's edge. It is not tall, and it is not ornate. It looks solid, patient and a little stubborn. That suits a building that has been knocked down, rebuilt and put to new uses more than once.

This is Paphos Castle, sometimes called the Harbour Fort. It guards the western end of the old port of Kato Paphos, the lower town by the sea. For most of its life, its job was simple. It watched the harbour mouth. Ships came here from the Levant, from Egypt and from the Aegean. A fort at this point could see trouble before it arrived. It could also make an attacking ship think twice before entering the harbour.

The story of this spot begins long before the building you see now. In the Byzantine period, a fort stood here to protect the harbour. Byzantine means the eastern, Greek-speaking half of the Roman Empire, which ruled Cyprus for centuries. Up the hill behind you, there was also a larger Byzantine castle. Locals call its ruins Saranta Kolones, or Forty Columns. An earthquake in the early thirteenth century wrecked that castle, and the harbour fort took on more importance.

The next rulers were the Lusignans. They were a French noble family who held Cyprus as a crusader kingdom from the late twelfth century. They rebuilt and strengthened the harbour defences in the thirteenth century. Medieval Paphos was smaller and quieter than its ancient self, but the port still mattered. Grain, wine and pilgrims all passed through here. A pilgrim on the way to the Holy Land might have looked at these same waters from the deck of a ship.

Here is the first surprise. The Venetians, who controlled Cyprus after the Lusignans, destroyed this fort themselves. In 1570, an Ottoman army landed on the island. The Venetians knew they could not hold every fortress. They chose to dismantle the Paphos fort rather than let the enemy capture it ready to use. It is a strange kind of defence... to pull down your own walls so that nobody else can stand behind them.

The Ottomans took Cyprus in 1571. Soon after, they rebuilt the fort on the remains of the older structure. Much of what you see today belongs to this Ottoman phase, from the late sixteenth century. As you approach the entrance, look up. Above the doorway there is a stone plaque with an inscription in Ottoman Turkish. It records the work of the Ottoman builders. It is a small detail, and many visitors walk straight under it without noticing.

Now step inside. The air changes at once. Thick stone walls keep the heat out, and the light drops to a soft grey. The ground floor has a central hall with small rooms around it. In Ottoman times, these rooms served partly as prison cells. Imagine the sound of the sea coming through the stone, close but out of reach. For a prisoner, the harbour was only a few steps away, and it might as well have been another country.

The second surprise comes from the British period. Britain ran Cyprus from 1878 until independence in 1960. For part of that time, the British used this old fort as a store for salt. Salt was a valuable product on the island, and a thick-walled, dry building by the harbour was a practical place to keep it. So a castle built for cannons and prisoners spent some of its life holding sacks of salt.

In 1935, the colonial authorities declared the fort an ancient monument. That decision protected it from further changes. Today, the stone shows its long history plainly. You can see patches of different masonry where one period met another. Some blocks are larger and roughly cut. Others are smaller and more regular. The building is a record of repair, written in stone rather than on paper.

Climb the stairs to the roof terrace. This is the moment most people remember. The flat roof gives you a wide view in every direction. To one side lies the open Mediterranean, deep blue on a calm day. To the other side, you see the harbour, the promenade, the cafés and the low town rising toward the hills. Further inland is the archaeological park, with its famous Roman mosaics, which UNESCO lists as part of the World Heritage Site of Paphos.

From up here, notice how small the fort really is. It was never a grand palace. It was a working building, a lookout and a gun platform. That is part of its charm. On the roof, you stand in the same position as the watchmen who scanned the horizon for sails. Turn slowly and follow the line of the coast. You can understand why this exact point was chosen, again and again, by different rulers.

The fort is not only a museum piece. In late summer, the square in front of it often becomes an open-air stage. The Paphos Aphrodite Festival has used the castle walls as a backdrop for opera performances. Picture floodlit stone, an orchestra, and the sea moving quietly behind the singers. In 2017, Paphos was a European Capital of Culture, and the harbour area was one of the centres of that year's events.

When you come back down, walk slowly along the pier. Look back at the fort from a little distance. In the late afternoon, the stone turns warm gold, and the shadows sharpen every edge. Fishermen still work in this harbour. Children chase pigeons on the promenade. People sit with coffee and watch the boats. The fort no longer guards anyone, but it still gives the harbour its shape.

Before you leave, put your hand on the wall for a moment. Feel how cool the stone is, even on a hot day. Byzantine soldiers, French knights, Venetian engineers, Ottoman builders and British clerks all knew this place. Now it is your turn. Listen again to the masts, the gulls and the small waves on the pier. The harbour sounds much as it always has.
