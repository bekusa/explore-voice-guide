The guide

The story

Listen first. Traffic hums on the streets below the hill. A horn sounds, then another. Voices carry from somewhere out of sight. Then your eyes settle on the cathedral, or what the earth has left of it. The light here is strong and white for most of the day. It falls straight through spaces where a roof once was, onto pale stone and broken plaster. You are standing in front of one of the most important buildings in Haiti's story.

This is the Cathedral of Our Lady of the Assumption, the seat of the Catholic Archdiocese of Port-au-Prince. Locals often call it simply the cathedral, or by its French name, Notre-Dame de l'Assomption. It stands on higher ground in the old downtown, above the harbour. For generations, its towers were a landmark you could see from much of the lower city. Ships coming into the bay could pick them out against the hills.

Construction began in the 1880s, and the work took about three decades. The cathedral was completed in the years just before the First World War. Its design came from France. The style is Romanesque Revival — a nineteenth-century return to the medieval churches of Europe, with their rounded arches, heavy walls and calm, solid shapes. Here in the Caribbean, that European form took on a local accent.

That accent was colour. Photographs from before 2010 show a façade painted pink and white, bright against the blue sky. Two towers framed the front. Between them, a large round rose window sat above the main entrance. Inside, the nave rose high and open, built to hold a great crowd. It was a European plan, dressed in tropical colours — and that mix made it feel entirely of this city.

For about a century, this was where the capital gathered at its biggest moments. Masses for national occasions were held here. Weddings, funerals and feast days filled the nave. The cathedral is dedicated to the Assumption of the Virgin Mary — the Catholic belief that Mary was taken into heaven at the end of her life. Her feast falls in mid-August, and it has long been an important day in the Haitian calendar.

Then came the afternoon of 12 January 2010. A powerful earthquake struck close to Port-au-Prince. In less than a minute, much of the city came down. The cathedral's roof collapsed. Large parts of the walls and towers fell into the nave. The building that had marked the skyline for a hundred years became a shell almost at once.

The loss was human as well as architectural. The Archbishop of Port-au-Prince, Joseph Serge Miot, was killed in the earthquake when the archdiocese offices collapsed nearby. Many other priests, staff and worshippers in the area also died. Across the city and the surrounding region, the death toll was enormous. When you look at these walls, you are looking at one small part of a national tragedy.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. In the weeks after the disaster, people did not abandon this place. They came back to pray among the ruins. Services were held outdoors, in the open air beside the broken walls. Tents and simple shelters appeared nearby. The building had fallen, but the cathedral as a community kept meeting. In a way, it never fully closed.

Now look closely at what still stands. Notice how thick the remaining walls are. You can often see the layers inside them — stone, brick and mortar exposed where the plaster has fallen away. Rounded arch openings still frame empty sky. In places, traces of the old pink paint survive on the surfaces. These fragments show you the scale of the original building better than any photograph.

A second surprise is close by. Port-au-Prince once had another cathedral near this one, an older and smaller church that came before it. That earlier building was lost to fire years before the earthquake. So within a short walk, the city has lost two cathedrals to two different disasters. Few cities carry that kind of history in one neighbourhood.

After 2010, the question became what to build next. An international design competition was held for a new cathedral. The idea was to create a modern building while keeping a connection to the memory of the old one, including the ruins. Progress has been slow. Funding, politics and years of instability in the capital have all held the work back. What you see today is a site still waiting for its next chapter.

It is worth being honest about the present moment. In recent years, parts of downtown Port-au-Prince have faced serious security problems. Access to this area can change quickly. If you are here, you are probably with someone who knows these streets well. Stay close to them, follow their judgement, and keep your visit calm and respectful. This remains a place of prayer and of mourning for many people.

Before you go, look up once more. See the sky through the gaps where the roof once stood. Notice the hills behind the city, and the line of the bay below. Listen again to the traffic, the voices, the ordinary sounds of a capital that keeps moving. This cathedral took decades to build and seconds to fall. Yet people still come here. As you walk away, you might keep that thought with you — a building can break, while what it means to a city can stay standing.
