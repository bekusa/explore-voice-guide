The guide

The story

Put your palm flat on the stone railing. It is cool, slightly rough, and worn smooth at the edges by many hands. Under your feet, the deck rises in a long, gentle curve. It is not a steep hump, more like a slow breath held. Below you, a single wide arch carries the whole weight of the crossing, as it has done for about fourteen hundred years.

This is the Anji Bridge, which locals usually call the Zhaozhou Bridge. It stands in Zhao County, southeast of Shijiazhuang, on the plains of Hebei Province. It crosses the Xiao River. The river is often low or dry today, so the arch can look as if it spans nothing at all. When the bridge was built, this water mattered. Floods came down across these flat plains, and any crossing here had to survive them.

The bridge dates from the Sui dynasty. That was a short-lived ruling house that reunified China at the end of the sixth century, after centuries of division. Most historians place construction around the years 595 to 605. That makes this the oldest standing open-spandrel stone arch bridge in the world. You may hear it called the oldest stone bridge in China. That is a simpler claim, but the precise engineering title is the remarkable one.

The builder is traditionally named as Li Chun, a craftsman of the Sui period. We know his name because a later inscription credits him. We know almost nothing else about him. There is no portrait, no detailed biography, no record of his training. What survives is this bridge. In a sense, it is his only document. Every block you see is part of a design he worked out without modern mathematics or steel.

Now look at the shape of the main arch. It is not a half circle. Most arch bridges of that era, in China and in Europe, used a full semicircle. A semicircle is strong, but it is tall. A semicircular arch this wide would have required a steep climb up and down. Li Chun used a flatter curve, called a segmental arch. It is only a slice of a much larger circle. The result is a low, easy slope for carts, horses, and people on foot.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. Look at the shoulders of the bridge, the triangular spaces above the main arch at each end. Instead of solid stone, there are four smaller arches, two on each side. Engineers call these open spandrels. They do two jobs. First, they remove a large amount of heavy stone, so the main arch carries less weight. Second, during a flood, water can rush through these openings instead of pushing against a solid wall.

That idea did not appear in European bridges for many centuries. Open spandrel arches became common in the West much later, especially with the iron and concrete bridges of the industrial age. Standing here, you are looking at a solution that a Sui craftsman reached first. In 1991, the American Society of Civil Engineers recognised the bridge as an international historic engineering landmark. It was one of the first sites outside the Americas and Europe to receive that honour.

The second surprise is how the main arch is built. It is not one single vault. It is made of many separate, narrow arch rings, laid side by side along the width of the bridge, and joined with iron ties and shaped stone. Each ring can stand on its own. This meant builders could work on one section at a time. It also meant that a damaged ring could be repaired without taking apart the whole bridge. That modular thinking helps explain why the structure has lasted so long.

The bridge has not survived untouched, of course. Over the centuries it has been repaired many times, after floods, earthquakes, and simple wear. In the 1930s, the architectural historian Liang Sicheng came here to survey it. His careful drawings and photographs brought the bridge to wider scholarly attention. A major restoration followed in the 1950s. During that work, many old carved stone panels were recovered from the riverbed, where they had fallen long ago.

Take a closer look at the railings. The panels carry carved dragons, some twisting through the stone, some shown face on, with flowing manes and clawed feet. Many of the pieces on the bridge today are careful copies. Some recovered originals are kept nearby for display. Compare them if you can. The older carving is softer and more weathered, and you can see how centuries of water and silt have rounded every edge.

And then there are the stories. Legend has it that the bridge was built in a single night by Lu Ban, the legendary master carpenter of Chinese folklore. According to one popular tale, two immortals heard about it and came to test its strength. One rode a donkey. The other pushed a cart loaded with the weight of mountains. The bridge shook, and Lu Ban dived into the river to hold it up with his hands.

Locals say the marks of that night are still here. On the deck, people point out dips said to be the donkey's hoof prints and the ruts of the cart. Beneath the arch, some say you can find the print of Lu Ban's hand. These are folk stories, not history, but they are part of the bridge's life. For generations, this tale was sung in a well-known northern Chinese folk song, and children here grow up knowing it.

Today, no traffic crosses the deck. The bridge sits inside a landscaped park, with paths along the banks and views of the arch from below. Walk down to the water's edge if you can. From there, the four small arches line up clearly against the sky, and you understand the design at a glance. The light from the side is often kindest, catching the texture of the grey stone and the shadows in the carving.

Before you leave, stand at the centre of the deck one more time. Feel how gentle the slope is under your shoes. Look down at the stones, worn into slight hollows by countless feet. People crossed here when this was a busy road between the plains and the north. You are standing on their path. As you walk away, turn back once and look at the outline of the bridge, low and light over the riverbed, still holding its shape.
