The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the mound and look up. The figure seems to keep rising. First come the folds of a long white robe, then the broad shoulders, then two arms held wide open. Far above, the head is tilted gently forward. A gold crown catches the light even on a grey Polish morning. Around you, the wind moves across open fields and car tyres hum on the road. This is the statue of Christ the King, in the small town of Świebodzin in western Poland.

The statue was finished in 2010 and dedicated in November of that year, close to the Catholic feast of Christ the King. That feast falls at the end of the church year. It celebrates the idea of Christ as ruler over all creation, and it gives the monument its full name: Jesus Christ the King of the Universe. Since then, many people have described the statue as the tallest figure of Jesus in the world, taller even than the famous statue above Rio de Janeiro.

The numbers here were chosen with care. The figure itself is thirty-three metres tall. That height refers to the traditional age of Jesus at his death, which Christians believe was thirty-three years. The crown adds a few more metres. The whole figure then stands on an artificial mound, a raised hill built for this purpose. So the total height rises to around fifty metres above the ground at your feet.

Now for the first surprise. This was not a project of the state, or of a large church institution. The idea came mainly from one local parish priest, Father Sylwester Zawadzki. He wanted to give his town a sign of faith that people could see from far away. Much of the money came from donations, many of them from ordinary believers. Over several years, the project moved slowly from a dream to a building site, and then to this white giant.

The statue is not carved from stone. It is built from concrete and fibreglass around a steel frame inside. The design is credited to the sculptor Mirosław Patecki. The individual sections were made separately and then lifted into place by cranes, piece by piece. The head and the crown went up last. Workers built the figure in the open air, through Polish weather, and people from the town came to watch it grow.

Walk slowly around the mound and look at the shape of the robe. From a distance, the surface looks smooth and simple. Up close, you can see the joints between the panels and the small marks of weather on the white finish. The design is plain on purpose. There are no complicated details, because the figure was made to be read from far away — from cars, from fields, from the edge of town.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a modern one. In 2018, Polish media reported that telecommunications equipment had been placed inside the gold crown. In other words, the crown of Christ was helping to carry internet and phone signals for the area. The story spread quickly around the world. For many people it became the perfect symbol of this monument: very old beliefs, held up by very new technology.

The statue also caused real debate when it was built. Some people in Poland praised it as a strong sign of faith. Others found it too large, too costly, or simply out of scale with a quiet provincial town. Some critics called it kitsch, meaning art that is showy rather than refined. Today, the statue still divides opinion. That debate is part of its story, and it is worth thinking about as you stand here.

The statue stands next to the Sanctuary of Divine Mercy, a Catholic church and place of prayer. Divine Mercy is a devotion that is especially close to many Polish Catholics. It is linked to the visions of Saint Faustina, a Polish nun of the early twentieth century. For pilgrims, the statue and the sanctuary belong together. Some people come here as tourists, but many come to pray, to light a candle, or to attend Mass.

The town itself carries a complicated past. Świebodzin was for centuries part of German lands, and before 1945 it was known by the German name Schwiebus. After the Second World War, the borders of Europe changed. The town became part of Poland, and its population was largely replaced by new Polish settlers, many of them from lands further east. Raising a giant Catholic figure here can be read, in part, as a statement of a newer identity.

If you have time after your visit, go into the old centre of Świebodzin. The town has a historic town hall and a market square with low, colourful houses around it. The streets are quiet and walkable. The contrast is striking. Down in the centre, the scale is human and old. Out here on the edge of town, the scale is enormous and new. Seeing both gives you a fuller picture of the place.

Many travellers first notice the statue from the road, because it stands close to a major route through western Poland. Drivers see the white figure appear above the fields, often with no warning. It is a strange experience to meet a building of this size in such open, flat country. Some people stop on impulse, just to see it closer. Perhaps that is how you arrived here too.

Before you leave, stand again where you began and look up once more. Notice how the open arms seem to reach beyond the mound, toward the town and the roads around it. Listen to the wind, the traffic, maybe a church bell from the sanctuary. Whatever you believe, this is a place where one community tried to make its faith visible on a huge scale. As you walk away, turn around once. The figure will still be watching the fields behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning for softer light and fewer visitors.
Walk the full circle around the mound for different angles.
Step back across the grounds to fit the whole statue in frame.
Respect worshippers if you enter the Divine Mercy sanctuary.
Combine your visit with a walk in the old town centre.
