The story

Church bells ring across a small square. Doves scatter from the towers, circle once, and settle again in the laurel trees. Under the trees, iron benches sit in deep shade. A white gazebo stands at the center, bright in the sun. This is Plaza Mijares, the main square of San José del Cabo, and the heart of the old town.

Every Mexican town keeps its life in a square like this. The plan is Spanish and colonial: a church, a square, and the town wrapped around them. Here, the church faces the plaza from one side. Shops, cafes, and the city offices stand nearby. School children cross in the morning. Families arrive in the evening. Music plays on weekends. If you want to understand the town rather than the resorts, start here.

The plaza carries a soldier's name. José Antonio Mijares was a young lieutenant in the Mexican navy. In 1847, during the war between Mexico and the United States, American forces occupied this port town. Mexican soldiers and local ranchers fought to take it back.

Mijares led an attack on the occupied garrison in November of that year. He was killed in the fighting. The struggle for the town continued into 1848, with a long siege before American ships brought relief to the garrison. When the war ended, Baja California remained part of Mexico, and the town remembered its fallen lieutenant. His name marks this plaza and the main boulevard beside it.

The town itself is much older than that war. San José del Cabo began as a Spanish mission in 1730, founded beside the freshwater estuary nearby. Farms and orchards followed the water. Ships called at the bay for water and supplies, and the plaza grew into the town's marketplace. By the nineteenth century, this was a quiet farming town of sugarcane, mangoes, and cattle, with its plaza at the center.

Look across the square at the church. Its two towers rise above the trees. It stands where the mission stood, and it is still the parish of Saint Joseph, the town's patron. The church has its own long story, told in a separate guide. From the plaza, notice how the whole square arranges itself around it.

Walk the edges of the plaza. The gazebo at the center works as a bandstand during festivals. Nearby, large letters spell out the town's name, a favorite spot for photographs. The surrounding buildings are low, one and two stories, with thick walls and bright paint. Many date from the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

On the boulevard side, look for the Palacio Municipal, the city hall, with its shaded courtyard. Government offices have stood near this square since the town's early days. City hall, church, market, plaza: the old Spanish town plan survives here, piece by piece, under the tourism of the present.

The plaza changes with the clock. In the morning, it is quiet. Shopkeepers sweep their doorways, and the benches belong to old men and pigeons. In the heat of the afternoon, the shade matters more than anything. At sunset, the square fills. Children chase each other around the gazebo. Vendors sell ice cream and sweet bread.

On Thursday evenings from November through June, the nearby streets hold the Art Walk. Galleries open late, and the crowd flows between the plaza and the gallery district one block away. Painters stand beside their work, and music drifts from open doorways. The square becomes a meeting point, full of slow walkers and conversation.

In March, the town honors its patron saint. The feast of Saint Joseph brings processions, music, dancing, and food stalls around the plaza. Fireworks end the nights, and the square smells of grilled corn and sugar. These celebrations are older than any hotel on the coast. For a few days, the square shows what the town has been for almost three centuries.

Resorts and golf courses now surround San José del Cabo, and cruise passengers pass through by the busload. The plaza absorbs it all without changing its pace. Waiters still set tables in shaded courtyards. Bells still mark the hours. The town square remains a place to sit rather than a place to rush.

Find a bench and stay a while. Watch the light move across the church towers. Listen to the mix of Spanish and English, sandals and church bells. A square like this holds a town's memory: a mission, a battle, a saint's day, ten thousand quiet evenings. For a few minutes, its memory includes you. Then let the bells send you on your way.
