The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. It is not stone. It is earth, packed hard, covered in grass, and shaped by human hands into long slopes and sharp angles. Walk a few steps and you feel the land rise and fall in a pattern that nature would never make. Wind moves across the open ramparts. In summer, insects hum in the long grass. This is the Fortress of Saint Elizabeth, and almost everything you see here is made of soil.

The fortress belongs to the middle of the eighteenth century. Construction began in 1754, when the Russian Empire pushed its southern frontier into the open steppe. The name honours Saint Elizabeth, the patron saint of Empress Elizabeth Petrovna, who ruled Russia at that time. The location was chosen with care. This was border country, close to the lands of the Crimean Khanate and the Ottoman Empire. Whoever held these grasslands held the road to the Black Sea.

To understand the shape around you, picture the fortress from the air. It was laid out as a star with six points. Each point is a bastion, an arrow-shaped projection of earth that stuck out from the main wall. This design was the standard military answer to cannon fire in that era. Defenders on one bastion could fire along the face of the next one. So no attacker could hide at the foot of a wall. Every metre of ground was covered.

Why earth and not stone? The answer is practical. Cannonballs shatter masonry, but they sink into thick earth and stop. Soil absorbs the shock. On the open steppe, earth was also everywhere, and good building stone was not. Thousands of labourers dug ditches and piled the spoil into ramparts. Some of these workers were soldiers. Many others were brought in from surrounding regions. Their shovels shaped the slopes you are walking on now.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. This fortress is the reason the city exists at all. A garrison needs food, horses, cloth, and tools. Merchants and craftsmen settled outside the walls to supply the soldiers. Their settlement grew into a town called Yelisavetgrad, which simply means Elizabeth's city. Over two and a half centuries, that town became the city of Kropyvnytskyi around you. The fortress is not a monument inside the city. It is the city's seed.

The frontier did not stay here for long. In the second half of the eighteenth century, Russia fought a series of wars against the Ottoman Empire. Each victory moved the border further south, toward the sea. Within a few decades, the fortress stood deep inside imperial territory instead of on its edge. Its guns faced an enemy who was no longer there. By the end of the century, it had lost its military purpose. The walls stayed, but the soldiers slowly moved on.

The history of this land is also more complex than one empire's map. Before the fortress, these steppes were open country. Zaporozhian Cossacks, the free warrior communities of the lower Dnipro River, used and claimed much of this territory. Imperial settlement here was part of a wider effort to bring the region under central control. That effort ended Cossack independence later in the century. So when you look at these ramparts, you are looking at a line of power drawn across someone else's grazing land.

Now look at the city's name itself, because it tells its own story. Yelisavetgrad became Zinovievsk in the 1920s, then Kirovo, then Kirovohrad. Each name followed the politics of its moment. In 2016, the city took the name Kropyvnytskyi. It honours Marko Kropyvnytskyi, a nineteenth-century playwright and actor who helped build Ukrainian professional theatre. That is the second surprise. A fortress named for an empress now stands in a city named for a man of the stage.

Take a moment to walk along the top of a rampart. Notice how the slope on the outer side is steeper than the slope on the inner side. That difference was deliberate. The outer face had to stop climbers. The inner face had to let defenders move quickly up to their positions. Look also for the low ground beyond the walls. Ditches once circled the fortress. Today grass softens their edges, but the hollows are still there if you look closely.

The interior of the fortress once held barracks, storehouses, and officers' quarters. There was also a church, as there was in almost every garrison of the empire. Most of those buildings are gone now. Time, rebuilding, and changing needs removed them. What survives best is the earthwork itself. That is a quiet lesson in materials. The wooden and brick structures disappeared, while the humble heaped soil outlasted them all.

Today the site works partly as a historical reserve and partly as open green space. Local families walk dogs here. Children run up the slopes and roll down them, the same slopes that were once designed to break an assault. Joggers trace the zigzag line of the old walls without thinking about it. This everyday use is part of the fortress's story too. A military machine has slowly turned into a public park.

If you stand on one of the higher points, look out toward the surrounding streets. You can see how the city has pressed close to the old defences. Rooftops, trees, and church domes appear above the line of the ramparts. The Inhul River runs nearby, and its valley helped make this site defensible. From up here, you can imagine the view an eighteenth-century sentry had. Then, there was only open steppe, grass moving in long waves to the horizon.

The light changes this place more than you might expect. At midday, the earthworks look flat and green. In the early morning or late afternoon, low sunlight throws long shadows across every slope and ditch. Suddenly the star shape becomes readable. The angles sharpen. The pattern that engineers drew on paper almost three centuries ago appears again on the ground. If you can, return at that hour and walk the line once more.

Before you leave, pause and look down at the grass under your feet one more time. Somewhere beneath it is the work of thousands of hands, moving soil one shovelful at a time. They built a wall against an enemy who soon disappeared. Yet their work gave a city its beginning. As you walk back toward the streets, notice how the ground slopes gently downward. You are stepping off the fortress, and into the city it created.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full rampart line to feel the star shape.
Come in early morning or late afternoon for shadows.
Wear sturdy shoes for grassy, uneven earthen slopes.
Climb a high bastion point for views over the city.
