The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, at the foot of the Helan Mountains, the wind has almost nothing to stop it. It moves across open gravel and dry scrub and catches the edges of your jacket. The ground crunches under your shoes. Ahead of you, rising from the flat plain, stand tall mounds of pale, golden-brown earth. Each one is shaped like a blunt, weathered beehive. In the afternoon light they look almost soft... but they are made of earth packed hard enough to outlast an empire.

These are the Western Xia mausoleums, the royal tombs of a kingdom that many history books barely mention. The Western Xia, also called Xixia, ruled this corner of northwest China for nearly two centuries, from the eleventh century into the thirteenth. Its people were the Tangut, a group with their own language, their own script, and their own deep Buddhist faith. Yinchuan, the city behind you, was their capital. And this plain was where they buried their emperors.

Take a moment to look at the scale of the site. The cemetery spreads across a wide strip of land along the base of the mountains. Archaeologists have identified nine imperial tombs here. Around them lie more than two hundred smaller burials — the graves of nobles, officials, and members of the royal family. So you are not standing at a single monument. You are standing inside a whole landscape of the dead, laid out with care, facing away from the mountains and toward the river plain.

Now look closely at one of the large mounds. Today it is bare earth. But it was not always so plain. The mounds were built from rammed earth, which means layers of soil pressed down hard, one on top of another. Look for rows of small holes running around the sides. Those holes once held wooden beams. The beams supported tiled eaves, so each mound probably looked like a tiered tower, roofed in glazed tiles that caught the sun.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. The tall mound is not the centre of the tomb. Each imperial burial was once a walled compound, with gates, towers, and spaces for ritual. The mound sits toward the back of that enclosure. Visitors in the past would have walked through a sequence of gateways and courtyards before they reached it. Look along the ground for low earthen ridges. Those are the worn remains of the walls that once framed the whole precinct.

The kingdom itself began with an ambitious ruler named Li Yuanhao. In 1038, he declared himself emperor and set the Tangut state on equal footing with the Song dynasty to the east. He gave his people a written language of their own. Tangut script looks a little like Chinese characters at first glance. But it is a separate system, dense with strokes, and it was used for law, poetry, and Buddhist scripture. Many scholars believe the largest tomb on this site belonged to Li Yuanhao himself.

That script is part of why this place matters so much. For centuries, Tangut writing was almost forgotten. Fragments of carved stone tablets found around these tombs carry inscriptions in it. Those broken pieces helped scholars piece together the language of a vanished state. So when you visit the on-site museum, take time with the stone fragments. They look modest. Yet each one is a small surviving voice from a culture that very nearly disappeared without a trace.

Why did it disappear? The answer is the Mongols. In the early thirteenth century, Genghis Khan led repeated campaigns against the Western Xia. The final war ended in 1227, and the kingdom was destroyed. Genghis Khan himself died during that last campaign. The destruction was severe. Cities were sacked, and the tombs were damaged and looted. When later historians wrote official histories of the neighbouring dynasties, the Western Xia did not receive one of its own.

That silence gives these mounds a strange power. Much of what we know about the Tangut comes not from their own records, but from what survived in the ground. Excavations here have uncovered glazed roof tiles, carved stone figures, and decorated building pieces. Inside the museum, look for the stone pedestals carved as crouching, muscular figures with heavy shoulders. They once held up stone tablets. Their faces are wide and fierce, and they are among the most memorable objects from the site.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a story, not a proven fact. Locals say that birds do not land on the great mounds, and that grass refuses to grow on them. People tell this as a sign of the tombs' sacred nature. It is a lovely tale to think about as you stand here. The simpler explanation is the hard, packed earth and the dry, windy climate. But watch the mounds for a while, and see what you notice for yourself.

Visitors sometimes call this place the Pyramids of the East. The comparison is not exact, of course. These are earth, not cut stone, and they were built for a very different faith and empire. But the feeling is similar — great shapes on a dry plain, built to make a ruler's memory permanent. In 2025, the tombs were added to the UNESCO World Heritage list, which brought new attention to a site that was long overlooked outside China.

As you walk between the tombs, notice the colours around you. The mounds shift from sand to ochre to soft grey as clouds pass. Behind them, the Helan Mountains form a dark, jagged wall along the western sky. That mountain range mattered to the Tangut. It sheltered their capital from desert winds and marked the edge of their world. The tombs were placed with the mountains at their back, a protective line for the emperors in death.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Let the wind fill your ears again. Look at the nearest mound and picture it as it once was — wooden eaves, gleaming tiles, walls and gates, people moving in ritual. Then look at it as it is now, plain and patient under the open sky. On your way back toward Yinchuan, glance over your shoulder. The mounds will grow smaller against the mountains... still holding the memory of a kingdom that almost vanished.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a hat and water; the plain has little shade.
Visit in late afternoon for warmer light on the mounds.
Pack a windproof layer for gusts off the mountains.
Start at the museum to understand the tombs first.
Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths between the tombs.
