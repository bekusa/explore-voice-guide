The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, water is moving over stones. That is the River Eamont, running low and steady past the castle walls. The grass underfoot is soft and often damp. Ahead of you, red sandstone rises in broken towers. The stone shifts colour with the weather, from dull rust under cloud to a warm pink when the sun comes through. Welcome to Brougham Castle, just outside Penrith, on the old road between England and Scotland.

Before we look at the castle, look at the ground around it. Those low, grassy banks to the south are not natural. They mark the outline of a Roman fort called Brocavum. Roman soldiers chose this spot for good reasons. There was a river crossing here, and important roads met nearby. More than a thousand years later, medieval builders came to the same conclusion. They built their castle beside the old fort and used its ready supply of cut stone.

The story of the castle begins in the early thirteenth century. A powerful lord named Robert de Vieuxpont was given lands in this part of the north. He started the great stone keep you can still see today. It was a tall, square tower, built to be lived in and defended at the same time. This was a dangerous frontier. Raids from across the Scottish border were a real threat for generations, and a strong tower was a practical answer.

Later, the castle passed by marriage to the Clifford family. The Cliffords became one of the great landowning families of the north of England. They strengthened Brougham in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries. They added a gatehouse, more towers, and stronger walls. Through all of this, the keep stayed at the heart of the castle. Each generation built around it rather than replacing it.

Here is a detail that many visitors walk straight past. When you reach the gatehouse, look up at the stonework above the inner entrance. Carved there is a short inscription in old English spelling. It reads, in effect, that a man named Roger made this. This is usually linked to Roger Clifford, who carried out building work here. It is a rare thing — a builder's name, cut into the wall, still readable after more than seven centuries.

Now step inside the gatehouse passage and notice how the space narrows. Medieval gatehouses were designed to slow people down. Visitors, and attackers, had to pass through a tight, controlled route under the eyes of the guards. Look for the slots and grooves in the stone. These held gates and barriers. Even in ruin, the passage still makes you feel watched as you walk through it.

The second surprise is older still. Inside the castle, you can find carved Roman stones. Some are tombstones, made for people who lived and died at the fort nearby. Medieval builders reused them as simple building material. Today they are displayed so that you can see them clearly. Look closely at the lettering. These are names and words carved by Roman hands, now resting inside an English castle.

As the centuries passed, the border grew quieter and the castle lost its military purpose. By the early seventeenth century, Brougham was in poor condition. Then one of the most remarkable people in northern English history arrived. Her name was Lady Anne Clifford. She had spent many years in a long legal fight to win back her family's northern estates. When she finally inherited them in the middle of the seventeenth century, she set to work.

Lady Anne restored several of her family's castles, and Brougham was one of her favourites. Her father, George Clifford, the Earl of Cumberland, had been born here. She repaired the buildings, made them comfortable again, and lived here for long periods. She was known for travelling between her castles with a large household. She was also known for keeping careful records of her life and work. She died here at Brougham in her old age, in 1676.

One of her most moving works is not inside the castle at all. A short distance away, beside the road, stands a stone monument known as the Countess Pillar. Lady Anne built it to mark the place where she said goodbye to her mother for the last time. She also arranged for money to be given to the poor of the parish each year at that spot. It is a small, personal memorial, and it is worth the short walk or drive to see it.

After Lady Anne's death, the castle's fortunes turned again. Her heirs had other homes and little use for Brougham. Over time, valuable materials were removed and sold, and the buildings were left open to the weather. Roofs went, floors went, and the walls slowly settled into the ruin you see now. Today the site is cared for by English Heritage, who keep it safe and open to visitors.

When you climb the keep, take your time on the stairs. The steps are worn and uneven, and the spaces are tight. From the upper levels, the view opens out across the river and the green fields. You can trace the line of the Roman fort below. On a clear day, the hills of the Lake District and the Pennines frame the horizon. It helps you understand why so many people wanted to hold this place.

As you walk through the empty rooms, look for traces of daily life. There are fireplaces set high in the walls, where floors once stood. There are window seats cut into the thick stone. There are small chambers and passages tucked into corners. These details turn a ruin back into a home. Someone sat in that window. Someone kept warm by that fire on a cold northern night.

Before you leave, stand for a moment by the river. Roman soldiers, medieval lords, and a determined countess all stood near this same water. The castle has changed many times, but the river and the crossing are still here. Listen again to the water over the stones. Then, as you walk back towards the road, turn once and look at the red walls behind you, quiet now in the open fields.
