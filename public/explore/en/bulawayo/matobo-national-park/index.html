The guide

The story

Put your palm flat against the granite. In the morning it is cool and slightly rough, like coarse sandpaper. By afternoon the same stone holds the heat of the sun and gives it back to your skin. Around you, enormous boulders sit stacked on one another at angles that seem impossible. A breeze moves through the dry grass, and somewhere above, a bird calls once and goes quiet. Welcome to Matobo, the granite hills south of Bulawayo.

This landscape is mostly rock, and very old rock. The granite here formed deep underground billions of years ago, long before any animal walked the earth. Over immense stretches of time, water and weather wore away the softer ground around it. What remained are the shapes you see now. There are smooth, bald domes that local people call dwalas. There are rocky hills called kopjes. And there are towers of boulders that look as if a giant stacked them and then walked away.

The name itself comes from that view. A popular explanation says the Ndebele king Mzilikazi named these hills in the nineteenth century. He looked at the rounded granite domes and thought of a gathering of old men with bald heads. Matobo, or Matopos in the older spelling, is often translated as bald heads. Whether or not the story is exactly true, look at the hills for a moment. The comparison is hard to forget.

People lived among these rocks long before any king arrived. For thousands of years, San hunter-gatherers sheltered in the caves and under the granite overhangs. They left behind one of the densest collections of rock art in southern Africa. Researchers have recorded thousands of painted sites across the hills. The paintings show giraffe, kudu, and other animals, along with human figures who hunt, dance, and move in lines across the stone.

Several of these painted shelters are open to visitors. Nswatugi Cave is known for its graceful giraffe and antelope. Pomongwe Cave has a small museum, and excavations there found long sequences of human occupation. Bambata Cave gave its name to a style of ancient pottery. Stand quietly inside one of these shelters. The paintings are often faint, in shades of red, ochre, and white. Your eyes need a little time to find them.

Here is something many visitors miss. The paintings are not simple pictures of a hunt. Many scholars believe they connect to spiritual beliefs, to trance, rain, and the power of animals. So when you see a line of figures or an antelope with strange marks, you may be looking at a record of ritual, not just daily life. We cannot fully read these images today. That uncertainty is part of what makes them so powerful.

These hills are still sacred. Matobo has long been linked to Mwari, the high god of the Shona and other peoples of the region. Shrines in the hills, such as Njelele, are traditional places where people ask for rain. Ceremonies still take place in some of these locations. If you visit a site that local people treat as holy, move gently, speak softly, and follow any guidance you receive. The spiritual life of these rocks did not end with history.

In 2003, UNESCO added the Matobo Hills to the World Heritage List. The recognition covered more than the scenery. It honoured the long relationship between people and this landscape. That means the rock art, the sacred shrines, and the communities who still live in and around the hills. The national park protects the core of this area, but the wider hills reach far beyond its fences.

Now a very different chapter. On top of one great granite dome lies the grave of Cecil John Rhodes, the British businessman and colonial politician. He chose this spot himself and called it World's View. He was buried here in 1902. The hill has an older Ndebele name, Malindidzimu, often translated as the place of the spirits. A plain bronze plate marks his grave, set straight into the living rock.

This summit is a place of strong and mixed feelings. For some, it is a historical landmark. For many Zimbabweans, it is a reminder of colonial conquest and the loss of land. Nearby stands a large stone memorial to the Shangani Patrol, British soldiers killed during the war of 1893. Other colonial figures are buried on the hill too. Mzilikazi himself rests elsewhere in the hills, at his own burial site. Two histories share the same granite.

Matobo is also a home for wildlife. The rocky hills hold one of the highest densities of Verreaux's eagles anywhere. These are large black eagles with a white mark on the back. They hunt rock hyraxes, small furry animals that sun themselves on the boulders. The park also protects white and black rhino in a fenced game area. Leopards live here too, though they are shy and rarely seen.

So watch the stone surfaces closely as you walk. Look for the hyraxes first; they often sit very still. Look for lizards with bright blue or orange heads. Notice the lichens, which paint the granite in patches of orange, lime green, and grey. Between the boulders, trees push their roots into narrow cracks. Some fig trees seem to grip the rock like fingers. Life here has adapted to very thin soil.

The light changes the hills completely through the day. At midday the granite looks pale and flat. In the late afternoon, the low sun turns it gold, then copper, and every boulder throws a long shadow. If you can, be on a high dome near sunset. From World's View, ridge after ridge of granite runs to the horizon. The silence up there is deep, broken only by wind.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Feel the stone under your shoes. Look at the boulders balancing in the distance, and remember the painters who sheltered under them, the pilgrims who still come for rain, and the eagles circling overhead. These hills have watched all of it. As you walk back, notice the next stack of rocks you pass. It has been standing there far longer than any story told about it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb World's View in late afternoon for golden light.
Wear shoes with good grip for smooth granite domes.
Bring plenty of water; the rock reflects strong heat.
Book a guided walk to track rhino safely.
Speak softly and follow guidance at sacred shrine sites.
