The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves across the flat land, carrying fine dust and the faint smell of river mud. Somewhere ahead, beyond the reeds and the fences, the Amu Darya slides past. It is wide, brown and slow. Across that water lies Afghanistan. Between the two banks runs a long, plain span of concrete and steel. This is the Afghanistan–Uzbekistan Friendship Bridge, and very few structures in Central Asia have carried so much history on so little ornament.

Let us begin with the river, because the river came first. The Amu Darya is the waterway that the ancient Greeks called the Oxus. For thousands of years it has marked a line between worlds. On this side lay the oasis lands of Bactria and later Sogdiana. On the far side, the routes climbed south toward the Hindu Kush. Merchants, monks, soldiers and pilgrims have all looked at this same water and wondered how to cross it.

Termez itself grew up because of that problem. It is one of the oldest cities in Central Asia. In the early centuries of the common era, Buddhism spread through this region, and monks built monasteries near the riverbank. Archaeologists have excavated Buddhist sites around Termez, including Fayaz Tepe and Kara Tepe. So when you stand here, you stand near a crossing point that is far older than any bridge. The concrete is modern... but the idea of crossing here is ancient.

The bridge you see today is a Soviet project. It was built while Uzbekistan was still a republic of the Soviet Union, and it opened in 1982. That timing matters. Soviet troops had entered Afghanistan at the end of 1979, and the army needed a reliable way to move fuel, vehicles and supplies south. A ferry was too slow. A pontoon was too fragile. So engineers built a permanent crossing that could carry both road traffic and trains.

That dual design is the first detail many visitors miss. This is not only a road bridge. A railway track and a roadway share the same deck. Trains and trucks take turns, rather than running side by side. On the Afghan side, the bridge lands at the river port of Hairatan. For a long time, this was one of the very few rail links into Afghanistan at all. Later, a railway line was extended south from Hairatan toward the city of Mazar-i-Sharif.

The name, Friendship Bridge, came from the official language of the Soviet era. Many projects of that time carried names about friendship and brotherhood between peoples. Here, the name sat beside a hard reality. For most of the 1980s, this span carried a war. Columns of trucks rolled south. Soldiers crossed in both directions. Families in Termez came to know the bridge as the road their sons took.

Then came the moment that made this place famous. In February 1989, the Soviet Union completed the withdrawal of its forces from Afghanistan. The last columns crossed this bridge northward. According to widely reported accounts, the commanding general, Boris Gromov, was the last Soviet soldier to walk across. Photographs and film from that day show him on the span, crossing back to Soviet soil. For many people, that walk became the image that closed a decade of war.

Here is the second surprise. After that withdrawal, the bridge did not become a busy, open border. The opposite happened. In the 1990s, Afghanistan fell into civil war, and the Taliban took control of large parts of the country. Uzbekistan, by then independent, closed the crossing for several years. The bridge stood mostly silent. Concrete blocks and soldiers replaced the convoys. The span that had carried an army now carried almost nothing at all.

It reopened at the end of 2001, after the fall of the first Taliban government. Its new role was humanitarian. Food, fuel and aid moved south across the river into northern Afghanistan. In the years that followed, the bridge also became part of a northern supply route used by international forces. Freight wagons, tanker trucks and containers became the everyday traffic. The bridge returned to its working purpose — moving heavy things from one bank to the other.

In 2021, the Taliban returned to power in Afghanistan, and this border became tense once more. Yet trade did not stop. Today the bridge remains one of the main channels for goods moving between Uzbekistan and Afghanistan. Grain, fuel and building materials still cross here. Termez has also developed trade facilities near the border. So the bridge is not a monument frozen in the past. It is a living piece of infrastructure, still doing heavy daily work.

Now, a practical and honest word. This is an active, guarded international border. It is not a promenade. Ordinary visitors cannot simply walk out onto the span, and photography near the crossing can be restricted. Soldiers and border officials take this seriously. Most travellers see the bridge from a distance, from approved viewpoints or from a vehicle on the approach roads. Ask your driver or guide where it is permitted to stop. And if anyone in uniform asks you to lower your camera, simply do so.

From wherever you are allowed to stand, take a long look at the shape of the thing. Notice how low and level it runs, close to the water. There are no towers and no decorative arches. It is a working structure, built for weight and endurance rather than beauty. Watch the river current pushing against the supports. See the line of the far bank, often hazy in the heat. On a clear day, that thin strip of land is Afghanistan, close enough to see and still far away.

Before you leave, let your eyes move from the bridge back to the river. The water does not care about names or flags. It ran past Buddhist monks, past caravans, past the trucks of the 1980s and the aid convoys after them. It is running past you now. As you walk or drive away, listen again to the wind across the flat ground. Notice the dust, the reeds, the wide sky. This crossing has seen armies come and go... and the river is still here.
