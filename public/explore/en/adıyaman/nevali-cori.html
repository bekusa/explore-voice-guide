The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across a wide sheet of water, and small waves slap against a pale, rocky shore. The air smells of dust, dry grass and warm stone. Somewhere out there, beneath the surface of this reservoir, lies one of the oldest known places of worship on Earth. You cannot walk into it today. But you are close to where it once stood, and its story is still worth hearing.

The site is called Nevalı Çori. It sat on the slopes of a small valley that ran down toward the Euphrates River. The place lies in the borderlands between Adıyaman and Şanlıurfa provinces, in southeastern Turkey. For thousands of years it lay hidden under soil and terraced fields. Farmers grew crops above it without knowing what lay below. Then, in the twentieth century, a great dam changed the landscape forever.

The dam was the Atatürk Dam, one of the largest engineering projects in Turkish history. As its reservoir began to fill, the valleys of the upper Euphrates were set to disappear under water. Archaeologists knew that dozens of ancient sites would be lost. So a race began. Teams from Turkey and abroad worked to record and rescue what they could before the water arrived. Nevalı Çori was one of the sites in that rescue effort.

The excavations were led by the German archaeologist Harald Hauptmann. His team worked here through the 1980s and into the early 1990s. What they found surprised the academic world. This was not a simple farming hamlet. It was a settlement from the Neolithic period, the New Stone Age, when people were only beginning to farm. And it contained a building unlike anything most scholars had seen from such an early date.

Picture a rectangular room, partly set into the hillside. Its walls were made of stone, and a stone bench ran along the inside. Into this bench, at regular intervals, people had set tall stone pillars. In the centre of the room stood more pillars, freestanding. Many of them had a distinctive shape: a tall, upright shaft with a wide flat block across the top. Seen from the side, each one looks like a giant capital letter T.

Here is the first surprise. Look closely at some of those pillars, and you see arms. Thin arms are carved in low relief along the sides, with hands that meet across the front. The pillars are not just supports. They seem to represent figures, perhaps human, perhaps something more than human. The T-shaped top may be a head seen in profile. These stones were standing people, made of limestone, gathered in a circle of silence.

The floor of this building also drew attention. It was hard and smooth, made from a mix of lime and small stones, polished to a firm surface. Archaeologists often compare it to terrazzo, the polished floor you might find in a modern hallway. For a community with no metal tools, this was careful and demanding work. It suggests the building mattered deeply to the people who used it. Most scholars interpret it as a ritual or cult building rather than a home.

The sculptures found here are just as striking. Among them is a carved head with a bald skull and a snake, or perhaps a braid of hair, running down the back. There are figures that combine human and bird forms. There are small clay figurines of people, too. These works are around ten thousand years old, give or take. That makes them older than the pyramids of Egypt by many thousands of years, and older than writing itself.

And here is the second surprise. Nevalı Çori was where a young archaeologist named Klaus Schmidt first worked with these T-shaped pillars. Some years later, Schmidt went on to excavate Göbekli Tepe, near the city of Şanlıurfa. That site has much larger pillars and far more carvings, and it changed how many people think about the birth of civilisation. In a real sense, the path to Göbekli Tepe began here, in this flooded valley.

There is also a story about bread. Close to this region rise the Karacadağ mountains, a volcanic range to the east. Genetic studies have suggested that wild einkorn wheat from that area is closely related to the earliest farmed einkorn. Einkorn is one of the very first cereals that humans domesticated. Nevalı Çori lies within this wider zone. So the people who carved these pillars lived in one of the places where farming itself may have taken shape.

When the excavations ended, the water came. The reservoir rose over the valley, and the site was submerged in the early 1990s. The great building, the bench, the polished floor... all now lie under the lake. But the archaeologists managed to save many of the finest finds. Several pillars and sculptures were lifted out before the flood. Today they are on display in the Şanlıurfa Archaeology Museum, where you can stand face to face with them.

So what does it mean to visit Nevalı Çori now? It means standing beside a landscape that has changed completely. The river valley has become a wide lake, with steep, bare hills on every side. Light on the water shifts from silver in the morning to deep blue later in the day. The modern lake is a reminder that human beings have always reshaped this land, from the first farmers to the dam builders.

Take a moment to look at the hills around you. The limestone in those slopes is the same kind of stone the Neolithic builders quarried for their pillars. Notice how pale it is, and how it breaks into flat, sharp-edged pieces. Imagine people with stone tools, shaping blocks taller than themselves, carrying them down the slope. Imagine them carving arms and hands into the rock, and setting each pillar into place with care.

As you turn away from the water, listen once more to the small waves against the shore. Somewhere beneath them, the stone figures that were too large to move still stand in the dark, with their carved arms folded across their bodies. They have waited ten thousand years already. Carry that picture with you as you walk on, through hills that held a quiet beginning long before anyone wrote a word.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Şanlıurfa Archaeology Museum to see the rescued pillars.
Combine this trip with a visit to Göbekli Tepe.
Bring water and sun protection for the exposed lakeshore.
Come in early morning for softer light over the reservoir.
