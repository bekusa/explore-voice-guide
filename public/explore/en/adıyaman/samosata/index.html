The guide

The story

Listen first to the water. Wind moves across a wide sheet of blue-green lake, and small waves tap against the stony shore. The air smells of dust, dry grass, and wet rock. The horizon is low and open on every side. It is hard to believe, standing here, that you are looking at the site of a royal capital. Ancient Samosata, the old seat of the kings of Commagene, lies beneath and beside this water.

For most of its long life, this place stood on the banks of the Euphrates. The great river of Mesopotamia ran past the city on its way south toward Syria and the Persian Gulf. A river crossing like this one was valuable. Armies, traders, and pilgrims all needed a place to cross. So for thousands of years, people built, rebuilt, and fought over this bend in the river.

The heart of the ancient city was a large mound. Archaeologists call this kind of mound a höyük in Turkish, or a tell in the wider Near East. A tell forms slowly. People build houses of mud brick and stone. The houses collapse, and new ones rise on top. Over many centuries, a city lifts itself on its own ruins. At Samosata, these layers reached far back into prehistory, long before any king or Roman soldier arrived.

The city became famous as the capital of Commagene. This was a small but wealthy kingdom that flourished in the last centuries before Christ. Its rulers sat between two great powers — Rome to the west, and Parthian Persia to the east. They survived by careful diplomacy. Their royal family mixed Persian, Armenian, and Greek traditions, and they proudly displayed all of them at once.

The most famous of these rulers was King Antiochus the First. You may know his name from Mount Nemrut, also in Adıyaman Province. There, on a high summit, he built a tomb sanctuary with giant stone heads of gods and kings. In his inscriptions, Antiochus claimed descent from both Persian kings and from Alexander the Great. It was a clever message. To the east and to the west, he presented himself as one of their own.

Samosata was the political centre of that small world. Here stood the royal palace, the court, and the administration of the kingdom. Rescue excavations on the mound uncovered remains from the royal period, including fine building fragments and mosaic floors. Even in pieces, they show a court that wanted to live in Greek style while keeping its eastern roots.

Commagene's independence did not last. In the first century after Christ, Rome absorbed the kingdom into its empire. Samosata did not disappear, however. It became an important Roman base on the Euphrates frontier. A Roman legion was stationed in the area, guarding the river against the east. The city remained a crossing point, a garrison, and a market for centuries.

Here is the first surprise. One of the sharpest comic writers of the ancient world was born in this city. His name was Lucian of Samosata, and he lived in the second century after Christ. He wrote in Greek, but he openly called himself a Syrian. His dialogues mock pompous philosophers, false prophets, and the gods themselves. He even wrote a story about a voyage to the Moon. Some readers today call it one of the earliest works of science fiction.

Samosata also gave its name to a figure in church history. Paul of Samosata became bishop of Antioch in the third century. His teachings about the nature of Christ were condemned by church councils, and he was removed from office. For later Christian writers, his name became a byword for heresy. So this one small river town produced both a famous satirist and a famous controversy.

In the medieval centuries, the city stood on a restless frontier. Byzantine armies, Arab dynasties, Crusader lords, and later Turkish rulers all held this crossing at different times. A fortress crowned the mound for much of this period. Each new power repaired the walls, reused the old stones, and added its own layer to the great heap of history beneath it.

Now the second surprise, and it is the reason the landscape looks the way it does. In the late twentieth century, Türkiye built the Atatürk Dam on the Euphrates. The dam created one of the largest reservoirs in the country. As the water rose, it covered fields, villages, and the old town of Samsat. The ancient mound of Samosata was also drowned. Before the flooding, archaeologists worked against the clock to record and rescue what they could.

The people of old Samsat were moved to a new town on higher ground nearby. That is the modern settlement you can visit today. Many finds from the excavations went to museums in the region, where you can see them in safety. Out on the reservoir, the exact place of the old city is now mostly water. When the level drops, some visitors report glimpses of the shoreline that once surrounded it.

So what do you do with a city you cannot see? You use the landscape. Look at the width of the water and imagine the river much narrower, running between banks. Picture the mound rising above it, with walls and towers on top. Then turn toward the hills. Somewhere beyond them is Mount Nemrut, where the kings of this city chose to be remembered forever.

Take a moment before you leave. Feel the wind off the water and listen to the small waves on the stones. Under that calm surface lie the palace floors of a forgotten kingdom, the streets where Lucian walked as a boy, and the stones of a frontier fortress. As you walk back toward the modern town, look at the ordinary houses and gardens. They are the newest layer of a very old place.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine this stop with a visit to Mount Nemrut.
Visit the regional museum to see rescued excavation finds.
Bring a windbreaker; wind off the reservoir can be strong.
Come in late afternoon for softer light over the water.
Carry water and sun protection along the open shoreline.
