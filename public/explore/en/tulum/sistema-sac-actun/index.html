The guide

The story

Lower yourself toward the water and feel the air change. It turns cool and damp, with a faint mineral smell, like wet stone after rain. The light from above falls in pale shafts and hits the surface. Below you, the water is so clear that it barely looks like water at all. It looks like glass laid over a stone floor, and that floor drops away into blue darkness.

You are standing at one doorway into Sistema Sac Actun. The name comes from Yucatec Maya, the language still spoken across this peninsula, and it means white cave. That fits. Much of the rock down here is pale limestone, and in the flashlight beams of divers it glows almost cream-white. What you see from the edge is only a small opening. Behind it runs one of the largest underwater cave networks known anywhere on Earth.

Here is the first surprise. Sac Actun is not one cave. It is a vast web of flooded passages that wind beneath the jungle, the highways and the towns around Tulum. For years, cave divers mapped it as separate systems. Then, in 2018, explorers working on a long-term research project confirmed a link between Sac Actun and the neighbouring Dos Ojos system. When two systems connect, the larger name absorbs the smaller one. With that link, Sac Actun became the longest known underwater cave system in the world, at well over three hundred kilometres of mapped passage.

To understand why this place exists, think about the ground under your feet. The Yucatán Peninsula is a huge flat shelf of limestone, built from the remains of ancient sea life. Limestone dissolves slowly in slightly acidic rainwater. Over a very long time, rain soaked down through the rock and carved channels and chambers. There are almost no rivers on the surface of this region. The rivers are here instead — underground, in the dark.

Now look closely at the formations if you can see them from where you stand. Stalactites hang from the ceilings, and stalagmites rise from the floors. These shapes grow only in air, drip by drip, as mineral-rich water falls. So how did they form in caves that are now completely flooded? The answer is the Ice Ages. Vast sheets of ice locked up so much of the world's water that sea levels dropped far lower than today. These caves were dry then. When the ice melted and the seas rose, the water table rose too, and the caves filled.

That flooding acted like a time capsule. In parts of the wider system, divers have found the bones of animals that disappeared long ago, including creatures related to elephants and giant ground sloths. In a deep chamber called Hoyo Negro, which means black hole, divers found the skeleton of a teenage girl. Researchers later dated her remains to roughly the end of the last Ice Age. She is one of the oldest and most complete human skeletons found in the Americas. Scientists believe she fell into the cave when it was still dry.

The water itself holds another secret. If divers swim deep enough near the coast, they pass through a strange shimmering layer. It is called a halocline. Fresh rainwater floats on top, and heavier salt water from the sea sits underneath. Where the two meet, the water blurs and wobbles, a little like heat rising off a road. Divers describe it as swimming through a mirage. You may not see it from the surface, but it is happening somewhere below you.

For the ancient Maya, these openings in the earth carried deep meaning. A cenote, the local word for a natural sinkhole filled with water, was often the only reliable source of fresh water. Many cenotes were also seen as sacred. In Maya belief, they could be doorways to Xibalba, the underworld, a realm of gods and the dead. Offerings and pottery have been found in cenotes across the peninsula. When you look down into the blue, you are looking at a place people treated with reverence for many centuries.

The entrances to this system have their own personalities. Some, like the well-known Gran Cenote near the road from Tulum toward Cobá, open into wide pools with wooden walkways and swimming areas. Others are narrow holes in the forest floor, almost invisible until you are beside them. Dos Ojos, which means two eyes, takes its name from two neighbouring pools that look like eyes from above. Each entrance gives a different view into the same hidden world.

Listen for a moment. Inside a cenote, sound behaves differently. Voices bounce off the rock and soften. Water drips somewhere you cannot see. Swallows and other birds often nest in the walls and dart across the opening. Small fish move in the shallows, and you may see turtles resting near the edges at some sites. Roots from the trees above sometimes hang down through the rock, reaching for the water like long ropes.

This place is beautiful, but it is also fragile. The aquifer, the underground body of water inside these caves, is the main source of fresh water for the region. Anything that goes into the ground can end up here. That includes sunscreen, insect spray and waste from fast-growing towns. In recent years, large construction projects in the area, including a new railway, have raised strong public concern among cave divers and scientists. Many sites ask visitors to shower before swimming. That small step genuinely helps protect the water.

A word about safety. Swimming and snorkelling in the open areas of a cenote is a common and joyful experience. Cave diving is something else entirely. It is one of the most demanding forms of diving, with no direct way to the surface. Divers train for years to do it safely. If the dark passages call to you, go only with certified local guides, and respect every rope and sign. They are there for a very good reason.

Before you leave, take one more look into the water. Notice the colour where the sunlight ends and the shadow begins. Somewhere beyond that line, the passages run on for kilometres, through rock shaped over thousands of years. As you walk back up toward the heat and the green noise of the jungle, remember what is under the path. The road, the trees, the town of Tulum — all of it sits on top of a hidden river.

Before you go

Practical tips

Shower before swimming to keep sunscreen out of the aquifer.
Go cave diving only with certified local cave guides.
Bring water shoes for slippery limestone steps and ladders.
Arrive early for calmer water and clearer light shafts.
Carry a waterproof bag for your phone and valuables.
