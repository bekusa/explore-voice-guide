The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the Caribbean breaks against pale limestone, a steady hush that never quite stops. The air tastes of salt. Underfoot, the path is dusty and warm, and somewhere close, a dry rustle in the grass tells you that you are not alone. Look down... an iguana, grey and patient, is watching you from a sun-baked stone. Ahead, low buildings of weathered rock sit at the edge of a cliff. Beyond them, the water shifts from green to deep blue. This is Tulum, a Maya city that chose the sea.

That choice is the first surprise. Most great Maya cities grew inland, among the forests and limestone plains of the Yucatán Peninsula. Tulum is different. It stands directly on the coast, on cliffs above the water. This makes it one of the very few Maya settlements built right at the edge of the sea. The people who lived here looked east every morning, toward the open horizon. Scholars believe the city may once have been called Zamá, a name often translated as dawn or sunrise.

The name you know today came later. In Yucatec Maya, the language still spoken across this region, the word tulum means wall or enclosure. And as you walked in, you passed through that wall. It runs along three sides of the city, built from stacked limestone. The fourth side needs no wall at all — the cliff and the sea protect it. Entry came through a few narrow gateways, so slim that people had to pass almost single file.

Why build a wall like this? The answer is not completely certain. It may have offered defence in an uneasy period of Maya history. It may also have separated the ruling and religious class from ordinary people. Many archaeologists think priests and leaders lived inside the enclosure, while most of the population lived in homes outside it. Those homes were likely made of wood and thatch, and they have not survived. So what you see is the stone heart of a larger community.

Tulum belongs to a late chapter of Maya civilisation. Its great age came in what experts call the Late Postclassic period, roughly the thirteenth to the fifteenth centuries. By then, the famous cities of the earlier Classic period had long been quiet. But life on this coast was busy. Tulum was a trading port. Canoes moved along the shore carrying goods such as cacao, which the Maya also used as money, along with obsidian, jade, and fine textiles.

Now turn toward the tallest building, standing above the cliff. This is El Castillo, Spanish for the castle. It was not a castle, of course. It was a temple and a ceremonial centre, raised on a platform and reached by a broad stairway. From the water, it would have been the first thing sailors saw. Some researchers suggest that small openings in the upper building worked as a kind of navigation signal. The idea is that a fire or light, seen through them, helped canoes find a safe gap in the reef offshore. It is an appealing theory, though not proven.

The reef itself is real, and it is close. A long barrier reef runs along this coast, and the line of white surf you may see offshore marks where waves break over it. For Maya traders, the reef was both a danger and a shelter. Inside it, the water is calmer. That calm water, and the small beach in the cove below El Castillo, explain much about why a city grew in exactly this spot.

Next, find the Temple of the Descending God. Look above its doorway. A carved figure is set there, upside down, with legs in the air and the head pointing toward the ground. Nobody knows exactly who this figure is. Some scholars link it to the setting sun or to the planet Venus. Others connect it to a bee god, because honey was an important product on this coast. Notice too that the walls of this small temple lean slightly. That tilt is intentional, not damage.

Nearby stands the Temple of the Frescoes, and here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. Inside, on the walls, painted murals still survive. They show deities and offerings in dark lines, with traces of the strong blue colour that the Maya made from a local plant and a type of clay. On the outer corners of the building, look for carved stucco faces. They are worn by centuries of wind and salt, but you can still trace eyes and noses in the stone.

The second surprise is that Tulum was still alive when Europeans arrived. In the early sixteenth century, Spanish ships sailed along this coast, and accounts from those voyages describe a large, walled town with white buildings. So this was not a ruin when outsiders first saw it. It was a working city. But within the following decades, disease and conquest emptied it. Over time the forest moved back in, and the buildings stood silent.

The outside world learned more about Tulum in the nineteenth century. The American writer John Lloyd Stephens and the English artist Frederick Catherwood travelled through the Yucatán in the early 1840s. Their book, with its detailed drawings, brought the city to a wide audience in Europe and North America. Today, the site is protected as part of a national park, and the paths are roped. Visitors cannot climb the buildings, which helps keep the old stone from wearing away.

As you walk, notice the small things. Iguanas sleep on steps that were laid by hand many centuries ago. Coatis, long-nosed relatives of the raccoon, sometimes cross the grass. The stone is pocked and rough, because this limestone formed from ancient sea life. Along this shoreline, sea turtles come ashore to nest in season, and parts of the beach may be closed to protect them. The city and the wild animals share this cliff quietly.

Before you leave, stand for a moment near the cliff edge, wherever the path allows. Look at El Castillo with the sea behind it. Then turn and look back toward the wall, toward the forest that pushes against it. People stood here and watched the same sunrise rise out of the water, and many years ago they named their city for it. Listen once more to the surf. Then follow the narrow path back through the gateway, walking out the way the old residents once did, single file.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to beat the heat and the largest crowds.
Bring a hat and water, since shade is scarce.
Pack a swimsuit if you plan to visit the beach cove.
Use reef-safe sunscreen to help protect the nearby reef.
Stay on roped paths; climbing the buildings is not allowed.
