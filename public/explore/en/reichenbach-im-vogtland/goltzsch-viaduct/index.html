The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look up. Above you, row upon row of red-brown brick climbs into the sky, arch stacked on arch, until the top seems to float above the valley. The air down here is cool and damp, and it smells of moss, wet stone, and the river running somewhere below. Then, faintly, you might hear it, a low rumble building in the distance. A train is crossing the bridge, just as trains have crossed it for more than a century and a half.

This is the Göltzsch Viaduct, near Reichenbach in the Vogtland, a hilly region in southwest Saxony. It carries the railway across the valley of the Göltzsch, a small river that cuts a deep trough through this landscape. The bridge is widely described as the largest brick bridge in the world. That is not a marketing slogan. By the sheer volume of brick in its piers and arches, nothing else built this way comes close. And it is still doing the job it was built for.

To understand why it exists, picture Germany in the 1840s. Railways were spreading fast, and towns and kingdoms competed to be connected. Saxony and Bavaria wanted a line linking Leipzig in the north with Hof and the Bavarian network in the south. The route had one stubborn problem. Between the hills of the Vogtland lay deep valleys, and the Göltzsch valley was the widest and deepest of them. A train cannot climb or drop steeply. It needed a level crossing, high in the air.

The railway company first tried an unusual approach. It announced a public design competition with a prize for the best solution. Many entries arrived. According to the usual account of the project, none of them was judged workable enough to build. The task of making the bridge real then fell largely to Johann Andreas Schubert, an engineering professor from Dresden. He is often credited with the structural calculations that made this enormous design possible, at a time when such calculations were still a young science.

Why brick, and not iron or cut stone? The answer is partly practical. The valley soil here is rich in clay, and clay makes brick. Brick could be produced locally, in great quantity, at a manageable cost. Iron bridges of this size were still untested, and quarrying and shaping enough stone would have been slow and expensive. So the builders chose a humble material and used it on a heroic scale. The figure most often quoted is around twenty-six million bricks.

Think about what that number means in human terms. Every one of those bricks was shaped, fired in a kiln, carried to the site, and laid by hand. Brickworks across the surrounding area worked to keep up with demand. Thousands of workers came to the valley during the building years. The work was hard and sometimes dangerous, with high scaffolding made of timber. Construction began in the mid-1840s, and the first trains rolled across in 1851, a remarkably short time for a structure of this size.

Now look at the shape of the bridge itself. It rises in four tiers of arches, one level resting on another, like a giant brick ladder laid on its side. The arches are not all the same. Near the middle, where the river runs, the lower levels open into wider spans. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The design responds to the ground below, which was not equally firm everywhere. The engineers adapted the arches to the conditions they found.

The height is the second thing that strikes most people. The track runs roughly seventy-eight metres above the valley floor, about the height of a twenty-five-storey building. The bridge stretches for more than half a kilometre from one side of the valley to the other. From below, you cannot see the trains on top, only the long straight line of the parapet against the sky. Walk a little way off and the full length comes into view.

Get closer to the piers and look at the surface. The brick is not one flat colour. You will see warm reds, darker browns, patches that are almost purple, and lighter, newer-looking sections. These differences show more than a century and a half of weather, soot from steam engines, and careful repair. Restoration work has continued over the decades to keep the structure sound. Put your hand on the brick, and you can feel how rough and solid it still is.

Here is a second surprise. This is not a museum piece standing quietly in retirement. It remains part of the working railway, and regional and freight trains still cross it today. Modern trains are much heavier than the small steam locomotives of the 1850s, yet the brick holds. That says a great deal about the quality of the original planning. The builders of the nineteenth century had no way to imagine today's traffic, but they built with a generous margin.

The viaduct also has a sister. A few kilometres away, a similar but smaller brick bridge, the Elster Viaduct, crosses another valley on the same line. The two were built in the same period and in the same spirit. Together they show how one railway project changed the look of a whole region. Today the Göltzsch Viaduct is a symbol of the Vogtland, and you will see its arches on local signs, postcards, and souvenirs.

The bridge sits between small communities, including the towns of Netzschkau and Mylau, and the land around it is green and quiet. There is a park area at its foot and paths that lead along the valley. Some visitors come for the engineering. Many others come simply to walk, picnic, or sit and watch the light change on the brick. In the late afternoon, the sun warms the colour of the arches to a deep, glowing red.

Before you leave, find a spot where you can see the whole length at once. Notice how the arches repeat, one after another, in a steady rhythm. Listen for the river below, and perhaps for the next train above. Millions of bricks, laid by thousands of hands, still carry people across this valley every day. As you walk away, look back once more... and see how the bridge settles into the hills, as if it has always belonged here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in late afternoon when sunlight warms the red brick.
Walk the valley paths to see the full length.
Wear sturdy shoes for damp, uneven ground below the arches.
Wait for a passing train for a sense of scale.
Combine your visit with the nearby Elster Viaduct.
