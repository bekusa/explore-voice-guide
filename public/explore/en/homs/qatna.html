The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. It is soft and pale, and it crumbles a little with each step. Around you, long earthen ridges rise from the flat farmland of the Homs plain. They look like natural hills at first. They are not. These grassy slopes are the remains of city walls, raised by hand nearly four thousand years ago. Listen for the wind moving across the open ground, and perhaps a dog barking from a nearby village. You are standing inside Qatna.

Today the site is known locally as Tell el-Mishrifeh. A tell is a mound built up over centuries, as people build new homes on the ruins of older ones. Qatna lies a short drive northeast of the city of Homs, in western Syria. In the Bronze Age, when this land was a crossroads of empires, Qatna was the capital of a powerful kingdom. Its kings traded, made alliances, and wrote letters to the great rulers of their day.

Look again at those ramparts. Together they form a vast enclosure, roughly square in outline, with gaps where the city gates once stood. The area inside is enormous — larger than many famous ancient cities of the same period. Moving this much earth was a huge public effort. It tells us that Qatna's rulers could command labour on a grand scale. The walls were defence, but they were also a statement of power to anyone approaching across the plain.

Qatna was at its height in the second millennium before the common era. That is the age archaeologists call the Middle and Late Bronze Age. The city sat on routes linking Mesopotamia, to the east, with the Mediterranean coast and Egypt, to the west and south. Goods, ideas, and armies all passed this way. Being in the middle of everything brought wealth. It also brought danger, because larger powers wanted control of these roads.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Qatna appears in the Amarna letters. These are clay tablets found in Egypt, part of the diplomatic mail of the pharaohs. A king of Qatna named Akizzi wrote to Egypt, asking for help as threats grew around him. So this quiet mound on the Syrian plain was once a voice in the correspondence of kings. Its name travelled hundreds of kilometres, pressed into clay with a reed stylus.

The first serious excavations here took place in the 1920s, led by a French archaeologist, Comte du Mesnil du Buisson. He identified the site as Qatna and uncovered parts of the great royal palace. For decades after that, the mound kept most of its secrets. Then, from the late 1990s, a joint mission of Syrian, Italian, and German teams returned. Their work changed what scholars knew about this kingdom.

The royal palace stood on high ground near the centre of the city. Archaeologists uncovered huge halls, thick walls of mud brick, and floors of plaster. Picture the building whole, with painted walls, wooden ceilings, and courtyards full of officials and servants. Fragments of wall painting found here show styles linked to the Aegean world, the islands and coasts around Greece. Artists, or at least artistic ideas, seem to have travelled a very long way to decorate these rooms.

Now for the discovery that made Qatna famous among archaeologists. In 2002, the German team found a passage beneath the palace. It led down to a royal tomb cut into the rock. Most ancient royal tombs in this region were robbed long ago. This one was largely intact. Inside lay the remains of many people, along with gold jewellery, carved ivory, fine stone vessels, and pottery. It was a rare, almost undisturbed view into the burial customs of a Bronze Age court.

At the entrance to the burial chambers, two seated statues of basalt, a dark volcanic stone, guarded the way. They show figures in long robes, their hands resting forward. Scholars believe they represent royal ancestors. Evidence in the chambers suggests that the living came back to share meals with the dead. Food offerings and vessels were found in the tomb. For the people of Qatna, the dead kings were not gone. They were still part of the family, still honoured, still fed.

The palace also gave up clay tablets written in cuneiform, the wedge-shaped script used across the ancient Near East. These records show the language of administration and the names of people and goods. They also help historians date the final chapter of the city. That ending came in the fourteenth century before the common era, when the Hittites, a great power from Anatolia in modern Turkey, swept south. The palace was burned. Ironically, fire can bake clay tablets hard, and that helped preserve them.

After the destruction, Qatna never again ruled a kingdom. Smaller communities lived on the mound in later periods. In modern times, a village grew inside the ancient walls. Houses stood where palace officials once walked. Later, the village moved to make way for excavation, and its name, Mishrifeh, stayed with the site. The recent history of Syria has also touched this place, and access and conditions can change, so local guidance matters.

As you walk, notice how little stands upright. This is not a site of tall columns or marble temples. Mud brick returns to earth when the roof is gone. What remains is shape — the lines of walls in the soil, the rise of the ramparts, the cut edges of excavation trenches. It asks a little imagination. But every slope you see was shaped by people, and every trench marks a question that archaeologists came here to answer.

Before you leave, climb a little way up one of the ramparts if it is safe, and turn slowly. Look across the enclosure, then out to the fields and the distant line of the horizon. This is the view a guard might have had, watching the roads for traders or for armies. The wind is the same. The light on the plain is the same. And somewhere beneath your feet, more of Qatna is still waiting in the quiet soil.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current local safety guidance before planning your visit.
Wear sturdy shoes for soft, uneven earthen ground.
Bring water and sun protection; shade is very limited.
Go with a local guide who knows the excavation areas.
Visit early or late for softer light on the ramparts.
