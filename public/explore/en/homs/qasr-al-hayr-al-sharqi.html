The guide

The story

The wind out here carries fine grit. It settles in the corners of your eyes and on the back of your hands. Around you the steppe runs flat and pale in every direction, the colour of dry bread. Then, rising out of that emptiness, two walled enclosures appear, their round towers glowing honey-gold in the sun. Stand still for a moment. Listen. There is almost nothing to hear except the wind, and perhaps a bird. Thirteen centuries ago, this same place was full of voices.

You are at Qasr al-Hayr al-Sharqi, in the Syrian desert, roughly a hundred kilometres northeast of Palmyra. The name means, loosely, the eastern palace of the walled enclosure. It is eastern in comparison with its sister site, Qasr al-Hayr al-Gharbi, the western one, which lies far to the southwest. Both belong to the Umayyads. They were the first great dynasty of the Islamic caliphate, and they ruled from Damascus from the middle of the seventh century until 750.

An inscription recorded at this site connects its construction to the reign of Caliph Hisham ibn Abd al-Malik, around 728 or 729 of the Common Era. Hisham was a careful, practical ruler. He was known for his interest in agriculture and estates. That detail matters here. The Umayyad princes did not see the desert as empty. They saw land that could be farmed, governed, and enjoyed, if only someone brought the water.

And water is the quiet miracle of this place. The word hayr refers to a large walled area, and here that wall once enclosed a huge stretch of land, many times bigger than the buildings in front of you. Inside it were gardens and fields. Channels carried water from springs a long way off, across the steppe, to feed them. Today you have to imagine green crops where now there is only gravel and thorn.

Look first at the larger of the two enclosures. It is roughly 160 metres on each side, and its walls are strengthened by a regular rhythm of rounded towers. This was not a single palace. Archaeologists usually describe it as something closer to a small town, with houses arranged around courtyards, a mosque, and space for workshops. Excavators found an olive press inside, a reminder that this was a working settlement and not only a royal retreat.

Now turn to the smaller enclosure, about seventy metres square. This is the one most people photograph, and with good reason. Its gateway is the finest surviving piece of the whole site. Here is the first surprise. Above the entrance, look for a projecting box set high on the wall. That box is a machicolation, an opening through which defenders could drop things on attackers below. It is often cited as one of the earliest examples of this feature in Islamic architecture, centuries before it became common on castles in Europe.

Stay with that gateway for a moment. The lower walls are built of cut stone, but higher up the builders used fired brick. They laid it in decorative patterns, and some surfaces once carried carved stucco, a hard plaster that could be shaped like lace. Notice how the gate is flanked by half-round towers. The effect is half fortress and half stage set. It was meant to impress anyone who arrived across the desert.

Why build such an elaborate place so far from any city? Historians still discuss this. These desert complexes may have served several purposes at once. They were agricultural estates. They were stopping points on the routes across the steppe. They were also places where a caliph or prince could meet the Bedouin tribes, whose loyalty the Umayyads needed. A grand gate, fresh water, and green gardens in the middle of the steppe sent a clear political message.

Here is the second surprise. The story of Qasr al-Hayr al-Sharqi did not end with the Umayyads. After the Abbasids took power in 750, the site continued to be lived in. Excavations showed occupation for several more centuries, with repairs, rebuilding, and changes in how the space was used. People seem to have abandoned it only much later, probably around the fourteenth century, when the wider region suffered from war and decline.

Much of what we know comes from excavations led by the art historian Oleg Grabar in the 1960s and 1970s. His team mapped the enclosures, studied the pottery and coins, and traced the long life of the settlement. Their work changed how scholars understood these desert sites. It showed that they were not lonely pleasure houses, but part of a larger system of land, water, and power.

It is worth knowing what happened to the western sister site. The decorated facade of Qasr al-Hayr al-Gharbi was taken apart and rebuilt in Damascus, where it now forms the entrance of the National Museum. So if you travel on to the capital, you can walk through that Umayyad gateway in the middle of the city. Here, by contrast, the eastern gate still stands where its builders placed it, in the open air and the wind.

The recent past has been hard on this landscape. The years of conflict in Syria brought damage and looting to many archaeological sites in the desert, and this region was not spared. As you walk, you may see holes in the ground or disturbed stones that are not ancient. Treat what remains with care. Do not climb on weak walls, and do not take anything away, not even a small piece of brick.

Before you leave, walk a little way from the walls and turn around. See how the two enclosures sit low against the huge sky, and how the towers cast long shadows across the gravel. Feel the heat coming up from the ground. Somewhere under your feet are the old water channels and the edges of fields that once fed a town. As you walk back to the road, the steppe will look empty again... but now you know it never really was.

Practical tips

Check current security conditions before travelling into the desert.
Travel with an experienced local driver or licensed guide.
Carry plenty of water, sun protection, and a hat.
Arrive early morning for softer light and cooler air.
Stay off fragile walls and leave every fragment in place.
