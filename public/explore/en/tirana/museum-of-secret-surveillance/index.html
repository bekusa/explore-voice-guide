The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of central Tirana hums just beyond the garden wall. Cars, scooters, someone calling across the street. Now look at the building in front of you. Its walls are wrapped in a thick coat of green climbing leaves, so dense in places that the plaster almost disappears. It looks soft, almost gentle. For decades, though, this quiet house was one of the most feared addresses in Albania.

Locals call it the House of Leaves, Shtëpia me Gjethe in Albanian. The name comes from that green coat of creepers on the facade. Today it is the Museum of Secret Surveillance. Inside, the museum tells a single, uncomfortable story. It shows how a state watched, listened to, and recorded its own citizens. It also shows how ordinary lives were bent and broken by that watching.

The building did not begin with secrets. It went up in the early 1930s as a private medical clinic, a place where babies were born. Picture that for a second. The rooms where you will soon see listening equipment were once filled with new mothers and the first cries of infants. That early, hopeful chapter makes everything that came later feel even heavier.

The Second World War changed the building's purpose. During the wartime occupation of Albania, foreign forces took over the house. After the war ended, a new power moved in. Albania became a communist state under Enver Hoxha, who ruled the country from the mid-1940s until his death in 1985. His regime became one of the most closed and isolated in Europe. And this house became part of its nervous system.

The state security service was called the Sigurimi. Its job was to find enemies of the regime, real or imagined. In this building, specialists worked on what they called operational technique. In plain words, that meant surveillance. They built and tested listening devices. They monitored telephone lines. They studied recordings. Behind these leafy walls, the regime learned what people said in their kitchens, their offices, and their bedrooms.

As you move through the rooms, notice how ordinary much of the equipment looks. Reel-to-reel tape recorders. Headphones. Telephones. Microphones small enough to hide inside a wall, a light fitting, or a piece of furniture. Nothing here looks like a spy film. It looks like an old radio repair shop. That is one of the quiet surprises of this museum. Fear did not need dramatic machines. It needed patience, cables, and people willing to listen.

The museum also shows the human side of surveillance. You will see files, photographs, and documents connected to real people. Some were writers. Some were workers. Some were simply unlucky enough to say the wrong thing to the wrong person. Under the regime, a careless joke could lead to prison or a labour camp. Families of the accused often suffered too, losing jobs, homes, or the right to study.

Here is something many visitors miss. Surveillance in Albania did not depend only on hidden microphones. It depended on neighbours, colleagues, and sometimes relatives. Informers reported on the people around them. The museum asks you to think about that web of trust and suspicion. Who could you speak to freely? In many homes, the honest answer was... almost no one. Parents learned to stay silent even in front of their own children.

Pay attention to the design of the exhibition itself. The curators kept much of the building's original structure. Narrow corridors, small rooms, closed doors. Moving from one room to the next can feel a little tight, a little watched. That feeling is deliberate. The space carries its own history, and the museum lets the architecture do part of the storytelling.

The museum opened its doors in 2017, after years of debate in Albania about how to face the communist past. Many former Sigurimi files remain sensitive, and some families are still searching for answers about relatives who disappeared. In 2020, the museum received the Council of Europe Museum Prize. That award recognises museums that help people understand human rights and democratic values. It was a sign that this small house in Tirana had something to say to all of Europe.

Take a moment in one of the quieter rooms. Many visitors find that the silence changes as they walk through. At the entrance, it feels like the calm of any museum. Deeper inside, the silence feels more like caution. You may catch yourself lowering your voice. That reaction is worth noticing. It shows how quickly a space can teach the body to be careful, even decades after the listening stopped.

When you step back outside, look at the city around you. Tirana today is loud and colourful. Painted apartment blocks, open cafes, people talking freely on their phones. The communist regime fell in the early 1990s, and the country has changed enormously since then. Yet this house stands in the middle of that new life, a reminder of how recent the old one was. Many of the people walking past you lived through those years.

Before you leave, turn once more toward the facade. The leaves move a little in the wind. Birds sometimes shelter in them. It is easy to see only a pretty green house in the heart of the capital. Now you know what those walls once held. As you walk back into the noise of the street, listen to the conversations around you... and notice how freely they flow.
