The guide

The story

Listen first. Under your feet, the stone of the square is warm, slightly uneven, and made of many colours. Around you, scooters hum on the ring road, pigeons lift off in a single rush, and somewhere a café radio plays. Then lift your eyes. Above the open plaza, a bronze rider sits on a horse that seems frozen mid-stride. This is the Skanderbeg Monument, in the heart of Tirana. The man on the horse is the most important figure in Albanian national memory.

His real name was Gjergj Kastrioti. He was born into a noble Albanian family in the fifteenth century, a time when the Ottoman Empire was expanding across the Balkans. As a young man he was taken into Ottoman service. He trained as a soldier and rose to command. The Ottomans gave him a new name: Iskender Bey, meaning Lord Alexander, after Alexander the Great. In Albanian, that became Skanderbeg. The name of a foreign title became the name of a national hero.

In 1443, during a battle in the Balkans, Skanderbeg left the Ottoman army. He returned to Albanian lands and took control of the fortress of Krujë, a mountain town north of here. There, according to tradition, he raised a red banner with a black double-headed eagle. That same eagle is on the Albanian flag today. You can probably see it flying somewhere around this square right now — a direct line from his banner to the modern state.

For roughly a quarter of a century, Skanderbeg resisted the Ottoman Empire, one of the strongest powers of its age. He united rival Albanian noble families into an alliance. He used the mountains well, with fast cavalry, surprise attacks, and fortresses that held against long sieges. Again and again, much larger Ottoman armies came. Again and again, they failed to break him. News of his campaigns travelled across Europe, and the Pope praised him as a defender of Christendom.

Skanderbeg did not die in battle. He died of illness in 1468, in the town of Lezhë, near the coast. Within about ten years of his death, most Albanian fortresses had fallen. The Ottoman period in Albania then lasted for more than four centuries. But his story did not end. Through folk songs, poems, and later through history books, Skanderbeg became the symbol of Albanian resistance and identity.

Now look closely at the statue itself. It was unveiled in 1968, to mark five hundred years since his death. The main sculptor was Odhise Paskali, one of Albania's best-known twentieth-century artists, working with fellow Albanian sculptors. Notice the rider's posture. He sits upright and calm, and he looks out across the city. The horse is full of movement, but the man is still. That contrast gives the monument its strength — power held under control.

Look at the head of the figure. Skanderbeg is shown wearing a helmet with the head of a goat on top, with curved horns. This detail comes from a real helmet associated with him. It is kept today in a museum in Vienna, together with a sword linked to him. Many visitors photograph the statue and never notice the goat. Walk around the base slowly, and the horns become clear against the sky.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The year 1968 was deep in the communist period. Albania was ruled by Enver Hoxha, and the country was one of the most isolated in Europe. Yet the regime chose to honour a medieval nobleman who fought under a Christian banner. Why? Because Skanderbeg stood for unity and independence against foreign powers. Those were ideas the government wanted to promote. The hero served the state's own message.

And here is a second surprise. For years, this square also held a large statue of Enver Hoxha himself. In February 1991, as the communist system collapsed, crowds gathered here and pulled that statue down. It was one of the defining images of the end of the regime. Skanderbeg stayed. The dictator was removed, but the medieval hero kept his place. Standing here, you are in the space where modern Albania turned a page.

The square around you has changed a lot in recent years. For decades it was a wide space with heavy traffic and open lawns. In the 2010s it was redesigned as a large pedestrian plaza, and it reopened in 2017. The new paving uses stone brought from different regions of Albania. So the colours under your feet are a kind of map of the country. Look down as you walk — the shades change from grey to pink to cream.

Turn slowly and look at the buildings that frame the monument. On one side stands the National History Museum, with a huge mosaic above its entrance showing Albanians through the ages. Nearby is the small Et'hem Bey Mosque, with its painted interior, and beside it the old Clock Tower. Across the square is the Palace of Culture, home to the national opera and ballet. Ottoman, communist, and contemporary layers all meet in one view.

Skanderbeg is not only honoured here. Statues of him stand in other Albanian towns, and in cities abroad where Albanian communities live, including Rome. In Krujë, his old stronghold, there is a museum dedicated to him inside the restored castle. If you have time during your trip, it is a rewarding day out. Seeing the mountains he defended will help you understand why the story of his resistance still matters so much to people here.

Before you move on, take one more look at the rider. Notice how small the people around the base appear, sitting on the steps, taking photos, eating ice cream. Families meet here. Students cross on their way to class. Couples stop for a picture. The monument is not behind a fence. It is part of daily life. As you walk away, listen again to the city around you — and glance back once at the goat-horned helmet, still looking out over Tirana.

Practical tips
