The guide

The story

Put your hand on the concrete. It is warm in the afternoon sun, rough in some places and smooth in others. Above you, the slope rises at a steep angle toward the sky. People are climbing it, step by step, on a staircase that runs straight up the face of the building. This is the Pyramid of Tirana, one of the strangest buildings in the Balkans, and few buildings anywhere have changed their meaning so many times.

Look at the overall shape first. This is not a smooth Egyptian pyramid. It is closer to a star or a spreading flower, with wings of concrete that slope down from a central peak. Glass fills the gaps between the wings. From the air, the plan looks almost like an emblem. Up close, it feels heavy and low. It sits on the ground like something that landed here and decided to stay.

The building opened in 1988 as a museum for one man. That man was Enver Hoxha, the leader who ruled Albania for about four decades after the Second World War. Under his rule, Albania became one of the most isolated countries on Earth. It cut ties with Yugoslavia, then the Soviet Union, then China. Hoxha died in 1985. Three years later, this monument to his life and legacy opened on the city's main boulevard.

Here is the first surprise. The design team included Hoxha's own daughter, Pranvera Hoxha, an architect, together with her husband and other colleagues. So the museum to the dictator was shaped, in part, by his family. Inside, visitors once walked past displays about his life and his ideas. The building was meant to last for generations. In the end, the museum lasted only a few short years.

In the early 1990s, the communist system in Albania collapsed. The country opened to the world, quickly and painfully. The exhibits about Hoxha came out. The building lost its purpose, and for many years nobody quite knew what to do with it. Parts of it served as a conference and exhibition space. Later, broadcasters and other tenants moved into sections of the structure.

During the Kosovo conflict in 1999, the building took on a very different role. It served as a base for NATO operations, at a time when large numbers of refugees crossed from Kosovo into Albania. Think about that for a moment. A shrine to a leader who saw the West as an enemy became a working space for a Western military alliance. Few buildings in Europe carry such a sharp reversal inside their walls.

Then came the long years of neglect. The marble that once covered the slopes cracked and fell away. Windows broke. Graffiti spread across the concrete. And here is the second surprise — the local children found a use for it. For years, young people climbed the steep slopes and slid back down. For a whole generation in Tirana, the Pyramid was less a political symbol and more a giant, slightly dangerous playground.

As the building decayed, the city argued about its future. Some people wanted it demolished. For them, it was a painful reminder of dictatorship and fear. Others wanted it kept. For them, it was part of the city's story, and erasing it would erase a lesson. At one point, there was a plan to tear it down and build a new parliament on the site. That plan did not go ahead, and the debate continued for years.

The answer, in the end, was transformation. The Dutch architecture firm MVRDV led a major renovation, and the building reopened in 2023. The designers kept the original concrete shape. But they added the staircases you see now, running up the sloping faces, so anyone can climb to the top safely. In a way, they made official what the children of Tirana had been doing for decades.

Look around the base of the building. You will see colourful boxes scattered around it, almost like toy blocks. These hold rooms for classes, cafés and small workshops. Today the Pyramid is home to a technology and education centre for young people, where teenagers learn coding, design, robotics and other digital skills. A building made to honour one man's authority now belongs, in a very real sense, to the city's young.

If you feel like it, climb the stairs. The steps are steep, so take your time and watch your footing. From the top, you look out over the main boulevard and the rooftops of central Tirana. You can see the mountains rising beyond the city on a clear day. The view is the reward, but the act of climbing matters too. Standing on top of this building is a small, quiet statement about how much has changed.

Nearby, in the green space around the Pyramid, look for the Peace Bell. It was made from cartridge cases collected by Albanian children after the unrest of 1997, when the country fell into chaos and many weapons were looted. Metal from bullets became a bell for peace. It fits well beside this building, which has also turned from one meaning into its opposite.

Before you leave, step back from the slopes and look at the whole structure once more. Notice the old grey concrete next to the bright new boxes. Notice the people on the stairs, small against the angled walls. Listen to the traffic on the boulevard and the voices of students coming and going. This is a building that has been a shrine, a ruin, a playground and a school. As you walk away, look back once. It is still changing.
