The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind you, the traffic of Skanderbeg Square circles the wide stone plaza. In front of you is a small building of pale stone with a single slender minaret. Its scale is modest, almost domestic. After the open emptiness of the square, it feels like stepping out of the wind and into a sheltered corner. This is the Et'hem Bey Mosque, one of the oldest buildings still standing in the centre of Tirana.

The first thing many visitors notice is how small it is. Tirana's government ministries, the opera house and the tall modern towers around the square all seem to lean over it. Yet this little mosque is older than almost everything you can see from here. When it was built, Tirana was a provincial Ottoman town. It had a market, a few mosques, and roads that led out to the hills. The capital city around you came much later.

The story of the mosque belongs to one family. Construction began in the late eighteenth century under Molla Bey, a member of a local landowning family connected to the early growth of Tirana. He did not live to see the work completed. His son, Haxhi Et'hem Bey, finished the building in the early nineteenth century. The mosque carries his name today. The title Haxhi, by the way, means that he had made the pilgrimage to Mecca.

Look at the front of the building. A covered porch, or portico, wraps around the prayer hall. It rests on slim columns and arches. In Ottoman mosques of this period, the portico was a waiting space. People gathered here before prayer, sat in the shade, talked, and left their shoes. Even now, this shaded walkway feels like a threshold between the noise of the city and the quiet inside.

Now raise your eyes to the walls under the portico. Here is the first surprise. They are covered with painted scenes — trees, buildings, bridges and waterfalls, in soft greens, blues and ochres. Islamic religious art usually avoids images of living beings, and many mosques use only geometry and calligraphy. Landscapes like these are much less common. Here, the painters filled the walls with an imagined world of nature and architecture.

Why landscapes? Art historians link this style to a wider fashion in the late Ottoman world. Wealthy patrons from Istanbul to the Balkans decorated houses and mosques with painted views of gardens, palaces and distant cities. These scenes often showed a peaceful, ordered paradise. In a small town like Tirana, the paintings also showed something else. They showed that this family was connected to the tastes of the wider empire.

If you go inside, the same painted world continues. The interior is one compact room under a single dome. Light comes in through the windows and falls on carpets and decorated walls. The dome is not huge, but it covers the whole hall. There are no columns blocking the view. Because the space is so small, you can see almost every painted detail from one spot. It feels less like a monument and more like a richly decorated room.

Take a moment to notice the sound in here. The traffic outside becomes a low hum. Footsteps on the carpet make almost no noise. Many visitors lower their voices without thinking. This is still a working mosque. Local people come here to pray, especially at Friday midday prayers. So please move quietly, and give space to anyone who is praying.

Now for the darker chapter, and the second surprise. In 1967, Albania's communist government declared the country an atheist state. Religious practice was banned. Across Albania, many churches and mosques were destroyed or turned into warehouses, cinemas and sports halls. The Et'hem Bey Mosque was closed. But it was not demolished. Its age and its artistic value helped to protect it, and it was kept as a cultural monument.

Then came January 1991. The communist system was weakening, and change was in the air. Thousands of people gathered here and entered the mosque to pray, even though permission was unclear and the regime had not yet fallen. For many Albanians, this gathering was not only about religion. It was a public act of courage. People remember it as one of the moments that showed the old system was ending.

After you leave, turn toward the tall stone tower beside the mosque. This is the Clock Tower of Tirana. It was also linked to Haxhi Et'hem Bey, and it was built in the early nineteenth century. For a long time, it was one of the tallest structures in the town. Its bell marked the hours for merchants and families before most people had their own clocks. Together, the mosque and the tower form a small group from the old Ottoman centre.

The mosque has been restored in recent years. Craftsmen cleaned and repaired the stonework and the painted surfaces. As a result, the colours on the walls are brighter than they were a generation ago. Some visitors find the fresh look surprising. Others like being able to see the paintings clearly. Either way, the building is in good condition and is ready for the next long chapter of its life.

Before you walk on, stand at the edge of the portico and look back across the square. On one side, you see the family mosque of an Ottoman town. Further away, you see the large buildings of a twentieth century capital. Around the edges are cafés, glass towers and the steady flow of modern Tirana. All of these layers fit into one view. As you step back into the noise, listen for the clock in the tower, and notice how this small building keeps its place among them.

Practical tips

Wear clothing that covers shoulders and knees.
