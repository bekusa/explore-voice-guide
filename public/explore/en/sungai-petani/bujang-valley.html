The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the trees around Merbok, the air is warm and heavy. Insects buzz in the undergrowth, and somewhere a bird repeats the same two notes. Underfoot there is grass, damp earth, and then something harder: low platforms of red-brown stone, square and deliberate, laid by human hands more than a thousand years ago. Above you, to the north, the long green shape of Gunung Jerai, also called Kedah Peak, rises out of the plain. You are standing in Bujang Valley.

The name sounds like one valley, but it is really a wide landscape. It stretches across southern Kedah, between the slopes of Gunung Jerai and the rivers that run toward the Strait of Malacca. Archaeologists have identified dozens of ancient sites here. Many are the foundations of temples, known in Malay as candi. Others are workshops, river landings, and spots where pottery and glass turn up in the soil. Together they make this one of the most important archaeological areas in Malaysia.

So why here? Look at the mountain again. For sailors crossing the Bay of Bengal from India, Gunung Jerai was a landmark visible far out at sea. It told them the coast was close. Ships came in on the monsoon winds, then waited months for the winds to turn before sailing on. A safe harbour with fresh water, timber, and goods to trade became a natural meeting point between the Indian Ocean and the routes toward China.

That meeting point grew into a trading kingdom. Old Indian texts in Tamil and Sanskrit mention a place called Kadaram or Kataha, and many historians link that name to ancient Kedah. Merchants came from South India, and later from Arabia and Persia. Chinese ships arrived too. With the goods came ideas — Hindu and Buddhist religion, Sanskrit writing, and new ways of building in stone. Local communities took these ideas and made something of their own.

Here is the first surprise. The temples you see were not always the whole story. The stone and brick platforms were only the base. Archaeologists believe that many of the upper parts were made of wood, with roofs of tiles or thatch. In a tropical climate, wood does not survive for centuries. What remains is the skeleton — the plinth, the stairs, the square sanctuary floor. You need imagination to complete the building.

Take a close look at the materials. Much of the stonework is laterite, a rusty, porous rock that forms in tropical soils. You can see small holes and pits in its surface, almost like a sponge. It is soft when first cut and hardens in the air, which made it practical for builders. Alongside the laterite you will find fired brick and smooth river stones, carried up from the streams nearby.

The best-known temple here is Candi Bukit Batu Pahat, a name that means roughly the Temple of the Hill of Chiselled Stone. It sits beside a small stream on the slope near the museum. Its stone base was carefully reconstructed after excavation. Look at the square inner chamber and the steps that lead toward it. Small pits under such shrines sometimes held offering caskets — little containers with precious items placed at the heart of the building.

The second surprise is much older. A few kilometres away, at a site called Sungai Batu, researchers have found brick structures, a riverside jetty, and evidence of iron smelting on a large scale. Some dates from this site reach back several centuries before Christ, long before the temples around you. The research team has argued that this makes Bujang Valley one of the oldest centres of civilisation in Southeast Asia. Other scholars still debate the dates, but the iron industry itself is clear.

Think about what that means. Iron ore from this land was smelted in clay furnaces, and the iron may have travelled across the sea as a trade good. Pieces of tuyere — the clay pipes that fed air into the furnace — and lumps of slag have been found in great numbers. Before the temples, before the Sanskrit inscriptions, this was already a place of industry and exchange.

The modern rediscovery came slowly. In the nineteenth century, a British officer named James Low recorded ancient stones and inscriptions in the region. In the 1930s, the archaeologist Horace Quaritch Wales excavated many sites and made the area known to scholars. Since then, Malaysian archaeologists have taken the lead, and new finds continue. The story of Bujang Valley is still being written in the soil.

The museum here gathers many of those finds. Inside you can see Chinese ceramics, glass from the Middle East, beads in many colours, stone carvings, and fragments of statues. Some pieces carry Hindu symbols, others Buddhist ones. Seeing them side by side shows how many worlds met on this coast. Spend a little time with the beads. They are small, but they travelled very far.

The valley is also fragile. Much of the land around the sites is farmland and plantation, and some ancient remains have been lost to development. In 2013, the destruction of one old temple base by a housing project caused a national outcry. It reminded many Malaysians that these quiet foundations are not ordinary ruins. They are part of the country's earliest written and built history, and they cannot be replaced.

Walk slowly as you leave. Look at the colour of the laterite in the afternoon light, the way it turns from brown to deep orange. Listen to the stream beside the temple base, still running where people once washed before prayer. Then look up at Gunung Jerai one more time. Sailors watched that same mountain from the deck of a ship, centuries ago. Now it watches you, as you walk back through the trees.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the museum to understand the scattered sites.
Visit in the morning before the tropical heat builds.
Bring insect repellent for the shaded temple paths.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone and damp grass.
Plan a separate trip to the Sungai Batu site.
