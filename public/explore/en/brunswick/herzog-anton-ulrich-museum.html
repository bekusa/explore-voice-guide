The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go in. The traffic on the street outside softens behind the trees of the little park. Ahead of you rises a long, pale facade of stone, with tall arched windows and a heavy, confident entrance. It looks like a palace. In a sense it is one, a palace built for pictures. Inside, the light falls from above, the floors are quiet, and some of the finest Dutch paintings in the world wait in calm, uncrowded rooms.

This is the Herzog Anton Ulrich Museum, and it carries a surprise in its age. The museum opened to the public in 1754. That makes it one of the oldest public museums on the European continent. It is older than the Louvre in Paris, which opened its doors to ordinary visitors only in the 1790s. Many people walk past this building without knowing that. Brunswick was showing art to its citizens before the French Revolution had even begun.

The museum carries the name of a duke, Anton Ulrich of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel. He lived in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, and he loved to collect. He bought paintings, porcelain, small sculptures, carved gems and curious objects of every kind. He gathered them at his pleasure palace at Salzdahlum, not far from here. That palace no longer stands. But much of what he collected survived, and it formed the heart of what you will see today.

It was a later duke, Charles the First, who took the bold step. In 1754 he opened the family treasures as a cabinet of art and natural curiosities. In that age a collection like this usually belonged to a court, and only guests of the duke could see it. Here, the idea was different. The collection was a source of pride for the whole duchy. People could come, look and learn. That idea is the reason this place matters beyond its walls.

The building in front of you is much younger than the collection. It went up in the late nineteenth century, in the 1880s, in a grand Neo-Renaissance style. That means the architects borrowed the look of Italian palaces from the fifteen hundreds: symmetry, columns, rounded arches and a strong horizontal line. In the early twenty-first century the museum closed for a long renovation and gained a modern extension. It reopened in 2016, and the old halls now sit beside clean, contemporary spaces.

When you walk through the galleries, notice how the old and new parts meet. The historic rooms have deep wall colours, gilded frames and high ceilings. The newer sections feel lighter and more open. The renovation also brought back the top-lit galleries, so daylight comes down from the roof onto the paintings. On a bright day the colours look warm and natural. On a grey northern German afternoon, the rooms take on a soft, even glow.

Now to the pictures that bring many visitors here. The museum holds one of the most important collections of Dutch and Flemish painting outside the Netherlands and Belgium. Among the treasures is a work by Johannes Vermeer, the master of quiet interiors from the city of Delft. It is called The Girl with the Wine Glass. Only around three dozen paintings by Vermeer survive anywhere in the world, so to stand in front of one is a rare thing.

Look closely at that canvas. A young woman sits at a table, turned toward you, with a glass in her hand. A man leans over her with a knowing look. In the background, another figure rests his head in his hand. Light comes in through a coloured window, and the scene carries a quiet tension. Is this a moment of courtship, or a warning about wine and temptation? Vermeer does not answer. He leaves the question to you.

Not far away hangs a late work by Rembrandt, known as the Family Portrait. A mother, a father and their children are gathered together, wrapped in deep reds and warm golds. Rembrandt painted it near the end of his life. By then his brushwork had become loose and bold. Step close, and you will see thick paint laid on almost roughly. Step back, and the faces come together with surprising tenderness. Try both distances. The painting changes as you move.

The Italian rooms hold another quiet surprise. There is a painting that has long been linked to Giorgione, the mysterious Venetian painter of the early sixteenth century. It shows a man, often read as the artist himself in the role of David, the young hero from the Bible. Very little is known about Giorgione's life, and very few works can be firmly given to him. So this face, looking out from the dark background, carries an air of mystery that suits its painter well.

Beyond the paintings, look for the smaller treasures. One of the most remarkable is a vessel from the ancient Roman world, often called the Mantuan Onyx Vessel. It was carved from a single piece of layered stone. The carver cut through the pale and dark layers to make figures stand out in relief, like a cameo. Imagine the patience it took, centuries ago, to shape something so delicate without breaking it. It is easy to walk past. Do not.

The museum also keeps a very large collection of prints and drawings, far too delicate to show all at once. These works are shown in changing exhibitions, so each visit can bring something new. And the museum's reach extends across the city. Its medieval collection is displayed in Dankwarderode Castle, near the old cathedral and the bronze lion that stands for Brunswick. If you have time, that is a natural next stop on your walk.

Before you leave, take one last look at the facade from the edge of the park. Think of the people who first came to see these collections, more than 250 years ago, in powdered wigs and buckled shoes. They looked at some of the same paintings you have just seen. The frames have changed, the building has changed, and the city outside has changed many times. But the girl with the wine glass is still waiting at her table, and the light still falls on her in the same quiet way.
