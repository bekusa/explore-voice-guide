The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere below you, waves break on a pebble shore. Above you, wet leaves drip after the morning rain. The air is warm and heavy, and it smells of damp earth, cut grass, and something sweet you cannot quite name. You are standing on a green hillside that drops straight into the Black Sea. Almost every plant around you began its life on another continent.

This is the Batumi Botanical Garden. It sits on a headland called Mtsvane Kontskhi, which means Green Cape in Georgian. The garden lies a short drive north of Batumi's city centre, along the coast road. It covers more than a hundred hectares of steep slopes, ravines, and terraces. The land climbs from the edge of the sea to wooded ridges high above. Wherever you stand, it is easy to forget that a busy port city is only a few kilometres away.

The garden opened in 1912. Its founder was Andrey Krasnov, a Russian botanist and geographer who had travelled widely in East Asia. Krasnov saw something special in this corner of Georgia. The climate here is humid and subtropical. Winters are mild, summers are warm, and rain falls generously through much of the year. To a botanist, this coast was a rare gift — a place where plants from Japan, China, and the southern hemisphere might actually thrive in open ground.

Krasnov's idea was not simply to collect beautiful plants. He wanted to arrange them by geography. So the garden is divided into sections, and each one represents a different region of the world. As you walk, you move from East Asia to the Himalayas, from North America to Mexico, from Australia and New Zealand to South America and the Mediterranean. There is also a section for the native plants of the Caucasus. In one long afternoon, you can cross most of the planet on foot.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Andrey Krasnov is buried inside the garden he created. He died only a few years after it opened, and his grave now rests among the trees on the Green Cape. It is a quiet spot, easy to pass without noticing. If you find it, take a moment there. Very few founders stay this close to their life's work.

The East Asian section is often the one people remember most. Here the paths wind past bamboo groves, camellias, magnolias, and Japanese maples. Bamboo stems can grow very tall in this damp climate, and they knock gently against each other in the wind. That hollow clicking sound is one of the most distinctive noises in the whole garden. Stop for a few seconds inside a grove and listen. The temperature often feels a little cooler in the shade.

The tea plants nearby carry another story. This stretch of coast became an important tea-growing region in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Botanists and growers experimented with plants brought from Asia to see what would survive on Georgian soil. Citrus also became a major crop across this region. So the garden is not only a place of beauty. It is also part of a working history of farming and trade along the Black Sea coast.

Now look for the eucalyptus trees in the Australian section. Their bark peels away in long strips, leaving smooth patches of grey, cream, and pale green underneath. Crush a fallen leaf between your fingers and smell it — the sharp, clean scent is unmistakable. Nearby you may see tree ferns, with their thick fibrous trunks and wide, curling fronds. They look almost prehistoric, and in a way they are. Ferns like these belong to a very old family of plants.

The garden is not flat, and that is part of its character. Paths rise and fall in long switchbacks. Steps climb between terraces. Small bridges cross streams that run down to the sea after heavy rain. At several points the trees open up and suddenly you see the coast stretching away in both directions. On clear days, the green mountains of Adjara rise behind you, and the water below changes from deep blue to grey with the passing clouds.

Keep an ear open for the trains. A railway line runs along the shore at the foot of the cape, between the garden and the sea. Every so often a train rumbles past below the trees. It is a surprising sound in such a green place, but it is also a reminder of how this coast has always been connected — by sea, by rail, and by the people who brought plants here from far away.

The seasons change the garden in different ways. In spring, magnolias and camellias bloom, and the paths fill with fallen petals. Summer brings deep shade and thick, humid air, with insects humming in the undergrowth. In autumn, the maples and other deciduous trees turn red and gold against the evergreen background. Even in winter, much of the garden stays green. Mist often rolls in from the sea and hangs between the trees, softening every shape.

The garden is still a place of science as well as leisure. Researchers continue to study, grow, and protect its plant collections. Many of the trees carry small labels with their names and places of origin. It is worth stopping to read a few. You may find a tree from Chile standing beside one from Japan, both growing happily on a Georgian hillside. That kind of meeting is exactly what Krasnov hoped to create.

Before you leave, find a bench or a quiet bend in the path. Look at the layers around you — the ground plants at your feet, the shrubs at eye level, the tall canopy above. Notice how many shades of green there are. Listen again for the sea, the bamboo, the dripping leaves. As you walk back toward the gate, you are carrying a small piece of every continent with you, gathered here on the Green Cape more than a century ago.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for steep paths and many steps.
Carry a light rain jacket; showers come often here.
Bring drinking water for long uphill walks.
Allow at least half a day to explore properly.
Choose the upper paths for wide sea views.
