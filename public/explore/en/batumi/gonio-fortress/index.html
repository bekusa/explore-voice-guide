The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere behind you, the Black Sea is rolling onto a shore of grey pebbles. In front of you, a long wall of rough stone rises out of the grass, warm under the hand and patched with moss. Swallows cut in and out of the gaps between the stones. This is Gonio, a short drive south of Batumi. For close to two thousand years, soldiers of different empires have watched this same stretch of coast from these walls.

The fortress stands near the mouth of the Chorokhi River, where the river meets the sea. That position explains almost everything about it. A river mouth is a doorway. Boats and trade come in through it, and so do armies. Whoever held this flat strip of ground could watch the coast road, the river valley, and the sea at the same time. The mountains of Adjara rise just behind, green and steep, so the land route along the shore narrows here.

The Romans knew this place as Apsaros. The Roman writer Pliny the Elder mentions it in the first century, which makes it one of the oldest recorded military sites on this coast. At its height, Apsaros held a Roman garrison on the far eastern edge of the empire. Picture men from distant provinces, posted to a humid green frontier. Rain came in off the sea, and the nearest large Roman city was many days away.

Now, here is the first surprise. The name Apsaros may be older than the Romans, and it may come from myth. In the Greek story of the Argonauts, Jason sails to Colchis, the ancient kingdom in western Georgia, to seize the Golden Fleece. The king's daughter Medea flees with him. According to one version of the legend, her brother Apsyrtus is killed during the escape. Some tellings link his name, and his grave, to this coast. This is legend, not history. Still, it shows how deeply this shoreline sits inside the ancient Greek imagination.

Walk along the outside of the walls for a moment. The fortress has a broadly rectangular plan, with towers set at regular intervals along each side. The towers project outward from the wall. That design is practical. Archers on a tower could shoot along the face of the wall at anyone trying to climb it. Look at the stone itself. You will see rougher sections and neater sections, larger blocks and smaller ones. Each change in texture can mark a different period of repair.

And those repairs matter. The walls you see today are not one single Roman build. After the Romans came the Byzantines, the eastern Roman Empire that ruled from Constantinople. Later, in the sixteenth century, the Ottoman Turks took control of the fortress. Each power patched, rebuilt, and strengthened what it found. So the fortress is a kind of layered document in stone. The plan follows Roman habits, while much of the visible masonry carries later hands.

Step through the gate and the mood changes. Inside, the space opens into a wide, quiet enclosure of grass and low ruins. After the hard stone outside, the softness of the lawn is a little unexpected. Trees give patches of shade. On a warm day you can hear insects in the grass and the faint traffic of the coast road beyond the wall. It feels less like a battlefield and more like a walled garden.

But under that grass, archaeologists have found the outlines of a working Roman base. Excavations here uncovered foundations of barracks and other buildings, and the remains of a Roman bathhouse. Yes, a bathhouse, here on the frontier. For Roman soldiers, the baths were not a luxury. They were a piece of home, a daily routine of hot rooms and cool water. Traces of floor decoration were found in this area too. Look for the low excavated walls and the marked areas as you cross the enclosure.

The second surprise is gold. Over the years, excavations at Gonio have turned up gold jewellery and other fine objects. They suggest that people of wealth and status lived here or passed through, not only ordinary soldiers. Some of the finds from the site are shown in the small museum on the grounds. If it is open during your visit, it is worth a few minutes. Seeing a delicate piece of metalwork changes how you picture the people who once stood where you are standing.

There is one more story at Gonio, and it is the one many Georgian visitors come for. Local Christian tradition holds that Saint Matthias, the apostle chosen to replace Judas, is buried within these walls. Inside the enclosure you will find a simple grave marked in his honour. Historians cannot confirm the burial. Other places also claim it. But for many pilgrims, the tradition is enough. You may see people pause here quietly, light a candle, or cross themselves before moving on.

Now, climb up onto the walls if the access paths are open. From above, the whole logic of the place becomes clear. To one side you see the sea, flat and silver or deep blue depending on the light. Behind you the mountains rise in folded green ridges. In between lies the narrow coastal plain. The Turkish border is not far to the south. This little corner has been a border region for a very long time, and from up here you can feel why.

Gonio's military story continued long after Rome. Under the Ottomans, the fortress guarded their northern coast here for centuries. In 1878, after a war between the Russian and Ottoman empires, the Batumi region passed to the Russian Empire. By then, a stone fort was no match for modern artillery. Gonio slowly shifted from a working stronghold into a monument. Today it is protected as a heritage site and studied by archaeologists, season after season.

Before you leave, think about the village outside the gate as well. Gonio today is a quiet seaside place with guesthouses, small cafés, and a pebble beach. Families come down from Batumi to swim. Fruit sellers set up by the road in summer. The same coast that once needed watching by soldiers is now a place for long, slow afternoons. The fortress sits right in the middle of ordinary life, which is part of its charm.

As you walk back toward the gate, run your hand along the wall one more time. Feel how the stones are rough in one place and smooth in another. Roman, Byzantine, Ottoman... all of them pressed against the same rock. Listen for the sea again behind you. It was here before the first garrison arrived, and it will keep rolling onto this shore long after you have gone.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning to explore the grassy enclosure in quiet.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone paths and wall walkways.
Visit the on-site museum to see excavated gold finds.
Combine the visit with a swim at Gonio's pebble beach.
Bring water and sun protection; shade inside is limited.
