The guide

The story

Before you see much of anything, you notice the softness underfoot. The ground inside the great house is covered with dried grass and woven palm mats. When you take off your shoes, it gives a little with each step. The air smells of reed, of thatch, and of smoke from distant cooking fires down the hill. Above you, the roof rises into a dark dome of straw. The light inside is low and amber, filtered through the doorway behind you.

You are standing on Kasubi Hill, in the north-west of Kampala. This is the royal burial ground of the Kabakas, the kings of Buganda. Buganda is the oldest surviving kingdom within modern Uganda, and the Baganda people are the country's largest ethnic group. The kingdom gave its name to the whole nation. Uganda simply means the land of the Baganda, as the Swahili-speaking traders who came here once called it. So in a quiet way, this hilltop sits close to the root of the country's identity.

The large thatched building in front of you is called Muzibu Azaala Mpanga. It began as a palace, not a tomb. Kabaka Muteesa the First built it in 1882 as his royal residence. When he died a couple of years later, he was buried inside his own home. That followed a tradition in Buganda, where a king's palace often became his resting place. From that moment, the house changed its purpose, and the hill became a place of the dead as well as the living.

Four Kabakas now lie here. Muteesa the First was joined by his son, Mwanga the Second. Next came Daudi Chwa the Second, and finally Sir Edward Muteesa the Second. Edward Muteesa was also the first President of independent Uganda. Political conflict later forced him into exile, and he died in London in 1969. His body was later brought home and buried here on the hill. Many members of the wider royal family are buried in the grounds around the main house too.

Look up at the roof. The building is made almost entirely from organic materials. These are wooden poles, reeds, wattle, and thick layers of grass thatch. The thatch comes down very low on the outside, almost to the ground. This gives the house its shape, which looks like a huge rounded hive. Inside, the ceiling shows rings of tied reed spiralling toward the top. Each ring is bound by hand. The craftsmanship is not decoration for its own sake. It follows rules and roles passed down through generations.

This is one of the things most visitors miss. The tombs are not only a building. They are a living system of duties. Different clans of Buganda hold hereditary responsibilities here. Some clans care for the thatch, some keep the drums, and others guard the graves. The site is tended by people connected to the royal households, including widows and their descendants. They live on the grounds. So the hill is not a frozen museum. It is a community that carries out its work day after day.

Inside the great house, notice the wooden poles wrapped in bark cloth. Bark cloth is one of the oldest crafts of the region. It is made by stripping the inner bark of a fig tree and beating it into a soft, rust-brown fabric. Its making has been recognised by UNESCO as part of humanity's intangible heritage. Here, it is not just material. It marks the house as royal and sacred. You may also see spears, shields, and portraits of the Kabakas placed near the front.

Now look toward the large bark-cloth curtain that crosses the back of the room. Behind it lies an area known as the Kibira, which means the forest. This is where the actual graves are. Ordinary visitors do not pass through. Entry is reserved for certain members of the royal family and the guardians of the site. So what you see in the front room is the public face of a much more private place. The real heart of the tombs stays hidden, and that is deliberate.

In 2001, the Kasubi Tombs were added to UNESCO's World Heritage List. The listing praised the site as an outstanding example of Ganda architecture, built with traditional skills. It also recognised the tombs as a centre of belief and identity, not only as a monument. For many Baganda, the Kabakas who lie here are still present in a spiritual sense. People come to make requests, to show respect, and to mark important moments in their own lives.

Then, in March 2010, disaster struck. A fire destroyed the main house. The thatch and timber burned quickly, and the loss shocked people across Uganda. The cause of the fire was disputed, and it became a political as well as a cultural wound. In the same year, UNESCO placed the site on its List of World Heritage in Danger. For a time, the hill held a scorched outline where the great house had stood.

What happened next is the second surprise. The house was not replaced with concrete or steel. It was rebuilt by hand, using the same traditional methods and materials. Clan members, thatchers, and craftsmen worked over several years. Rebuilding meant reviving skills that few people still practised at this scale. It also meant ceremonies and agreements between clans, because every step has meaning. After the reconstruction, UNESCO removed the site from its danger list. Much of what you see now is the result of that patient work.

As you walk the grounds, notice the gatehouse at the entrance and the smaller houses around the courtyard. Some of these hold drums, which play an important role in Buganda's royal ceremonies. Others are homes or burial houses for other members of the royal family. The open courtyard was designed for gatherings. On important occasions it fills with people in bark cloth and traditional dress. On a quiet weekday, you may hear only birds, wind in the thatch, and distant traffic from the city below.

Before you leave, stand for a moment in the doorway of the great house. Behind you is the dim, grass-scented room, the bark cloth, and the hidden graves. In front of you is the bright courtyard and the modern city spreading across the hills. Both belong to the same place. Notice the low edge of the thatch as you step back out, and the way it almost touches the earth. Then let your feet find the hard ground again, and carry the quiet of the hill with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Remove your shoes before entering the main thatched house.
Wear clothing that covers shoulders and knees out of respect.
Hire an official site guide to hear clan stories firsthand.
Ask permission before photographing inside the great house.
Visit on a weekday morning for a quieter, calmer courtyard.
