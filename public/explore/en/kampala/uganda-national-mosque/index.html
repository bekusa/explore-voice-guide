The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here on Old Kampala Hill, the noise of the city drops away below you. The boda boda motorcycles, the minibus horns and the market voices still rise from the valley, but they arrive softened, like sound carried across water. Under your feet the paving is warm. In front of you, a pale building with a broad dome and a single tall minaret fills the sky. This is the Uganda National Mosque, one of the largest mosques in East Africa.

Before we go any further, a few words about the hill itself. Kampala is often described as a city of seven hills, although the city has long since spread across many more. Old Kampala is one of the original ones. In the 1890s, the British officer Frederick Lugard built a fort on this hill, and for a short time it was the seat of colonial administration. So the ground you are standing on has been a place of power for well over a century.

Islam reached this region long before the mosque did. In the nineteenth century, Arab and Swahili traders travelled inland from the East African coast. They brought cloth, guns and goods, and they also brought their faith. Several of the rulers of Buganda, the old kingdom at the heart of modern Uganda, showed real interest in Islam during that century. Today Muslims make up a significant minority of Uganda's population, and this hill has become their symbolic centre.

The story of the building is longer than its appearance suggests. The idea of a grand national mosque here dates back to the 1970s, during the rule of Idi Amin. Work began, then stalled, and the site sat unfinished for many years. Uganda went through war, instability and rebuilding. A great mosque on a hilltop was not a priority. The project only came back to life in the early years of this century.

Here is the first detail many visitors do not know. The money that finally finished the mosque came largely from Libya, from its leader at the time, Muammar Gaddafi. When the building opened in 2007, it carried his name. For several years, people across Kampala called it the Gaddafi National Mosque. After his fall from power in 2011, the name changed. Today it is the Uganda National Mosque, although you may still hear older taxi drivers use the earlier name.

The mosque is more than a place of prayer. It also serves as the headquarters of the Uganda Muslim Supreme Council, the main body that represents Muslims in the country. So on a weekday you may see people in office clothes walking between buildings with folders under their arms. On Friday, the weekly day of congregational prayer, the whole complex changes. Thousands of worshippers arrive, and the courtyard fills with rows of people moving together.

When you go inside, you will be asked to remove your shoes. Women are usually offered a headscarf and a long wrap at the entrance, and men in shorts may be asked to cover their legs too. This is not a ritual for tourists. It is simply the respect expected in any working mosque. Guides from the mosque often walk visitors through, and they are usually happy to answer questions about the faith and the building.

Step onto the carpet and notice how quickly the temperature drops. The prayer hall is wide and open, built so that very large numbers of people can stand shoulder to shoulder. Look up. Light enters through coloured glass and falls across the floor in soft patches of red, blue and green. Above you, the dome rises on its supports, and a large chandelier hangs from the centre. The design borrows from several Islamic traditions, rather than copying one single style.

A quick note on direction. Every mosque in the world is oriented toward Mecca, the holy city in Saudi Arabia where Muslims face when they pray. Inside, look for the mihrab, a decorated niche in the wall. It marks that direction. Next to it stands the minbar, a raised pulpit with steps, where the imam delivers the Friday sermon. These two features appear in mosques from Morocco to Indonesia, and here they anchor the whole hall.

Now for the second surprise. Visitors can usually climb the minaret. The staircase is narrow, it turns and turns, and the climb is long enough to leave most people breathing hard. Take it slowly and use the small windows as resting points. Each one gives you a different slice of the city, and each one is a little higher than the last.

At the top, Kampala spreads out in every direction. Red earth, green trees and metal roofs cover the hills. On nearby hilltops you can pick out other places of worship, including the large Christian cathedrals of Namirembe and Rubaga. On a clear day, a pale haze to the south hints at Lake Victoria. It is one of the best ways to understand how this city is built — hill after hill, each with its own history.

From up here, you can also see how close the old and the new sit together. Directly below are the crowded streets of Old Kampala, with their small shops, mechanics and mosques. Further away are the glass towers of the central business district. Kampala has grown fast, and the view from the minaret shows that growth more clearly than any map.

When you come back down, take a minute in the courtyard before you leave. Notice the sounds again. If you are here at the right moment, you may hear the call to prayer rise from the minaret above you, and then answers from smaller mosques on the hills around. Watch how people move — some hurrying to pray, some sitting quietly in the shade, some children chasing each other along the edge of the paving.

As you walk back down the hill, look over your shoulder once more. The dome and the minaret stay in view for a long time, pale against the sky. A building that waited decades to be finished now watches over the city every day. And the hill that once held a colonial fort now holds a place where thousands of people gather to pray together.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly; wraps and headscarves are offered at the entrance.
Avoid Friday midday prayers if you want a quiet tour.
Ask for an official mosque guide at the gate.
Wear comfortable shoes for the long minaret climb.
Come in the late afternoon for softer light over the city.
