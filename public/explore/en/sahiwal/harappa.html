The guide

The story

Listen to the ground under your shoes. It crunches. Look down and you will see why: tiny fragments of fired clay, red and orange and dusty brown, scattered across the surface of the mounds. Some of those fragments are broken pottery. Some are pieces of brick. Many of them were shaped by human hands more than four thousand years ago. Here, outside the modern town of Harappa, a short drive from Sahiwal in Pakistan's Punjab, you are walking on one of the oldest cities in South Asia.

This place gave its name to an entire civilization. Archaeologists often call the ancient culture of the Indus Valley the Harappan Civilization, because Harappa was the first of its cities to be excavated and identified. At its height, this culture spread across a huge area. That area covered much of present-day Pakistan and parts of northwest India. Its people built planned cities, traded across long distances, and used a script that no one has yet been able to read.

The story of the discovery is a story of slow understanding. In the nineteenth century, British travellers and surveyors noticed the large mounds here. Alexander Cunningham, who led early archaeological survey work in colonial India, visited and collected some objects. Among them was a small carved seal with an animal and strange signs. Nobody knew what it was. Nobody guessed how old it might be. The real excavations began in the 1920s, led by the Indian archaeologist Daya Ram Sahni.

Here is the first surprise, and it is a painful one. Before scholars understood the site, much of it was carried away. In the mid-nineteenth century, engineers were building the railway line between Lahore and Multan. They needed hard material to lay under the tracks. The ancient fired bricks of Harappa were perfect for the job. Large quantities were taken from the mounds and broken up for the railway bed. Trains in this region ran, for many years, on the ruins of a Bronze Age city.

Because of that damage, Harappa does not look like a city of standing walls. What you see instead are low hills, exposed brick foundations, and excavated trenches. This can feel strange at first. But give your eyes a little time. Follow the lines of brickwork. Notice how the walls meet at clean right angles. Notice how even the brick courses are. Slowly, the plan of a living city starts to appear in front of you.

Those bricks deserve a closer look. Builders across the Indus cities used bricks of very similar proportions. The thickness, the width, and the length follow a ratio of roughly one to two to four. That consistency suggests shared standards over a wide area. Think about it for a moment — thousands of years ago, without any modern state as we know it, people hundreds of kilometres apart were making bricks to the same pattern.

The site is organised into several large mounds. Archaeologists have given them letters and names. One high mound to the west is often called the citadel, and it is raised above the surrounding ground. To the north sits another mound with a large brick structure. Earlier excavators called this building a granary. Today many researchers are more cautious. They describe it simply as a great hall, because nobody is certain how it was used.

Near that hall, excavators found circular brick platforms. For a long time, people believed workers stood on them to pound grain. That idea is still possible, but it is an interpretation, not a certainty. This is a good habit to bring with you around the site. Many of the names you will hear, like citadel or granary, are labels from the twentieth century. They are guesses made by archaeologists, and some of those guesses are now being questioned.

What is clear is how much these people cared about water. Harappa had wells lined with brick. It had drains running along the streets and out of houses. Waste water was carried away in covered channels. In many parts of the ancient world, cities were far messier than this. Walking past the remains of these drains, you are looking at a very early form of urban planning, designed for daily life and public health.

Now for the second surprise. Some of the smallest objects from Harappa tell the biggest stories. Archaeologists found sets of cubical stone weights, carefully cut and graded in size. They found beads of carnelian, a red stone, drilled with remarkable precision. And they found many square seals, carved with animals and short lines of script. These seals were probably used in trade or administration. The script on them remains undeciphered, so every inscription here is still a question.

Harappa was not a single moment frozen in time. People lived here over many centuries. The earliest settlement began long before the city reached its full size. Later, the city declined, and its great urban period came to an end. Archaeologists still debate the reasons. Changes in rivers, climate, and trade networks are all part of the discussion. The old course of the Ravi River once ran closer to the city than it does today, and the land around you has shifted over thousands of years.

The work here continues. Since the 1980s, an international research team has worked alongside Pakistani archaeologists at the site. Their careful excavations have changed how scholars see Harappa. They have studied craft workshops, burials, and the growth of the city layer by layer. If you visit the small site museum, you can see some of the objects found here. Pottery, beads, figurines, and seals give a human scale to the mounds outside.

Before you leave, stop for a moment on one of the higher points. Look around you. You will see fields, trees, the modern village, and perhaps a few goats moving between the mounds. Listen to the wind and the voices from the nearby houses. Then look down again at the brick dust and the broken pottery under your feet. People cooked, traded, built, and argued here, in a language we cannot yet read. As you walk back, notice how ordinary the ground looks... and how much is still waiting inside it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning to avoid the strongest Punjab heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven mounds and loose brick rubble.
Visit the site museum first to understand the mounds.
Carry water and a hat; shade is limited on site.
Stay on paths and never pick up surface pottery.
