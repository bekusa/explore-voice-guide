The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, the village is quiet. There is the hum of a passing car, a bicycle bell, perhaps cowbells drifting in from the fields of the Freiamt countryside. Then the abbey church comes into view. Its pale walls and twin towers rise above the rooftops, and a wide dome sits in the middle like a crown. Take a breath before you go inside. You are standing at a place that has been tied to one of Europe's most powerful families for almost a thousand years.

The story begins in 1027. That year, a Benedictine monastery was founded here. The Benedictines are a religious order that follows the rule of Saint Benedict, a life built around prayer, work and study. The founders were members of an ambitious noble family from this region. Their castle stood not far away, at a place called Habsburg. From that modest castle, the family took its name. Over the following centuries, the Habsburgs would rule Austria, Spain and much of central Europe. Muri was one of their earliest foundations.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. The monks of Muri wrote down the story of their own beginnings. This account, known as the Acta Murensia, is among the oldest written sources on the early Habsburgs. Historians still use it today. So this quiet abbey in the Swiss countryside holds part of the origin story of a dynasty that would one day sit on imperial thrones. The family's power grew far beyond these fields. The abbey kept the memory of where it started.

Now look at the church itself. It is really two buildings joined together. The older parts, including the towers and the choir, keep traces of the Romanesque church. Romanesque is the heavy, round-arched style of the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Then, in the late seventeenth century, the middle of the church was rebuilt. The builders created a large octagonal space, an eight-sided hall under a broad dome. That is the Baroque layer, full of light and movement.

Step inside and let your eyes adjust. The contrast is striking. White walls, gilded altars, stucco ornament and painted ceilings fill the central space. Light falls from the windows in the dome and moves across the floor through the day. Stand in the very centre of the octagon and turn slowly in a full circle. You will notice how every altar seems to face you. The Baroque designers wanted worshippers to feel surrounded by heaven. Below your feet, older foundations remain from the medieval church.

Listen again. This church is known for its organs. There is more than one, placed in different parts of the building. Music has always been central to Benedictine life, with monks singing the daily hours of prayer together. Today, concerts in Muri make use of these instruments, and the sound can travel between them across the dome. If you are lucky enough to hear one being played, stay still. The acoustics of the octagon carry the notes up and around in a way recordings never quite capture.

Now for the second surprise. In a small side chapel, the Loreto Chapel, rest the hearts of the last Austrian emperor and empress. Charles the First ruled until the collapse of the empire after the First World War. His wife was Empress Zita. Their hearts were brought here to Muri, the family's ancient foundation, while their bodies lie elsewhere. Zita died in 1989. So the story of the Habsburgs, which began with this abbey, comes full circle in the same building.

The abbey did not have an easy history. In the nineteenth century, Switzerland went through bitter conflicts between liberal and Catholic forces. In 1841, the government of the canton of Aargau dissolved the monasteries on its territory, and Muri was one of them. A canton is a Swiss state, with its own government. The monks were forced to leave. They did not disappear, though. The community moved south and settled at Gries, near Bolzano, in what is today northern Italy.

After the monks left, the great convent buildings found new uses. For many years they housed a school. Then, in 1889, a fire swept through part of the complex and caused heavy damage. Some sections were rebuilt, others changed character. As you walk around the outside, notice how the buildings do not all match. That patchwork shows the abbey's long life: medieval beginnings, Baroque ambition, nineteenth century loss, and later repair.

Do not leave without the cloister. This is the covered walkway around an inner courtyard, where monks once walked in quiet reflection. Its windows hold a remarkable collection of Renaissance stained glass from the sixteenth century. These are not dark medieval windows. They are detailed scenes and coats of arms, painted in clear colours, made by Swiss glass painters for noble donors. Walk slowly and look closely. Each panel tells a small story, and many show the pride of the families who paid for them.

The abbey is also home to museums today. One is devoted to the history of the monastery itself. Another honours Caspar Wolf, an eighteenth century painter born here in Muri. Wolf is remembered for his paintings of the Alps, made at a time when most people saw mountains as frightening places. He painted glaciers, gorges and waterfalls with real care. In a way, he helped teach Europe to look at the Swiss mountains with wonder rather than fear.

And the Benedictines? Their connection with Muri was never fully broken. In the twentieth century, a small group of monks returned to live here again. Their community is modest, but prayer has come back to this place after a long gap. Officially, the monastery in Gries and the house in Muri are still linked. That one shared name, Muri-Gries, carries the memory of exile and return across the Alps.

Before you go, step back outside and look once more at the church. See the older towers and the newer dome standing side by side. Notice the quiet village around you, the farmland beyond, the soft hills of the Freiamt. Nearly a thousand years ago, a noble family chose this spot to found a house of prayer. Their descendants ruled empires, and some of them came home here in the end. As you walk away, listen for the bells. They have marked the hours here for centuries.
