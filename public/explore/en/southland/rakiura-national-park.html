The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Beneath the trees the air is damp and cool, and it smells of wet moss and soft, rotting wood. Somewhere above you a bird clicks, then whistles. Underfoot the track is spongy, laced with roots and dark peat. This is Rakiura, the forest at the bottom of New Zealand, and almost everything here grows slowly, quietly and close to the ground.

Rakiura National Park covers most of Stewart Island, the third-largest island in New Zealand. It lies across Foveaux Strait from the southern tip of the South Island. Most visitors arrive by ferry from Bluff or by small plane from Invercargill. The crossing can be rough. Locals often joke that Foveaux Strait is the island's first test. The water here is cold, fast and exposed to the Southern Ocean.

The park itself is young. It was established in 2002, which makes it one of the newest national parks in the country. Yet the land it protects is very old and very little changed. Roughly eighty-five percent of the island lies inside its boundaries. There are almost no roads here. The forest, the wetlands, the granite domes and the long empty beaches are reached on foot or by boat.

The name carries its own story. Rakiura is the Māori name for the island, and it is commonly translated as glowing skies. Some people link it to the long, red southern sunsets. Others link it to the aurora australis, the southern lights, which can sometimes be seen from here. In 2019 the island was recognised as an International Dark Sky Sanctuary. On a clear night, away from the lights of Oban, the Milky Way looks thick enough to touch.

There is an older story too. In Māori tradition, the South Island is the canoe of the demigod Māui, and Stewart Island is Te Punga o Te Waka a Māui — the anchor stone of Māui's canoe. Imagine that for a moment. The whole of the South Island, held in place by the island under your feet. It is a legend, but it says something true about how this place is seen: small, heavy, and holding everything steady.

The English name comes from an officer named William Stewart, who charted parts of the coast during the sealing era of the early nineteenth century. Sealers, whalers, timber cutters and fishermen all came and went. Little of their work remains inside the park today. The forest has grown back over old sawmill sites and tramlines. If you walk the coastal tracks, you may pass rusting machinery half hidden in ferns — quiet traces of people who tried to make a living here.

Look up at the canopy. Much of the forest is podocarp and hardwood forest, with tall rimu, kamahi and southern rātā. The lower layers are thick with ferns, mosses and lichens. Because the island is so wet and so windy, the trees near the coast grow bent and low. Further inland, the forest opens into wetlands, tussock and granite tops. The highest point, Hananui, also called Mount Anglem, rises to just under a thousand metres.

Now listen again for the birds. This is one of the best places in New Zealand to hear native forest birds in large numbers. Tūī and bellbirds sing in the trees. Kākā, a large brown forest parrot, move noisily through the branches and sometimes over the township itself. The heavy wingbeat you may hear is the kererū, the native pigeon. On the ground, the curious brown weka often walks right up to people. Keep your lunch close.

Here is something most visitors do not expect. Stewart Island is home to the southern brown kiwi, known here as tokoeka. Unlike most kiwi, which are strictly nocturnal, tokoeka on this island are sometimes seen in daylight. They feed on beaches and in grassland, probing the sand for insects. Mason Bay, on the remote west coast, is especially known for them. Seeing a wild kiwi without a torch is a rare experience anywhere in New Zealand.

A second surprise sits just offshore. In Paterson Inlet lies Ulva Island, a small predator-free sanctuary reached by a short water taxi ride. Because rats and other pests have been removed, rare birds that struggle elsewhere live here in good numbers. You may hear the saddleback, or see the tiny yellowhead and the Stewart Island robin hopping close to your boots. It feels a little like walking into the forest as it was before people arrived.

For walkers, the park offers a range of experiences. The Rakiura Track is one of New Zealand's Great Walks, a loop usually completed over about three days, with huts along the way. It follows beaches, crosses forested ridges and passes old milling sites. The much longer North West Circuit is for experienced trampers, and it is known for deep mud. Shorter walks start close to Oban, so you do not need days to feel the forest.

The Māori connection to this place continues today. Rakiura Māori have harvested tītī, the sooty shearwater, often called muttonbird, on the small islands around Stewart Island for many generations. This seasonal harvest is a cultural practice guarded with care, and it carries knowledge passed down through families. The wider story is one of people who understood the sea, the weather and the birds here long before any map showed this island.

The weather shapes every visit. It can change several times in one afternoon, from bright sun to cold rain and back again. Wind comes straight off the ocean. Tracks are often wet and muddy, and sandflies gather near the water. None of this is a reason to stay away. It is simply part of the place. The rain is what feeds the mosses, the ferns and the long green silence of the forest.

Before you move on, take one more breath of this air. Notice how quiet it is between the birdcalls, and how the light comes down green through the leaves. Look at the ground near your feet: the moss, the small ferns, perhaps a weka's footprint in the mud. When you walk back towards the bay, watch the sky over the water. On this island, the name Rakiura often feels like a simple description.

Before you go

Practical tips

Pack waterproof layers even when the morning looks clear.
Bring insect repellent for sandflies near beaches and water.
Book huts ahead if walking the Rakiura Track.
Take a water taxi to Ulva Island for rare birds.
Step away from Oban's lights on clear nights for stars.
