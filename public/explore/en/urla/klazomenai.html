The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere behind you, the gulf moves against a stony shore. The wind comes off the water and moves through the olive trees in short, dry gusts. Underfoot, the ground is a mix of pale earth, broken stone, and low scrub. It does not look like much at first glance. But you are standing on the ground of one of the old cities of Ionia, a place that was once important on this coast.

Ionia was the name the ancient Greeks gave to this strip of the Aegean coast of Anatolia, the land we now call Turkey. Twelve cities here formed a loose religious and cultural union, known as the Ionian League. Klazomenai was one of those twelve. Its neighbours included Smyrna, the city that became modern Izmir, and Erythrai, further out along the peninsula. Together these cities shaped much of early Greek art, trade, and thought.

The town of Urla sits just inland, and the coast below it was settled long before the classical Greeks arrived. Near the harbour area, at a mound often called Liman Tepe, archaeologists have studied layers that go back thousands of years, into the Bronze Age. Some of that work happens under the water itself, because the old shoreline has shifted. So the story of this place does not begin with the Greeks. They added a new chapter to a coast that people had already used for a very long time.

Now look around at the olive trees. They are not just scenery. Olive oil was central to the life of Klazomenai. At this site, excavators found the remains of an olive oil workshop from the Archaic period, roughly the sixth century before Christ. It is considered one of the earliest known examples of organised olive oil production in the ancient world. That is the first surprise most visitors miss — an industrial workshop, over two and a half thousand years old, in the middle of a quiet field.

The workshop has been reconstructed so you can understand how it worked. The basic idea is simple. Olives were crushed, then the pulp was pressed with a long wooden beam weighted at one end. The oil ran out into stone channels and settling basins. Heavy oil and water separate naturally, and the workers used that fact to collect the clean oil. When you stand beside the press, imagine the smell of crushed fruit, the creak of timber, and the slow drip of oil into stone.

This oil did not stay at home. Klazomenai traded across the Aegean and beyond, and its products moved in clay jars on ships. Archaeologists recognise particular styles of pottery from this city at sites far away. That trade made the city wealthy enough to support fine craftsmen. And the most famous of their works are not vases or statues, but coffins.

The painted clay coffins of Klazomenai are known to specialists simply as Klazomenian sarcophagi. They were made of terracotta, which is baked clay, and decorated mostly in the sixth century before Christ. The painted frames around the opening often show animals, warriors, chariots, and bands of ornament in dark red and black on a pale background. Many are now in museums, including collections in Izmir and abroad. They are one of the clearest windows we have into the art of Archaic Ionia.

The second surprise is a person. Klazomenai was the birthplace of Anaxagoras, a philosopher of the fifth century before Christ. He moved to Athens and became part of the circle around the statesman Pericles. Anaxagoras argued that the sun was not a god, but a hot, glowing mass of stone or metal. For an Athenian audience, that was a dangerous idea. He was charged with impiety, which meant disrespect toward the gods, and he left Athens for the rest of his life.

Think about that for a moment. One of the first people to describe the heavens as physical matter came from this coast, from a town of olive presses and painted coffins. Ancient sources also say that Anaxagoras believed a cosmic mind, which he called nous, set the universe in order. His ideas travelled far beyond this small harbour. They became part of the long Greek conversation that later led to Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle.

Now turn toward the water, to the small island just offshore. Ancient writers say that the people of Klazomenai moved to an island to protect themselves from Persian attack. The Persian Empire took control of Ionia in the sixth century before Christ, and the coastal cities lived under that pressure for generations. According to ancient accounts, Alexander the Great later planned to join the island to the mainland. The precise details of that link are still discussed by scholars, so treat it as a story with ancient roots rather than a settled fact.

The island in front of you is known locally as Karantina Island, which means Quarantine Island. In much more recent times, it served as a quarantine station for travellers arriving by sea. Today it has a hospital, and a road connects it to the shore. So the same piece of land has been a place of refuge twice — once from armies, and later from disease. The landscape keeps returning to the same idea of safety across the water.

As you walk through the site, notice how little stands above knee height. That is normal for an ancient Ionian city. Stone was valuable, and later builders often carried it away for new houses, walls, and terraces. What remains are foundations, cut blocks, and the outlines of rooms. Read them like a floor plan. Notice where a doorway might have been, where a wall turned a corner, and how close everything sits to the shoreline.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute and look across the gulf. The light here changes quickly, from hard white at midday to softer gold in the late afternoon. Fishing boats move slowly past. The olive trees around you are the descendants, in a sense, of the groves that fed the old presses. Listen again to the wind and the water. A philosopher who looked at the sun and asked what it really was once heard these same sounds. As you walk back toward Urla, you carry a small part of that question with you.
