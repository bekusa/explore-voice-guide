The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, the wind is steady and salty. It moves through the steel cables above the water and makes a low, soft hum. Below, the sea is pale green near the shore and a deeper blue further out. White lines of road cross the bay on tall grey pillars, and the bridge runs low and level until it reaches the hills of a long, rocky peninsula. This is the Pelješac Bridge, and it is one of the youngest landmarks on the Croatian coast.

To understand why this bridge exists, look at a map in your mind. Croatia's coastline runs south toward Dubrovnik. But just north of Dubrovnik, a short strip of coast belongs to another country, Bosnia and Herzegovina. That strip surrounds the small town of Neum. For decades, anyone driving from Dubrovnik to the rest of Croatia had to leave the country, cross a few kilometres of Bosnian territory, and then enter Croatia again. That meant two border checks on a single short drive.

So the southern tip of Croatia was, in practical terms, cut off by land. The answer was simple on paper and very hard in practice. Instead of going around the Neum corridor, go over the water. The bridge leaves the mainland near the village of Komarna and lands on the Pelješac peninsula near Brijesta. From Pelješac, a road continues south toward Ston and Dubrovnik. The whole journey now stays inside Croatia from start to finish.

The idea is older than the bridge itself. Work first began in the late 2000s. A foundation stone was laid, and then the project stopped. Money was short, and the plans were debated for years. Bosnia and Herzegovina also raised concerns about the bridge, mainly about access to the open sea from its own small port area. The project came back to life in the late 2010s, when the European Union agreed to fund most of the cost.

Here is the first detail many visitors do not expect. Although the money came largely from the European Union, the main construction work was done by a Chinese state company, China Road and Bridge Corporation. It was one of the most visible Chinese infrastructure projects inside the European Union at that time. Construction started in 2018, and crews worked through difficult years, including the pandemic. The bridge opened to traffic in the summer of 2022, with celebrations and fireworks over the bay.

Now look at the shape of it. The Pelješac Bridge is a cable-stayed bridge. That means tall towers, called pylons, rise above the road deck. Straight steel cables run down from these towers in fan-like lines and hold up the road. There is not just one tower here, but a whole series of them across the bay. The design came from the Slovenian engineer Marjan Pipenbaher, and its long, even rhythm is easiest to see from the shore.

The bridge is long, at more than two kilometres from end to end. It is also high in the middle. The central section rises well above the water, so that ships can pass underneath into the inner bay. The engineers also faced a hidden problem. The seabed here is soft, and solid rock lies deep below it. So the pillars stand on very long steel piles, driven far down into the ground beneath the sea.

This brings us to the second surprise. The water under the bridge is not just a shipping lane. It is one of the most valued shellfish areas on the Adriatic. Mali Ston Bay, just to the south, is known across Croatia for its oysters. The water here mixes fresh water from rivers and springs with salt water from the sea. That mixture suits oysters and mussels very well. During construction, protecting these clean waters was a serious concern for local farmers.

If you look closely across the bay, you may see rows of floating buoys or wooden frames on the water. These belong to shellfish farms. Families in this area have farmed oysters for generations. In the nearby village of Mali Ston, small restaurants serve them fresh, often with just lemon and bread. The bridge did not replace this old way of life. It now simply rises above it. Modern traffic passes overhead, and the oyster farmers keep working below.

On the Pelješac side, there is more history nearby. The town of Ston, a short drive south, is known for its long medieval stone walls. These walls climb up and over the hills. They were built when this area belonged to the Republic of Dubrovnik, a small and wealthy trading state. Ston also has old salt pans, shallow pools where seawater dries in the sun and leaves salt behind. The bridge gives these places easier access from the north.

The peninsula itself is worth noticing too. Pelješac is long and narrow, with steep hills and small villages. It is famous in Croatia for wine, especially red wine made from a local grape called Plavac Mali. Vineyards grow on sunny slopes, some of them very steep, facing the sea. Before the bridge, many travellers from the north saw Pelješac only from a distance. Now the bridge leads drivers straight onto the peninsula, and many stop to explore it.

A practical note. The bridge is part of a fast road, and it is built for cars, not for people on foot. You cannot stop on the bridge to take photos. The best views come from the shore at either end. Small roadside spots and nearby villages give good angles across the water. In the early morning, the sea is often calm and the light is soft. In the evening, the pylons stand dark against the sky as the sun goes down.

Think for a moment about what this bridge changed for local people. For someone living in Dubrovnik, a trip to Split or Zagreb used to mean queues at two borders, especially in the busy summer months. Now that same drive crosses the sea in a few minutes. For many Croatians, the bridge was not only about transport. It was about feeling that the whole country was joined together by its own road.

So take one more look before you go. Follow the line of the road from the mainland, across the towers, to the hills of Pelješac. Notice the cables catching the light. Notice the small boats and the oyster buoys far below. Feel the wind coming off the sea. As you drive on, or walk back to the shore, remember that this calm crossing took many years of argument, planning, and work. And now, it is simply part of the coast.

Before you go

Practical tips

Admire the bridge from shore viewpoints near Komarna or Brijesta.
Do not stop on the bridge deck to take photos.
Come in early morning for calm water and soft light.
Combine the crossing with oysters in nearby Mali Ston.
Continue south to walk the medieval walls of Ston.
