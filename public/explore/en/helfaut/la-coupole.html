The guide

The story

Cool air moves against your face as you enter the tunnel. It carries the smell of damp chalk and old concrete. Water ticks somewhere in the dark. Overhead, the rock holds the steady chill of underground places, the same in July as in December. You are inside a hillside in northern France, near the small town of Helfaut, a short drive from Saint-Omer. Above you sits one of the strangest structures of the Second World War — a vast concrete dome, built to hide the preparation of rockets.

The French name, La Coupole, simply means the dome. Seen from outside, the structure looks almost like a grey shell pressed into the edge of the hill. That modest shape is misleading. The dome is several metres thick, made of heavily reinforced concrete, and it is among the largest bunker roofs built during the war. Its builders designed it to survive the heaviest bombs of the time. The chalk underneath was hollowed into galleries, chambers and rail tunnels.

To understand why it is here, picture the map in 1943. Germany occupied this corner of France. The coast is close, and London is within rocket range from here. German engineers had developed the V-2, a liquid-fuelled ballistic missile. The V stood for Vergeltungswaffe, a German word that means weapon of retaliation. Unlike an aircraft, the V-2 flew faster than sound. People in its target area received no warning and could not defend against it.

The plan for this site was ambitious. Rockets would arrive by rail and move into the tunnels below. Crews would fuel and prepare them in the protected galleries. Then they would roll out to launch positions near the quarry. The location was chosen with care. An old chalk quarry gave the builders a natural cliff face. The soft white rock was easy to dig, and the surrounding countryside could hide the work from the air.

The construction had a heavy human cost. The Germans relied on forced labour on a large scale. Workers came from many places, including prisoners of war and men conscripted from occupied countries. Organisation Todt managed the work. It was the Nazi state's engineering and construction body. Conditions were hard, and the work went on under constant pressure. The museum treats this part of the story seriously. As you walk through, notice how often the exhibits return to the people who poured this concrete.

Here is the first surprise. This enormous bunker never fired a single rocket. Allied intelligence watched the site closely through aerial reconnaissance photographs. From 1943 onward, bombers struck it again and again as part of a wider campaign against German V-weapon sites. The British named that campaign Operation Crossbow. The dome itself held firm. The ground around it did not. The bombing shook the chalk and caused landslides that buried access routes and damaged the surrounding works.

In the summer of 1944, the Royal Air Force used some of its heaviest bombs against the site. These were the Tallboy earthquake bombs. Designers built them to strike close to a target and shake its foundations from below, not to punch through the roof. The plan worked well enough. The site became unusable. After the Allied landings in Normandy, the Germans abandoned the bunker. Allied troops reached it in September 1944. By then, V-2 rockets were already falling on London and Antwerp, but they came from mobile launchers elsewhere.

For decades afterwards, the bunker stood mostly forgotten. It was too massive to demolish easily and too grim to celebrate. Local people knew it, and children sometimes explored its edges. Only in the 1990s did the site become a museum and a centre for remembrance. It opened to the public in 1997. Today it tells the story of the German occupation of northern France, the V-weapons, and the people who lived through those years in this region.

The second surprise comes as you move deeper into the exhibition. The story does not end in 1945. The V-2 was the first human-made object to reach the edge of space. After the war, its engineers and its technology passed to the victorious powers. Wernher von Braun led the German rocket programme. He later became a central figure in the American space programme, which carried astronauts to the Moon. The rockets of the space race grew in part from the machine designed to strike London.

That is an uncomfortable line to follow, and the museum lets you sit with it. The same engineering that led toward the Moon also depended on terror and forced labour. In recent years the site added a planetarium, which turns visitors' eyes to the stars and to modern space science. Standing between those two stories, you hear both of them at once. One is a story of discovery. The other is a story of destruction.

As you walk, pay attention to the scale of the galleries. The tunnels are wide and high, with arched ceilings cut into the white rock. In places, the concrete lining shows the marks of wooden formwork, the moulds builders poured the concrete into. Elsewhere you will see bare chalk, pale and slightly rough. When the exhibition takes you up beneath the dome, the space opens around you. You get a sense of how much material it took to build this roof.

Outside, take a few minutes to look back at the structure from the open ground. The quarry face is now softened by trees and grass. Birds move through the hedges, and the fields of the Artois region stretch away under a wide sky. It is a quiet landscape, ordinary and green. That ordinariness is part of what makes the dome so startling. In the middle of farmland sits a war machine on the scale of a cathedral, built for a purpose it never served.

When you leave, notice the change in temperature as you come back into the daylight. The air warms, the light grows brighter, and the sounds of the countryside return. The dome stays behind you, heavy and still. Many visitors arrive expecting a military curiosity and leave thinking about the workers, the bombed towns, and the long road from this hillside to the Moon. Take a last look at that grey curve before you go. It has stood unmoved for more than eighty years.

Key facts

Built by Nazi Germany in 1943 and 1944 as a V-2 rocket base.
Dug into a former chalk quarry near Saint-Omer.
