The guide

The story

Listen first. Shoes come off at the door, and the polished floor feels cool under your socks. Voices drop. Outside, the wind that crosses the Kazakh steppe pushes against the walls, but inside the air is still. Above you, a white dome curves high overhead. Light comes in through rows of windows and falls across the carpet in soft bands. This is the Hazrat Sultan Mosque, and for a moment the city seems to stay outside.

Astana is a young capital. Much of what you see around you rose from open grassland in the late 1990s and the decades after. The mosque belongs to that wave of building. It opened in 2012, and at the time it was described as the largest mosque in Central Asia. Even now, as you look at it from the square, its size is the first thing you notice. It is white, wide, and calm, and it stands under a very large sky.

The name carries a story much older than the building. Hazrat Sultan is an honorific title. It belongs to Khoja Ahmed Yasawi, a Sufi poet and teacher of the twelfth century. Sufism is the mystical tradition within Islam. It focuses on the inner, personal search for God. Yasawi wrote his verses in a Turkic language, not in Arabic or Persian. Because of that, ordinary people across the steppe could understand his teaching.

That choice made Yasawi one of the most loved spiritual figures in Kazakh culture. His mausoleum stands in the southern city of Turkistan, far from here. It is a large blue-tiled building that pilgrims have visited for centuries. So when the new capital named its main mosque after him, the message was clear. This building was meant to connect a modern city with a very old line of faith and language.

Now look up at the four minarets. A minaret is the slender tower from which the call to prayer traditionally sounds. Here there is one tower at each corner of the complex. Each rises about seventy-seven metres into the air. On a clear day their tips look sharp against the blue. On a grey winter day, they can almost disappear into the low cloud. Either way, they help you find the mosque from far across this part of the city.

The overall style follows classical Islamic architecture. You see the central dome, the arched openings, and the clean symmetry. This is a design language that people from Istanbul to Cairo would recognise. But walk closer and study the surfaces. The ornament includes patterns drawn from Kazakh tradition. These are the curling, horn-like shapes found on felt carpets, on wooden chests, and on the walls of the yurt. The yurt is the round felt tent of the nomads.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Those curling motifs are not only decoration. In Kazakh folk art, the ram's horn shape is often linked with wellbeing and plenty. Herding animals was the base of nomadic life, so that link makes sense. When you see these patterns carved into a mosque, you see two traditions sitting side by side. One is Islamic. The other is the older culture of the steppe.

Step inside the main prayer hall, and the scale changes how you move. The hall is built to hold thousands of worshippers. Figures commonly given suggest up to ten thousand people across the complex during major prayers. Most of the time, though, you will see only a few people. Someone kneels near a column. A small group sits quietly. Perhaps a child is following a parent across the carpet. The emptiness makes the space feel even larger.

Raise your eyes to the inside of the dome. The decoration spreads outward from the centre in layered rings of pattern and calligraphy. Calligraphy is the art of beautiful writing, and in a mosque it usually shows verses from the Quran. Islamic tradition generally avoids images of people in places of prayer. So writing and geometry carry the visual weight instead. Notice how the pattern repeats and repeats. Then notice how your eye keeps returning to the centre.

Find the mihrab, too. This is the niche set into the wall that shows the direction of Mecca. Every worshipper in the hall faces it. Near it stands the minbar. This is the raised pulpit with steps, where the imam gives the Friday sermon. These two features appear in mosques all over the world. Here, though, they sit inside a hall built on a scale that matches the size of the capital around it.

This is a working mosque, not a museum. That shapes the visit. People come here to pray five times a day, and Friday midday prayer draws the largest crowds. Weddings and family ceremonies also take place in the complex. Couples sometimes pose for photographs on the steps outside. If you arrive during a prayer, stay near the back. Keep your voice low, and simply watch. You will learn more from that quiet than from any sign.

When you walk back outside, turn slowly in a full circle. Not far away, you can see the pyramid-shaped Palace of Peace and Reconciliation. It was built to host gatherings of world religious leaders. Around you is the wide open ground near Independence Square. The government towers of the left bank rise across the river. This is a city that builds big symbols and places them in open space. The mosque is one of the calmest of those symbols.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the square for a moment. Feel the wind again, now that you are back in it. Look at the white walls one more time. Notice how the colour changes with the hour. It is warm cream in the morning, bright white at noon, and pale gold near sunset. Then, as you walk away, glance back once at the four minarets. Watch them grow smaller over your shoulder.

Practical tips

Dress modestly; women should bring a headscarf.
Remove your shoes before entering the prayer hall.
Avoid Friday midday prayer if you want a quiet visit.
Visit near sunset to see the white walls turn gold.
Combine with the nearby Palace of Peace and Reconciliation.
