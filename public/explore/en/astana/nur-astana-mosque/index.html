The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go in. The traffic of Astana runs past in a steady hum, and the steppe wind pulls at your coat. Then you pass through the doors, and the sound drops away. Underfoot there is soft carpet. Above you there is a wide, high space. For many visitors, the first thing they notice inside is the quiet.

This is the Nur-Astana Mosque. The name joins two words. Nur means light, a word used across the Islamic world, often for divine light. Astana is the name of the city, and in Kazakh it simply means capital. So the name says, quite plainly, the light of the capital. It is a fitting name for a building that went up as a new capital was taking shape.

It helps to know what was happening here at the time. In the late 1990s, Kazakhstan moved its capital from Almaty, far to the south, to this windswept city on the northern steppe. What followed was one of the fastest building campaigns of modern times. Ministries, towers, parks and boulevards rose in only a few years. Religious buildings were part of that plan too.

The mosque opened in the mid-2000s. It is widely reported as a gift from the State of Qatar, whose ruling family paid for its construction. That detail surprises many visitors. They expect a national project funded at home. Instead, this mosque came from a friendship between two Muslim countries, thousands of kilometres apart. One is a small Gulf state. The other is a huge Central Asian republic.

When it opened, the mosque was described as one of the largest in Central Asia. That title did not last long. Astana kept building. The Hazrat Sultan Mosque opened in the early 2010s, and an even larger grand mosque followed in the 2020s. Today Nur-Astana is no longer the biggest. But it was the first major mosque of the new capital, and many local people still think of it that way.

Now look up at the building itself. There is a central dome, with four tall minarets around it. A minaret is the slender tower that traditionally carried the call to prayer across a town. Here, the towers rise well above the surrounding streets. The shapes follow a style familiar from the Middle East and the Ottoman world, rather than the older brick traditions of Central Asia.

Here is a detail most visitors walk past without knowing. The proportions are often said to carry a meaning. The minarets are commonly described as sixty-three metres tall, the age at which the Prophet Muhammad is believed to have died. The dome is said to be forty metres high, the age at which, in Islamic tradition, he received his first revelation. So the numbers you see here are meant to tell a story.

Inside, the space opens up under the dome. The prayer hall is designed to hold several thousand worshippers at once. On ordinary days you may see only a few people. They kneel, bow, or sit quietly against a wall. On Fridays and during religious holidays the scene changes completely. Then the carpet fills with rows of men standing shoulder to shoulder, all facing the same direction.

That direction is marked by the mihrab. This is the niche in the wall that points toward Mecca, the holy city in Saudi Arabia. Every mosque in the world has one. Look for the decoration around it, and for the minbar nearby. The minbar is the raised pulpit with steps, where the imam gives the Friday sermon. These two features are the heart of the room. Everything else is arranged around them.

It is worth thinking about faith in this country for a moment. For most of the twentieth century, Kazakhstan was part of the Soviet Union. Religion was discouraged, and many mosques were closed or used for other purposes. After independence in 1991, religious life returned in the open. A large, new mosque in the capital carried a clear message. It said that Islam had a public place again.

At the same time, Kazakhstan describes itself as a secular state with many faiths. Astana is home to Orthodox churches, a Catholic cathedral, a synagogue, and a pyramid-shaped building for interfaith gatherings. So Nur-Astana stands as one part of a wider picture. It is a working mosque for the city's Muslims. It is also a place that visitors of any background can respectfully enter and see.

A few words on etiquette. You will be asked to remove your shoes before the prayer hall. Women are usually expected to cover their hair, and both men and women should cover their arms and legs. Speak softly, and do not walk directly in front of someone who is praying. If you arrive during a prayer, wait at the back. The calm of the space is part of what you came to see.

In winter, this contrast is even stronger. Astana is one of the coldest capitals in the world. Outside, the air can be bitterly cold, and the snow squeaks under your boots. Inside, the warmth and the soft carpet feel like shelter. In summer the light is different. Long evenings throw the minarets into sharp outline against a wide steppe sky.

Before you leave, stand outside once more and turn back to face the mosque. Notice how the four towers frame the dome from every angle. Notice the new apartment blocks and offices that now surround it, many built after the mosque itself. Then listen again. The traffic returns, the wind returns. And somewhere behind you, the quiet room carries on as it does every day.

Practical tips
