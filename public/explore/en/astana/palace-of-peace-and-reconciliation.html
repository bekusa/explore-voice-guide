The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the slope and look up. The wind on Astana's left bank carries a dry, open feel, the air of the steppe that never quite leaves this city. In front of you a pyramid rises from a grassy mound. Its sides are pale stone and glass, and its tip catches the sky. On a clear day the upper triangle glows with colour, almost as if someone has lit a lamp inside the mountain.

This is the Palace of Peace and Reconciliation. Many locals simply call it the Pyramid. It is one of the most recognisable buildings in Kazakhstan's capital. Yet its purpose is less obvious than its shape. This is not a tomb, a temple, or a royal residence. It was built as a meeting place, a neutral house where the world's religions could sit at one table.

To understand why, it helps to know a little about the city itself. Kazakhstan moved its capital from Almaty to this northern steppe city in 1997. What followed was an enormous building programme. New ministries, towers, and monuments appeared within a few years. The country wanted its new capital to say something about national identity — and about the kind of state it hoped to be.

Kazakhstan is home to many ethnic groups and faiths. Islam and Orthodox Christianity are the largest, but there are many smaller communities too. In the early 2000s the government began hosting a gathering called the Congress of Leaders of World and Traditional Religions. Clerics, scholars, and religious leaders from around the world came to Astana to talk. The pyramid was designed as a permanent home for that conversation.

The design came from Foster and Partners, the London architecture practice led by Norman Foster. The same firm later designed the large tent-shaped shopping centre called Khan Shatyr, also here in Astana. The pyramid opened in 2006. Its geometry is simple on purpose. It is commonly described as about sixty-two metres tall, with a square base of roughly the same width. In other words, the building fits almost perfectly inside a cube.

Why a pyramid? Foster's team chose a shape that does not belong to any one faith. A dome might suggest a mosque or a cathedral. A spire might suggest a church. A pyramid is ancient and shared, found in many cultures across many continents. It points upward without preaching. That neutrality was the whole idea... a form that every visitor could read in their own way.

Now look again at the very top. The upper section is filled with stained glass by the British artist Brian Clarke. From inside, the glass shows doves in flight against fields of blue and gold. Here is a detail many visitors miss — there are said to be 130 doves, one for each nationality living in Kazakhstan. Whether you count them or not, the effect is the same. Light falls downward through a cloud of wings.

The building works almost like a vertical journey. Its lowest levels sit partly inside the earth mound you climbed. Down there is a large auditorium used for concerts and opera, with seating for many hundreds of people. This is the second surprise of the pyramid. A building made for solemn religious dialogue also holds a performance hall below ground. Music and diplomacy share the same foundations.

Above the auditorium the interior opens into a tall central atrium. The walls slope inward as they rise, so the space narrows toward the light. Galleries and exhibition rooms circle the edges. Plants have been placed along the terraces, and some guides call them hanging gardens. At the top, under the coloured glass, is the round chamber where religious leaders gather. Its table is circular, so no seat stands above another.

Pay attention to how you move through the building. Some visitors ride lifts that travel at an angle, following the slope of the pyramid's walls. It is a small engineering curiosity, and it changes how the space feels. You are not rising straight up a shaft. You are climbing along the inside of a triangle, with the city slowly spreading out beyond the glass.

Step outside again and look around the hill. The pyramid is part of a wider ceremonial landscape. Nearby stands the Palace of Independence, with its sharp modern lines, and the tall white column of the Kazakh Eli monument on Independence Square. The large Hazret Sultan Mosque is close by as well. Together they form a group of national symbols set on open ground, with wide sky between them.

The city around you has carried more than one name. For a few years it was called Nur-Sultan, before returning to Astana in 2022. Through those changes the pyramid stayed the same. It has hosted the religious congress many times, along with conferences, exhibitions, and cultural events. For many residents it has become a familiar backdrop... a place for wedding photographs, school trips, and evening walks.

Before you leave, stand back from the slope once more. Watch how the stone and glass change as the light moves across the steppe. In the morning the faces look cool and grey. Near sunset they turn warmer, and the coloured tip seems to hold the last of the day. Take a moment here. Listen to the wind, the traffic beyond the park, the voices of other visitors. A simple shape, built for a difficult conversation, still stands above the hill.
