The guide

The story

Listen first. On most days the wind comes straight off the open steppe and moves across this wide boulevard. There are few hills here to slow it down. Then look up. A white lattice of steel rises from the paving, opens into curving branches, and holds a large glass sphere high above the city. When the sun hits that sphere, it can look gold, then amber, then almost copper. In winter the air is cold and dry, and the light can be very sharp.

This is Bayterek, the tower that many Kazakhs treat as the emblem of their capital. The name roughly means a tall poplar, and in Kazakh tradition it also suggests the tree of life. The tower was completed in the early 2000s. It stands at the heart of a district that did not exist in this form a generation ago. To understand why it was built, you need to know one decision that changed the map of Kazakhstan.

In 1997, the country moved its capital from Almaty to this city. Almaty sits in the far southeast, near the mountains and close to the borders. This city sits in the north of the vast steppe. Back then it was a modest regional centre. The move was bold and expensive, and many people doubted it at first. Bayterek was conceived as a symbol of that new beginning. It was a vertical statement that a new capital was rising from open grassland.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. The observation level inside the sphere sits at 97 metres above the ground. The number is deliberate. It refers to 1997, the year the capital moved. So when you ride up later, you are rising to a height that marks a date. Few towers in the world use their own height to record a political decision. This one does.

The shape comes from an old story. Legend has it that a magical bird called Samruk lays a golden egg each year in the crown of the tree of life. The egg holds the hopes and dreams of people. In the tale, a dragon living below the tree eats the egg, and the cycle starts again. The glass sphere at the top is meant to be that golden egg. The branching steel below it is the tree.

This matters because the tower is not just a viewing platform. It turns a piece of steppe folklore into city architecture. Kazakh culture has deep nomadic roots, and stories of birds, trees and the sky appear often in its traditions. When you look at the tower, try to see it the way the designers intended. The trunk is planted firmly in the earth. The branches reach upward. The precious egg is held safely above everything else.

Now take in the setting. Bayterek stands on a long, straight boulevard on the left bank of the Ishim River. This side of the city grew up quickly after the capital moved. The boulevard works like a ceremonial axis. Turn one way and, in the distance, you can see the presidential palace with its blue and white dome. Turn the other way and the view runs toward Khan Shatyr, the huge tent-shaped shopping and entertainment centre. The tower stands roughly in the middle, as a pivot point.

The buildings around you are worth a slow look. Glass towers, golden domes, angular office blocks and sweeping curves all share the same skyline. Some people find this mix exciting. Others find it surprising, even theatrical. Either way, it is rare to stand in a capital where almost everything you can see was built within a few decades. Most capitals grow over centuries. This one was largely drawn, planned and built within living memory.

When you go inside, lifts carry you up through the trunk to the sphere. The ride is short, but the change is complete. Down below, you feel the scale of the boulevard. Up here, you see the full plan of the city at once. On a clear day, the steppe stretches to the horizon. It is flat, wide and pale. The new city looks like an island of glass and steel set into that open land.

Here is the second surprise. Somewhere on the upper level you will find a gold-coloured panel with the imprint of a human hand. It is the handprint of Nursultan Nazarbayev, Kazakhstan's first president, who championed the move of the capital. Locals say that if you place your own palm in the print and make a wish, it may come true. You will often see a short queue of people waiting their turn. Some laugh, some close their eyes, and some are quietly serious.

That ritual brings the old legend and the modern state together. The golden egg in the story holds people's dreams. Inside that egg, visitors now press their hands into a gold panel and make their own wishes. You do not need to believe in it to enjoy watching. Families, students, couples and visitors from across Central Asia all take part. It is one of the most human moments in a place that otherwise feels very formal.

The city's name has also changed during the tower's lifetime. In 2019 the capital was renamed Nur-Sultan, in honour of the first president. In 2022 it went back to Astana, which simply means capital in Kazakh. Through all of that, Bayterek stayed exactly where it is. Names on maps changed, but the tower remained the fixed point that people photographed and arranged to meet beside. In many ways it became the visual shorthand for the city itself.

Before you leave, step back from the base and look up once more. Notice how the white branches catch the light differently from the glass above them. Feel the wind again, coming in from the open land beyond the last buildings. A few decades ago, much of this ground was empty steppe. Now you are standing at the centre of a capital. As you walk away along the boulevard, turn around once or twice. The sphere will keep catching the sun, like an egg still held safely in its tree.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit on a clear day for views to the steppe horizon.
Bring a windproof layer, even on sunny days.
Join the handprint queue early before crowds build.
Walk the boulevard toward Khan Shatyr after your visit.
Return at dusk to see the tower lit up.
