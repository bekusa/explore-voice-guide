The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The ground here is wide, open, and pale, and the wind out on the steppe tends to find you before anything else does. Then lift your eyes. A great dome sits ahead, framed by tall, slender towers, and against the big Kazakh sky the whole building looks almost weightless. Take a slow breath. You are standing in front of one of the newest large religious buildings in Central Asia.

A word about the name first, because it tells a story of its own. This mosque was planned and built during the years when the capital itself was called Nur-Sultan. The city took that name in 2019, in honour of Kazakhstan's first president. In 2022 it returned to the name Astana. So the building carries a trace of a short chapter in the city's life, and you may hear locals use more than one name for it.

The mosque opened in 2022, and it was presented as the largest in Central Asia. That is a claim about scale, and you can feel the scale even before you step inside. Exact figures for its height and capacity vary between sources, so let your eyes do the measuring. Notice how small the people near the entrance look. Notice how far the courtyard stretches before it reaches the walls.

To understand why a building this size exists here, it helps to know the city around it. Astana is young as a capital. It became the seat of government in the late 1990s, when the capital moved north from Almaty. Since then, the government has built a skyline of bold, ambitious buildings on what was once open steppe. This mosque belongs to that same story — a national project, built to be seen.

But it also belongs to a much older story. Islam reached the lands of present-day Kazakhstan many centuries ago, travelling along trade routes and through the towns of the south. For much of the twentieth century, under Soviet rule, religious life was restricted, and many mosques were closed. After independence in 1991, Kazakhstan saw a wave of mosque building. This building is one of the largest results of that revival.

Now look closely at the architecture. The design draws on the classic language of mosque building — a central dome, tall minarets, arches, and an enclosed courtyard. A minaret, by the way, is the tower from which the call to prayer is traditionally given. Here there are four of them, one near each corner, and they frame the dome like the posts of a great tent.

That image of a tent is worth holding on to. Kazakh culture has deep nomadic roots, and the yurt — the round, felt-covered home of the steppe — is a symbol you will meet again and again in this country. Many Kazakh buildings echo its rounded form. As you study the dome, ask yourself whether you see a classical Islamic dome, a yurt roof, or a little of both.

Next, look at the surfaces. The exterior is mostly light in colour, and it changes with the weather. Under a clear sky it can look bright, almost white. On grey days it turns soft and pearly. Look for patterned details around the doors and windows. Kazakh decorative art often uses curling, horn-like motifs drawn from the world of herding, and Islamic art favours geometry and calligraphy. Here the two traditions sit close together.

If you go inside, the mood changes. The noise of the wind drops away, and sound softens under your feet. Prayer halls are usually covered with carpets, and visitors walk on them in socks or bare feet. Look up into the dome. Large chandeliers and painted or carved ornament are common in mosques of this kind, and the light from high windows falls slowly across the floor through the day.

A practical note before you enter. This is a working place of worship, not a museum. Visitors are generally welcome outside prayer times, but modest dress is expected. Shoulders and knees should be covered, and women are usually asked to cover their hair. Shoes come off before the prayer hall. If you are unsure, watch what others do, or ask a staff member at the entrance. A quiet voice and a respectful pace go a long way.

Here is something many visitors miss. A mosque this large is not designed only for ordinary days. Its true scale shows during major religious holidays, such as the festivals at the end of Ramadan, the month of fasting. On those days, worshippers can fill the halls, the courtyard, and the open ground beyond. On a quiet weekday the space can feel almost empty. Both experiences tell you something true about this place.

And here is a second detail worth noticing. Inside, look for the mihrab. That is the decorated niche in the wall that shows the direction of Mecca, the direction of prayer. Every mosque in the world has one, from a village room to a building like this. Find it, and you will understand how the whole hall is arranged. The rows, the carpet lines, the focus of the space — everything turns toward that one small arch.

As you head back outside, turn around once more. Watch how the minarets line up as you move, how the dome slips behind them and then appears again. In the evening, the building is often lit, and the light makes the pale stone glow against the darkening sky. If you hear the call to prayer, stop for a moment and listen. It is the same call heard across the Muslim world, carried here over the open steppe.

So take one last look. A dome shaped by many traditions. Towers rising from flat, windswept ground. A name that belongs to a few short years of the city's history. Feel the wind again on your face, and listen for the quiet that hangs around a large building at rest. Then walk on into Astana, a city still writing its own story — and you have just stood inside one of its biggest new chapters.
