The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under your feet, the wooden boards of the footbridge give a soft, hollow knock with every step. The sea lies on both sides, pale and calm, and the air smells of salt and pine. Somewhere ahead, children are laughing. Through the trees, a tall round house of bright blue rises above the rocks. Welcome to Moomin World, on the small island of Kailo, just off the old town of Naantali in southwest Finland.

This is not a park of roller coasters or loud machines. There are no big rides here at all. Instead, the whole island is built as a living picture book. Paths wind between granite rocks and birch trees. Small houses appear around each bend. The idea is simple and quite rare in the world of theme parks: you walk into a story, and the story moves at the pace of a child. It opened in the early 1990s and has welcomed families every summer since.

The story belongs to Tove Jansson. She was a Finnish artist and writer who belonged to Finland's Swedish-speaking minority, and she wrote her books in Swedish. The first Moomin book appeared in 1945, at the end of the Second World War. Many readers see the shadow of that time in the early stories — floods, comets, families searching for a safe home. The Moomins are round, white, gentle creatures who look a little like hippos. But Jansson always said they were simply trolls.

Here is something many visitors miss. Jansson spent much of her life in this archipelago landscape — the thousands of rocky islands scattered off Finland's southwest coast. She spent summers on small, bare islands in the Gulf of Finland. So when you look around Kailo, at the smooth grey rocks, the low pines and the open water, you are not looking at a set built to imitate Moominvalley. In many ways, you are looking at the kind of place that gave Moominvalley its shape.

Now, turn your eyes to that blue house. This is the Moominhouse, home of the Moomin family, and it is the heart of the island. It is tall and round, with a pointed red roof, a little like a tiled stove. That shape comes straight from the books. Inside, you can climb through several floors. You will find the kitchen where Moominmamma cooks, the bedrooms, and the rooms where the family keeps its collected treasures. Take your time. Children love to open drawers and peer into corners.

As you walk, you will meet the characters themselves. Moomintroll and Snork Maiden often stroll the paths. Moominmamma might be there, with her handbag on her arm. And watch for Little My — the tiny, fierce girl with her hair tied up in a tight knot on top of her head. In the books she is sharp-tongued and fearless. Many adults quietly say she is their favourite. The characters stop for hugs, photos and small moments with the youngest visitors.

One figure is more elusive. Snufkin, the wandering musician in the green hat, loves solitude in the books. He leaves Moominvalley every autumn and returns each spring. In the stories, he plays the harmonica and owns almost nothing, because he believes that owning things makes life heavy. Many grown-up readers find his quiet freedom more moving as they get older. If you spot him on the island, notice how different his mood feels from the cheerful bustle around the Moominhouse.

Around the island, smaller homes and corners belong to other characters. Keep an eye out for the Hemulen, a serious collector who wears a dress inherited from an aunt. Look for traces of the Groke, a lonely, cold creature who freezes the ground wherever she sits. In the books she frightens everyone. But later readers often see her with sympathy — she only wants warmth and company. This is typical of Jansson. Even her scary characters have a sad, human side.

Theatre is a big part of a visit here. Short performances take place during the day, with songs, music and familiar characters. In one of the books, the Moomins end up living in a floating theatre after a flood, and they learn about drama from a grumpy old theatre rat named Emma. That love of the stage carries through to the island today. The shows are simple, playful and easy to follow, even if you do not understand every word.

Here is a second surprise. Moomin World is mostly a summer place. For much of the year, the island rests and the paths are quiet. It opens mainly during the long, light Finnish summer, when the sun barely sets in June. In some years there has also been a short winter season. So if you are standing here now among other families, you are part of a brief, bright window — a few warm months when the valley comes alive.

Notice how much of the experience is about doing, not watching. Children climb, explore small huts, follow paths into woods and play in open spaces. There are places to sit by the water and simply rest. Parents often find that the pace slows down here. Nobody rushes from ride to ride. That gentle rhythm fits the books well. In Moominvalley, a good day might be nothing more than a swim, a picnic and a long talk at dusk.

When you leave, the footbridge will carry you back to Naantali itself. It is one of Finland's older towns, which grew up around a convent in the fifteenth century. The old town has low wooden houses painted in soft yellows, reds and greens, along narrow streets that slope down to the harbour. The stone convent church still stands above the water. It is worth a slow walk after the noise and colour of the island.

So before you go, stand still on the rocks for a minute. Feel the breeze off the sea. Look back at the blue tower above the trees, and listen to the voices drifting along the shore. Tove Jansson wrote about a family that always kept its door open to strangers, wanderers and lost creatures. As you cross the bridge back toward town, notice the islands on the horizon. Somewhere out there, it still feels as though a small valley is waiting.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check the season before visiting; it opens mainly in summer.
Wear comfortable shoes for rocky paths and wooden bridges.
Head to the Moominhouse early to avoid long queues.
Catch at least one theatre show during your visit.
Combine your visit with a walk through Naantali's old town.
