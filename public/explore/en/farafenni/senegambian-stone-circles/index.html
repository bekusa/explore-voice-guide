The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. It is hard, reddish, and gritty, a crust of iron-rich earth that colours your shoes within minutes. The air is dry and warm. Around you, in the quiet savanna, stand rough pillars of rust-coloured stone. They are arranged in rings, some no taller than your knee, others well above your head. Nobody hurries here. The only sounds are insects, the wind in dry grass, and perhaps a hornbill calling from a distant tree.

You are standing among the Senegambian stone circles. This is one of the densest groupings of standing stones anywhere on Earth. In 2006, UNESCO listed them as a single World Heritage Site. The listing covers four main groups. Two, Sine Ngayène and Wanar, lie in Senegal. Two, Wassu and Kerbatch, lie here in The Gambia, on the north bank of the river. Together they mark a wide band of land that follows the Gambia River across hundreds of kilometres.

The scale is easy to underestimate. Within this zone, archaeologists have recorded more than a thousand stone circles, plus many burial mounds and single standing stones. Most people know Stonehenge in England. Very few people know that West Africa holds circles in far greater numbers. That is the first surprise of this place. It is not one monument. It is a whole landscape of monuments, spread across villages, farmland, and open bush.

Now look closely at the stone itself. It is laterite, a rock that forms in hot, wet climates when iron and aluminium build up in the soil. When it is freshly cut, laterite is relatively soft. When it is exposed to air, it hardens. The builders understood this. They cut the pillars with iron tools, and old quarries have been found close to some of the circles. Run your eye down a stone and you may see the pitted, bubbly texture typical of laterite.

The pillars were shaped with care. Many are cylinders. Others are square-sided, or taper slightly toward the top. Some have flat tops, and a few have a small hollow or a rounded cap. These are not boulders dragged into place. Each one was quarried, dressed, moved, and set upright in the ground. The heaviest stones weigh several tonnes. Imagine the coordination that took, with no machines, in this heat.

Who made them? The honest answer is that we do not know their names. No written records survive from the builders. Archaeological dating places the circles across a very long span, roughly from the third century before Christ to the sixteenth century after Christ. So these stones were not the work of one generation. Many generations of people, over well over a thousand years, kept returning to the same idea of the stone ring.

Here is the second surprise. These circles are not only ceremonial spaces. Many of them mark graves. Excavations beneath and beside the stones have uncovered human remains, along with iron objects, pottery, and personal ornaments such as bracelets and beads. So when you stand at the edge of a circle, you may be standing at the edge of a burial place. That is why a respectful pace, and a quiet voice, feel natural here.

Wassu is the group most visitors reach first. Its stones are among the tallest in the whole region, and they stand close together in clear, readable rings. A small museum stands beside the site. Inside, simple displays explain how the stones were cut, what the excavations found, and how researchers think the circles were used. It is worth a few minutes before you walk among the stones, because it gives you a frame for everything you see outside.

Kerbatch has a different character. It is quieter and feels more remote. Its best-known feature is a stone split into a V shape, often called a lyre stone, because the two arms rise like the frame of a harp or lyre. Lyre stones are rare across the whole region. Archaeologists still debate their meaning. Some suggest they marked a special burial or a family link, but no one can say for certain.

As you walk, notice that many circles are not perfectly alone. Look for stones set slightly outside the ring, often on its eastern side. Researchers call these frontal stones. Their exact purpose is unknown, but their careful placement shows that the builders followed shared rules. The same patterns repeat from site to site, across a wide area. This suggests a common culture, with common beliefs about death and memory, along this stretch of the river.

You may also notice small pebbles balanced on top of some pillars. This is a living local custom, not an ancient one. Visitors and local people sometimes place a pebble on a stone and make a wish. Guides say that if the pebble stays, the wish may come true. Treat it as a gentle tradition, not a historical fact. If you join in, place your pebble lightly, and never climb or lean on the stones.

Lift your eyes now to the wider scene. The circles sit in open country of grass, scattered trees, and farmland. Baobabs, with their thick grey trunks, appear in the distance. In the dry season, the land turns gold and the dust hangs in the air. In the rainy season, everything turns green and the red stones stand out sharply. The river itself is never far away, and it shaped where people lived and where they buried their dead.

Farafenni, on the north bank, is a practical base for reaching this part of the country. The road east toward the circles passes through small villages, markets, and fields of groundnuts and millet. Life along that road continues much as it has for generations. Families farm, children walk to school, and herders move cattle across the land. The stones are part of this everyday world, not separated from it behind high fences.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in the centre of the open ground. Look at the nearest ring. Count the stones, if you like. Notice how the light falls on their rough red surfaces, and how their shadows stretch across the earth. People stood here many centuries ago, setting each pillar for someone they wanted to remember. As you walk back toward the road, the red dust on your shoes is the same earth they shaped.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Wassu museum before walking among the stones.
Come early morning to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Bring water, a hat, and sun protection.
Never climb, sit on, or lean against the stones.
Hire a local guide to explain the excavation finds.
