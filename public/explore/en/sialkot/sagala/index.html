The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. A motorbike horn, a cart wheel on gravel, water moving slowly through a canal at the edge of a field. The ground here is flat and soft, the fine silt that rivers have laid down over thousands of years. The light over the Punjab plain is wide and pale, often hazy with dust. This is the country around Sambrial. Somewhere in this landscape, layered under fields and towns, lies one of the oldest city names in South Asia: Sagala.

First, an honest word about what you are looking for. Sagala is not a single building with a gate and a ticket booth. It is an ancient city, and most historians identify it with the area of present-day Sialkot, a short drive east of here. The old city lies largely beneath the modern streets. So this guide is less about one monument and more about a place that people have kept living in, rebuilding, and renaming for well over two thousand years.

The oldest stories about Sagala come from the Mahabharata, the great Sanskrit epic of ancient India. In the epic, a city called Sakala is the capital of the Madra kingdom. Its king, Shalya, is a famous warrior who fights in the epic's great war. Remember that this is epic poetry, not a historical record. But it tells us something real. Very early on, people in the subcontinent already knew this corner of the Punjab as the seat of a kingdom.

Next comes a story with Greek sources behind it. In 326 BCE, Alexander the Great marched through the Punjab. The Greek historian Arrian describes a siege at a city he calls Sangala, defended by a people who circled their wagons around a hill. Many writers have linked Sangala with Sagala. Others place it at a different site entirely. So treat this connection as possible, not proven. Still, the image is hard to forget — Macedonian soldiers on this same flat plain.

Alexander did not stay, but Greek influence did. After his death, kings of Greek descent ruled in Bactria, in today's Afghanistan, and pushed south and east. In the second century before the common era, one of these Indo-Greek kings, Menander, ruled from Sagala. His coins carry Greek writing on one side and an Indian script on the other. Think about that for a moment. A Greek-speaking court once held power here, in the middle of the Punjab.

Here is the surprise most visitors miss. Menander lives on in a Buddhist book. The text is called the Milindapanha, or the Questions of King Milinda, and Milinda is the Indian form of Menander's name. It records a long conversation between the king and a Buddhist monk named Nagasena. The book opens with a description of Sagala itself, praising its parks, gardens, markets, and well-planned streets. Few cities anywhere have a portrait like that, written so long ago.

The most famous moment in that conversation is about a chariot. Nagasena asks the king: what exactly is a chariot? Is it the wheels? The axle? The frame? None of them alone, the king admits. The chariot is only a name for all the parts together. And so, the monk says, is a person. It is a simple question, asked by a Greek king and an Indian monk, in a city that once stood near where you are now.

Sagala did not fade after the Greeks. In the sixth century, a Huna ruler named Mihirakula is said to have made Sakala his capital. The Huna were Central Asian invaders who swept into northern India in that period. Later, the Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Xuanzang travelled through the region in the seventh century. His account is generally understood to describe the old city as already largely in ruins. So by then, Sagala was already ancient.

Why did a city rise here at all? Look at the land. Sambrial sits in the Rechna Doab. A doab is the land between two rivers, here the Ravi and the Chenab. The Chenab runs not far to the west. To the northeast, on clear days, the first hills toward Jammu and Kashmir can appear on the horizon. Fertile soil, water, and routes between the plains and the mountains — that combination has drawn people here for thousands of years.

Today, the energy of this area belongs to work. Sialkot is known around the world for making sports goods, especially footballs, along with surgical instruments and leather. Small workshops line many of its lanes. Sambrial itself is a working town of shops, rice fields, and traffic on the road between Sialkot and Wazirabad. Sialkot's international airport is also nearby. The ancient city of markets described in the Buddhist text has become a modern city of makers.

It helps to know how old cities survive in a place like this. On river plains, towns are often built on top of earlier towns. Mud brick crumbles, new houses go up on the same ground, and over centuries the land rises into low mounds. Little of ancient Sagala has been excavated or put on public display. So if you notice raised ground or an old lane that follows a strange curve, you may simply be looking at the slow shape of history.

If you have time, spend it the way people here do. Stop at a tea stall and order a cup of sweet milky chai. Listen to the call to prayer drift across the rooftops at dusk. Ask an older resident what they know about the old city. Local stories may not match the history books, and that is fine. Every generation has added its own layer to the name.

Names are the strongest thread here. Sakala, Sagala, Sangala, and perhaps Sialkot itself — scholars still debate how these names connect. But the idea of a city in this place has lasted longer than any wall, palace, or temple. Kings came from Greece and Central Asia. Monks argued about the nature of the self. Pilgrims arrived to find ruins. And people kept farming the same rich soil.

Before you leave, look around once more. Notice the green or golden fields, depending on the season. Notice the dust in the air and the straight lines of the canals. Watch a bicycle or a cart pass along the road. None of it looks ancient. But you are standing in a landscape that a Greek king, a Buddhist monk, and an epic poet all knew by name. Carry that thought with you as you walk on.
