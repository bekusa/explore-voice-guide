The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the ground under your shoes. It is soft, pale, and powdery, a mix of dust and broken mud brick. A dry wind moves through the date palms nearby. Somewhere beneath this low, uneven earth lies the heart of ancient Babylon. You are standing near Esagila, the great temple of the god Marduk, the most sacred building in one of the oldest cities in the world.

The name comes from Sumerian, the ancient language of southern Mesopotamia, the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. Esagila is usually translated as the house whose top is raised high. For the people of Babylon, this was the home of their patron god on earth. Marduk was believed to live here, in the form of a cult statue. Priests fed, clothed, and cared for that statue every day.

Here is the first surprise. Most of what you came to see is not visible. Esagila lies deep below the surface, buried under many metres of later debris, silt, and settlement. Over the centuries, layer after layer built up on top of it. Today the area appears as a low mound. The temple is still here — it is simply underground, beneath your feet.

The temple was not built once. It was rebuilt again and again over a very long time. Many Babylonian kings claimed the honour of restoring it. The most famous of them was Nebuchadnezzar the Second, who ruled in the sixth century before Christ. His inscriptions describe how he decorated the temple with gold, precious woods, and fine materials. He wanted his capital to be the centre of the world, and Esagila was its spiritual centre.

Just to the north stood a second giant structure, the ziggurat called Etemenanki. A ziggurat is a stepped temple tower made of mud brick. Etemenanki and Esagila belonged together, part of one sacred complex for Marduk. Many historians connect this tower with the biblical story of the Tower of Babel. The tower is gone now, but its outline in the ground can still be traced by visitors who know where to look.

The most important event of the year at Esagila was the New Year festival, called the Akitu. It took place in spring. During the festival, the statue of Marduk left the temple and travelled in a procession along the Processional Way to a special festival house outside the city. Priests recited the Enuma Elish, the Babylonian creation story. In that poem, Marduk defeats the forces of chaos and becomes king of the gods.

The second surprise concerns the king himself. During the festival, according to ritual texts, the king of Babylon was humbled before the god. A priest removed his royal symbols and struck him on the cheek. If tears came to the king's eyes, it was taken as a good sign. Only after this ritual was his kingship renewed for another year. Even the most powerful man in the city knelt here.

Esagila also lived through violence. In 689 BC, the Assyrian king Sennacherib attacked Babylon and caused great destruction. Later Assyrian kings, including his son Esarhaddon, worked to restore the city and its temple. The Babylonians believed that the statue of Marduk could be carried away by enemies, and its absence was a disaster for the whole land. Its return was a cause for celebration.

The temple outlived the Babylonian empire itself. After the Persian conquest, and later under Alexander the Great and the Greek kings who followed him, Esagila continued to function. Alexander died in Babylon in 323 BC, and ancient writers say he planned major works on the sacred complex. Scholars attached to the temple kept writing in cuneiform, the wedge-shaped script pressed into clay, for centuries after it had disappeared elsewhere.

Those scholars left a remarkable legacy. Babylonian astronomers recorded the movements of the moon, the planets, and the stars, night after night, over many generations. They also noted weather, river levels, and prices in the market. These records, known today as the astronomical diaries, are among the longest scientific observation projects in human history. Some of this careful work is connected to the temple community here.

The modern rediscovery came at the very end of the nineteenth century. A German team led by the archaeologist Robert Koldewey excavated Babylon for many years, starting in 1899. Because Esagila lay so deep, the team had to dig shafts and tunnels to reach its walls. Groundwater made the work difficult. Much of the temple remains unexcavated, and its full plan is still not completely known. In 2019, Babylon was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list.

As you walk the site, notice the bricks around you. Some are ancient, baked in kilns and stamped with royal inscriptions. Others are modern, from reconstruction work carried out in the 1980s. The newer bricks are often cleaner and more regular. Learning to tell the two apart changes how you see the whole city. It is a quiet reminder that every age has tried to shape Babylon in its own image.

Now look around you once more. The palms move in the wind. The light falls flat and bright across the mound. Below you, in the dark, lie the walls where priests sang to their god, where kings knelt, and where astronomers watched the sky. As you walk away, listen to the crunch of the earth under your feet... and remember what still rests beneath it.
