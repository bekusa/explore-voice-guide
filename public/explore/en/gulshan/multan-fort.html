The guide

The story

Under your feet lies a hill that people made. Layer by layer, over thousands of years, houses rose and fell on this spot beside a river. Their bricks and dust piled into a mound, and the mound became a fortress. Multan Fort is not just a building. It is time itself, stacked high above the plain.

Multan is among the oldest living cities in South Asia. Writers in Persia, Greece, and China mentioned it under different names. Many scholars trace the name to Mulasthana, linked to the sun god once worshipped here. The citadel on this mound guarded a crossing of the Ravi River, which then flowed against these walls. Rivers move slowly but completely. The Ravi drifted away centuries ago, and today the Chenab passes west of the city. The fort stayed behind.

One famous story clings to these slopes. Around 325 BC, Alexander the Great fought the Malli people of this region. During the storming of their citadel, an arrow struck him in the chest, and he nearly died. Tradition places that moment here, on this mound. Historians still argue about the exact spot. Multan itself has no doubts at all.

For centuries the mound held one of the most famous temples in Asia: the Sun Temple of Multan. The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang described it in the seventh century, with its great image and crowds of pilgrims. Arab travelers wrote that its wealth astonished them. The temple vanished in the wars of the tenth century, but its fame survives in those old pages.

The Islamic centuries turned the mound into a walled fortress. At its height, the fort had thick brick walls, dozens of round bastions, and four great gates. Inside stood palaces, a mint, a mosque, and deep wells. Armies from Ghazni, Delhi, Persia, and the Sikh kingdom all fought for this ground, because whoever held Multan held the road between Central Asia and India.

The fort's last battle destroyed it. In 1848, the local governor Mulraj rebelled against British control, and a British army besieged the city for months. In December 1848, a shell struck the fort's great mosque, which was being used as a powder store. The explosion was heard far across the plain. The fort surrendered in January 1849, and afterward the British leveled most of the walls so it could never resist again.

You reach the top today by a gentle road, and the climb takes only minutes. Notice how the ground rises in broad steps. Each terrace hides the rubble of an older Multan. Coins, seals, and pottery found in these slopes span more than 2,000 years of city life. Archaeologists read this hill the way others read a book.

So you arrive not in a castle but in an open park. The space inside the vanished walls is now Qasim Bagh. Families picnic under the trees. A cricket stadium curves where garrisons once stood. On the north and east sides, stretches of wall and a few heavy bastions survive, their brick worn by salt and wind.

Walk the surviving wall for a while. The brick courses are enormous up close, and weeds grow from the old gun slits. Lizards sun themselves where sentries once paced. It is quiet now, which may be the strangest thing a fortress can become.

The mound's greatest treasures outlived every siege: its tombs. The mausoleum of Bahauddin Zakariya stands near one edge, and the grand dome of Shah Rukn-e-Alam rises at another. Soldiers and empires came and went. The saints stayed. Locals will tell you, with a smile, that it was never the walls protecting this hill.

Walk to the Damdama, the raised platform at the high point of the mound. From there the whole of Multan spreads below you: the tight lanes of the walled city, blue-tiled domes, minarets, rooftops crowded with water tanks. On a clear winter day, the view runs far across the plain.

Evening is the fort's best hour. The heat loosens its grip. Paper kites and pigeon flocks climb into the pink air, flown from rooftops below. Food carts at the base of the mound sell roasted chickpeas and pressed sugarcane juice. The call to prayer rises from many mosques at once, overlapping like ripples on water.

Fortresses usually tell stories about power. This one tells a longer story: how walls fall, rivers wander, temples vanish, and a city simply continues. People built this hill out of their own lives, one layer at a time. Standing on top of it, you are standing on all of them.
