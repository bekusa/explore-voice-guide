The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, you will hear the forest. The engine cuts out, and the dust settles on your sleeves. Somewhere a peacock calls, sharp and metallic. Dry leaves crackle under something you cannot see. The air smells of warm earth and crushed grass. Out here, in the eastern edge of Rajasthan, silence is never empty. Every sound may be a message, and your guide is already listening for one.

You are in Ranthambore National Park, near the town of Sawai Madhopur. Two old landscapes meet here. The Aravalli hills, some of the oldest mountains in India, meet the Vindhya plateau to the south. The result is a broken country of rocky ridges, flat-topped hills, deep ravines and sudden lakes. Two rivers frame the wider reserve, the Chambal and the Banas. It is rough ground, and that roughness is part of why wildlife survived here.

Look at the trees around you. Much of this forest is dry deciduous woodland, which means many trees drop their leaves in the hot, dry months. The most common tree here is the dhok, small and grey-barked, with twisting branches. In the dry season the forest turns gold and brown, and the views open up. In the greener months after the rains, the same hills look soft and lush. The light changes everything. Early morning paints the rocks a pale pink.

For centuries, this land belonged to kings. The forests around the fort were the private hunting grounds of the Maharajas of Jaipur. Royal hunts brought visiting dignitaries, elephants and long lines of beaters. That history is strange to think about now. The same protection that kept ordinary people out also kept the forest standing. When India began to build a modern conservation system, Ranthambore already had large wooded areas still intact.

The change came in stages. The area was declared a game sanctuary in 1955. In 1973 it became one of the first reserves under Project Tiger, India's national programme to save its wild tigers. In 1980 it was declared a national park. Villages inside the core area were relocated over the following years. That process was difficult and is still debated. It is part of the honest story of this place, not only a footnote.

One name comes up again and again here. Fateh Singh Rathore served as field director during those early years, and he is widely credited with bringing the tigers back. Before protection, tigers were shy and mostly moved at night. As the forest grew safer, they began to appear in daylight. That change is a big reason why Ranthambore became known around the world. Tigers walking calmly beside lakes, in full sun, turned this park into a place photographers dreamed about.

The most famous of those tigers was a female called Machli. Her name means fish, from the fish-shaped marking on her face. She ruled the lake area for many years and raised several litters. Visitors and filmmakers followed her for most of her life. Many people say she was one of the most photographed tigers anywhere. She died in 2016, at an unusually old age for a wild tiger. Many of the tigers in the park today belong to her extended family.

Now, a word about seeing a tiger. It may happen, and it may not. Ranthambore has a strong reputation, but these are wild animals in a large forest. Safaris move along fixed routes, divided into zones. Your vehicle may be an open jeep, called a gypsy, or a larger open truck, called a canter. The guides read the forest closely. They watch the dust for fresh pugmarks, the paw prints of a big cat. They listen for warnings from other animals.

Here is something many visitors miss. The forest has an alarm system. When a spotted deer sees a predator, it gives a short, high bark. A sambar, the large dark deer of these woods, gives a deep, honking call. Langur monkeys shout warnings from the treetops. When you hear one of these calls, your guide will usually stop and wait. Sometimes nothing happens. Sometimes, a few minutes later, a striped shape walks out of the grass.

There is far more to see than tigers. Leopards live here, usually in the rockier, higher areas. Sloth bears, shaggy and black, forage for termites. Mugger crocodiles rest on the banks of the lakes, motionless in the sun. Nilgai, the large blue-grey antelope of India, graze in open patches. And the birds are everywhere. Kingfishers, eagles, storks and painted spurfowl. A slow morning with no tiger can still be full of life.

The lakes are the heart of the park. Padam Talao, Rajbagh Talao and Malik Talao hold water through much of the year. Animals gather around them, especially as the land dries out. Near Padam Talao stands Jogi Mahal, a former royal hunting lodge painted in pale colours. Beside it grows a huge banyan tree. Its branches drop roots down to the ground, and those roots slowly become new trunks. Locals say it is one of the largest banyan trees in India.

And then there is the fort. Look up at the ridgeline and you may see long stone walls following the edge of the cliffs. This is Ranthambore Fort, a Rajput stronghold built over many centuries. It saw famous sieges, including one in the early fourteenth century by the armies of Alauddin Khalji, the Sultan of Delhi. In 2013 it was listed by UNESCO as one of the Hill Forts of Rajasthan. Inside, a temple to the god Ganesh draws a constant stream of pilgrims.

That temple holds a lovely custom. Families from across India send wedding invitations to Ganesh here, by ordinary post. Ganesh is the god of beginnings, and inviting him first is seen as a blessing. Letters arrive in large numbers, and are read out at the temple. It is a reminder that this is not only a wilderness. It is a living, sacred landscape too. Monkeys, pilgrims, tigers and old stone walls all share the same hills.

Before you leave, pause for a moment. Look at the ground beside the track. Notice the fine dust, the tracks of deer, maybe a paw print pressed into the soft earth. Listen again for the peacock, the barking deer, the wind in the dry leaves. Whatever you have seen today, this forest has watched you too. As the vehicle starts up and rolls away, keep looking back at the ridges. The fort walls catch the light, and the forest closes behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book your safari well ahead, since zones are allocated.
Wear muted earth-toned clothing to blend into the forest.
Bring warm layers for cold winter dawn safaris.
Stay quiet when the guide stops to listen.
Carry binoculars for distant birds and big cats.
