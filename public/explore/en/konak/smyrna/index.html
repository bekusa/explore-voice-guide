The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The ground here is uneven, with packed earth, worn marble, and the edges of old paving stones. Somewhere beyond the fences, traffic hums through Konak. Yet inside this open pit of columns and arches, the city noise sounds farther away. Pale stone catches the Aegean light, and lizards move across blocks that were carved almost two thousand years ago. You are standing in the heart of ancient Smyrna, in what remains of its agora.

An agora was the public square of a Greek city. It was the place for markets, law courts, speeches, gossip, and business. Think of it as a town hall, a shopping street, and a meeting point, all in one open space. Here in Smyrna, the agora was framed by long covered walkways called stoas. Those are rows of columns with a roof, where people could walk in the shade. Some of those columns stand again around you today.

The name Smyrna is very old, and it has travelled with this coastline for thousands of years. The first settlement was not here, though. Old Smyrna stood on a small hill at the northern end of the bay, in today's district of Bayraklı. That earlier city is one of the places that later Greeks claimed as the birthplace of Homer, the poet of the Iliad and the Odyssey. Several cities made that claim, so treat it as an ancient tradition rather than settled fact.

The Smyrna you see here is the second city. It was refounded in the fourth century BCE on the slopes of Mount Pagos — the hill that rises behind you, now called Kadifekale. A famous story tells how it happened. According to the ancient writer Pausanias, Alexander the Great fell asleep while hunting on the mountain. In a dream, goddesses told him to found a new city there. It is a legend, but the people of Smyrna told it with pride for centuries.

Under Roman rule, Smyrna grew into one of the great cities of Asia Minor, the region we now call Anatolia. It had a deep harbour, rich trade, and a reputation for beauty. Then, in 178 CE, a severe earthquake struck. Much of the city, including this agora, was badly damaged. Ancient sources say the emperor Marcus Aurelius supported the rebuilding. Most of the structures you see around you belong to that second-century reconstruction.

Now look for the arches, and one carved face above them. On an arch of the western side, a woman's portrait in relief looks out over the square. She is usually identified as Faustina the Younger, the wife of Marcus Aurelius. Many visitors walk past without noticing her. For a city that owed its recovery to the imperial family, placing her image here was a public way of saying thank you.

The most surprising part of the site is underground. On the northern side stood a large basilica, a long Roman hall used for law and business. Its lower level survives, with a row of sturdy stone vaults. Walk into that cool, dim space if it is open. The temperature drops, and the light becomes soft and grey. This basement once supported the grand hall above, and it also held shops and storage.

Here is the second surprise. On the plaster walls of the basilica, archaeologists found ancient graffiti. Ordinary people scratched words, drawings, and messages into the surface. There are ships, figures, names, and short phrases, some of them very personal. Scholars count it among the richest collections of Roman graffiti in this region. It is the closest you will come to hearing the voices of everyday people in Smyrna — not emperors, but shopkeepers, sailors, and passers-by.

Listen, too, for water. In parts of the basement, channels still carry water from natural sources under the hill. Smyrna was always a city shaped by springs, aqueducts, and the sea. On a quiet day, the trickle sounds very clear under the vaults. It is a small, living link between the Roman city and the modern one that grew over it.

The agora was not always open to the sky like this. Over the centuries, earth and later buildings covered it. In the Ottoman period, part of the area became a Muslim cemetery. As you walk, look for carved Ottoman gravestones gathered on the site. Systematic excavation began in the 1930s, and new digging in recent decades has uncovered more of the basilica and the stoas. The work continues, so the site you see is still changing.

Some of the finest finds are no longer here. Statues of the sea god Poseidon and the goddess Demeter were discovered in the agora. They are now displayed in the İzmir Archaeology Museum, not far away. If you visit the museum after this, you can picture them back in this square, standing among the crowds, the merchants, and the officials who once filled it.

Step back and look at the setting. Above you is the hill of Kadifekale, with the remains of its castle walls. Below and around you are the dense streets of Konak. The old Kemeraltı market district lies a short walk away, with its narrow lanes, copper shops, and coffee smells. Smyrna's later history was turbulent, including the great fire of 1922 that destroyed much of the city. Yet trade and street life never really left this part of town.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment between the columns. Notice how the light moves across the marble, how the street sounds return at the edges, and how the hill rises behind the stones. People have bought, argued, prayed, and written their names on walls here for many centuries. As you walk back into the city, look at the shop fronts and the crowds. In a quiet way, the agora is still at work.
