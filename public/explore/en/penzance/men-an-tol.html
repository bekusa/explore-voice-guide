The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the heather. A skylark calls somewhere overhead, too high to see. Under your boots the path is soft and peaty, broken by grey granite that pushes up through the turf. Then the moor opens a little, and there it is: a flat stone ring standing on its edge, with a single upright stone on either side. It is smaller than many people expect. It is also stranger, the longer you look at it.

This is Mên-an-Tol. The name is Cornish, the old Celtic language of this peninsula, and it means simply "the stone of the hole". You are in West Penwith, the far western tip of Cornwall, a few miles northwest of Penzance. This is some of the most ancient landscape in Britain. Fields here still follow boundaries that farmers laid out thousands of years ago, and the moor around you is scattered with tombs, standing stones and old mine workings.

Look at the central stone. It is a disc of local granite, set upright, with a round hole cut cleanly through the middle. The hole is wide enough for an adult to crawl through, if they do not mind a little mud. The edges are smooth, worn by weather and by countless hands and shoulders. Granite is a hard stone to shape. Someone chose to make this opening, and it took real effort with simple tools.

How old is it? The honest answer is that nobody knows exactly. Most archaeologists place it in the late Neolithic or the Bronze Age, the long era before writing arrived in Britain. That puts it somewhere around four thousand years old, give or take many centuries. There is no inscription and no written record from the people who raised it. What survives is the stone itself, and the landscape it sits in.

Here is the first surprise. What you see today may not be the original arrangement. Old drawings and descriptions suggest the stones have been moved over time. Some researchers believe the holed stone was once part of a larger stone circle, and that other stones still lie hidden in the grass or were carried away. Look closely around the site. A few low, flat stones sit near the edges, easy to miss.

Others have a different idea. They suggest the holed stone may once have been the entrance to a burial chamber, a kind of doorway into a tomb. Holed stones like this were used in some ancient tombs elsewhere in Europe. Neither theory is proven. That uncertainty is part of the place. You are standing in front of something people have puzzled over for at least three hundred years.

One of the first people to write about it carefully was William Borlase, an eighteenth-century Cornish vicar and antiquary, a scholar who studied ancient remains. He recorded the stones and the beliefs local people held about them. Thanks to writers like him, we know that by his time the stone was already wrapped in ritual. Those rituals are the real story of Mên-an-Tol.

Legend has it that the hole could cure illness. According to local tradition, children with rickets, a disease that softens the bones, were passed naked through the opening, sometimes three times, sometimes nine. Adults with back pain were told to crawl through it too. This gave the monument another local name: the Crick Stone, because it could cure a crick in the back. Locals say the direction you moved mattered, though versions of the tale disagree.

There are other stories too. One popular tale says that a woman who wished for a child should pass through the stone at full moon. Another old belief says that if you laid two brass pins crossed on top of the stone, they would move by themselves to answer a question. These are folklore, not history. But they show how this stone stayed part of people's lives long after its builders were forgotten.

The second surprise is how many neighbours this stone has. Mên-an-Tol is one of a group of ancient sites within walking distance. A short way along the moor stands Men Scryfa, an inscribed stone carved with Latin letters from the early medieval period. Not far beyond is the Nine Maidens stone circle. And Lanyon Quoit, a giant tabletop tomb, is only a short drive away on the road toward Madron.

Now lift your eyes from the stones to the skyline. On a nearby ridge you may see the roofless shape of an old engine house, the tall stone building that once held a steam pump for a tin mine. Mining shaped this landscape for centuries. So the view from here holds two very different pasts: prehistoric ritual close by, and industrial Cornwall on the horizon.

The moor itself changes with the seasons. In late summer the heather turns purple and the gorse glows yellow, with a faint smell of coconut on warm days. In winter the light is low and silver, and the ground can be very wet. Ponies sometimes graze nearby. Mist can roll in fast from the Atlantic, so the stones may appear and vanish within a few minutes.

This is a protected ancient monument, and it is fragile in ways that are easy to forget. Please do not climb on the stones or leave anything behind. Many people still crawl through the hole, following the old tradition. If you do, go gently. These stones have survived thousands of winters, and they depend on each visitor to survive many more.

Before you walk away, stand still for a moment and look through the hole itself. It frames a small circle of moorland, sky and distant hills, like a window with no house around it. Whoever made it saw this same horizon. The wind is still moving through the heather. Take that view with you as you follow the path back down.
