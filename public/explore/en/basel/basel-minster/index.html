The guide

The story

Put your hand flat against the wall for a moment. The stone is red sandstone, rough and slightly warm, the colour of dried earth after rain. Now look up. Above you, a roof of glazed tiles in green, yellow, black and white forms a diamond pattern that catches the light. Somewhere below the terrace you may hear the Rhine moving fast and quiet. This is Basel Minster, and for about a thousand years it has stood on this hill above the river.

The hill matters as much as the church. Long before any cathedral, people chose this high ground above a bend in the Rhine. It gave them a clear view and a natural defence. Archaeologists have found traces of Celtic and Roman settlement under this square. So when you stand here, you stand on one of the oldest inhabited spots in the city. The church came later, and it was built on top of all that earlier life.

A cathedral has stood on this site since early medieval times. The building was consecrated in the early eleventh century, and the Holy Roman Emperor Henry the Second attended the ceremony. Basel never forgot him. He became a patron figure for the church. Later builders gave the minster a Romanesque form, the heavy round-arched style of the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Then, piece by piece, Gothic elements arrived — pointed arches, taller windows, lighter lines.

Then, in 1356, the ground moved. A powerful earthquake struck Basel, and fires spread through the city afterwards. It is often called the strongest earthquake recorded in Central Europe. The minster was badly damaged. Its vaults and parts of its towers fell. What you see today is largely the result of the rebuilding that followed. That is why the church mixes styles so freely. Older Romanesque parts sit beside later Gothic work, like layers in a cliff.

Turn to the main west front, facing the square. Two towers rise above it. The north tower carries the name of Saint George, and the south tower carries the name of Saint Martin. Look for their figures on the facade. Saint George is on horseback, fighting the dragon. Saint Martin is also mounted, and he cuts his cloak in half to share it with a beggar. These are two old stories about courage and generosity, told in stone.

Now for a detail most visitors walk straight past. Near the main portal, look for the figure of a well-dressed man. Locals call him the Prince of the World. From the front, he is elegant and smiling. Walk around to see his back. It is covered with toads and snakes, a sign of decay hidden behind charm. Beside him stands a young woman, one of the Foolish Virgins from a Bible parable. The message is simple: worldly beauty can hide something rotten.

On the same facade, you can also find the emperor himself. Henry the Second stands with his wife, Kunigunde, and he holds a model of a church. It is a medieval way of saying thank you to a founder. Now walk around to the north side of the building. There you will find the Gallus Gate, a Romanesque portal from the twelfth century. Scholars count it among the oldest carved figure portals in the German-speaking world. Look for Christ in judgement above, and the apostles and evangelists around him.

Above the Gallus Gate is a round window built as a wheel of fortune. Small figures climb up one side and fall down the other. Medieval viewers understood the idea at once. Today you may be at the top of the wheel, but tomorrow you might fall. It is a strange thing to place on the side of a church — and a very honest one. Stand back a little to see the whole wheel.

Inside, the light changes. The red stone looks deeper and cooler in the shadows. In 1529, the Reformation reached Basel, and the city became Protestant. Reformers removed or destroyed many statues, altars and paintings. That is why the interior feels quite plain compared with Catholic cathedrals elsewhere. The minster is still a Reformed Protestant church today. Some carved capitals and older stonework survived, and they are worth a slow look.

One of the most famous people buried here was not a bishop or a king. Erasmus of Rotterdam was a Dutch scholar and one of the great thinkers of the European Renaissance. He spent important years in Basel, where the city's printers published his work. He died here in 1536. His memorial is inside the minster. A medieval tomb also remembers Queen Anna, the wife of Rudolf of Habsburg, and their young son. These stones connect this church to the wider history of Europe.

In the fifteenth century, the eyes of Europe turned to Basel again. From 1431, a great church council met in the city. Bishops and theologians came from across Christendom to debate reform and the power of the Pope. Some sessions took place in and around the minster. The council lasted for many years and brought scholars, books and money to Basel. Many historians link it to the founding of the city's university soon afterwards.

Before you leave, find the cloisters next to the church. These are quiet covered walkways around open courtyards. Many old Basel families placed memorial plaques along their walls. Then walk behind the choir to the terrace that locals call the Pfalz. From here, the Rhine runs wide and green below you. On clear days, you can see far across the river, towards the hills of Germany and France. Below the terrace, a small ferry crosses the water. It has no engine. The river current pushes it along a cable.

Stand at the railing for a minute. Listen to the water and the voices in the square behind you. Look back at the coloured tiles and the red towers above the trees. This hill has seen Celts, Romans, emperors, an earthquake and a Reformation. Today it is a place where people sit, talk and watch the river. As you walk away, look back once more. Notice how the red stone glows when the light lands on it.
