The guide

The story

Listen first. On this hillside above the village of Dornach, the city noise of Basel is gone. You hear wind moving through orchard trees, the crunch of gravel under your shoes, perhaps a bell from the valley below. Then you look up, and the building in front of you does not behave like a building at all. Its grey concrete walls lean, fold, and swell. There are almost no straight lines. It looks less like something that was built and more like something that grew out of the hill.

This is the Goetheanum. Strictly speaking, you are no longer in Basel itself. Dornach sits just across the cantonal border in Solothurn, a short tram or train ride from the city centre. But for more than a century, people travelling to Basel have made this trip up the slope. Some come for the architecture. Some come for the theatre. And some come because this is the world centre of a movement called anthroposophy.

So what is anthroposophy? In one sentence, it is a spiritual philosophy founded by the Austrian thinker Rudolf Steiner in the early twentieth century. Steiner believed that human beings could study the spiritual world with the same care that scientists bring to the physical one. His ideas did not stay in books. They shaped Waldorf schools, biodynamic farming, a style of medicine, a form of movement art called eurythmy... and this building. The name honours Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, the German poet whose scientific writings deeply influenced Steiner.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. The building you see is not the original. It is the second Goetheanum. The first one stood on this same spot, and it was made almost entirely of wood. Construction began in 1913. It had two great intersecting domes, carved timber inside and out, and coloured glass windows. Volunteers from many countries came to work on it, even as the First World War raged in the countries around neutral Switzerland.

Then came the night of New Year's Eve, 1922. A fire broke out in the wooden structure. By the morning of the new year, the first Goetheanum was gone. Almost ten years of carving, painting, and devotion had burned in a single night. The cause has long been debated, and arson was widely suspected at the time. What is certain is that Steiner and his followers watched the building they loved disappear in the dark.

Steiner did not give up. Within a short time, he began designing a replacement. This time, the choice of material was deliberate. Instead of wood, he chose reinforced concrete, which was still a fairly new building material for anything this sculptural. Steiner only produced an exterior model before he died in 1925. Others carried the work forward, and the second Goetheanum was completed in 1928. In other words, the man who imagined this building never saw it finished.

Now look at the walls again with that in mind. Concrete is usually poured into flat boxes. Here, the builders had to make wooden formwork in curving, faceted shapes, almost like casting a giant sculpture. Steiner wanted the building to feel alive, to express movement and change rather than rigid order. Walk slowly along the side. Notice how the surfaces bend, how the window frames tilt, how the roof seems to settle down over the structure like a heavy cloak.

This idea has a name: organic architecture. It means design that takes its forms from living nature rather than from geometry. Many architects of the twentieth century were interested in it, but few went this far, this early. Today the Goetheanum is protected as a Swiss heritage site of national importance. Architecture students still come here to sketch it, because it is so different from the concrete buildings that came after it.

Inside, the building opens into a large stage hall, where performances take place. This is the second surprise for many visitors. The Goetheanum is one of the very few places in the world where Goethe's Faust, both parts, is regularly staged in full. That is not a short evening at the theatre. A complete production can stretch across many hours, sometimes spread over more than one day. Eurythmy performances, conferences, and concerts also fill the calendar.

Look for the coloured glass windows when you are inside. Each one is cut from a single colour, with images engraved into the glass so that light passes through it in different depths. When the sun comes through, the hall and stairways take on soft washes of red, green, blue, and violet. The effect changes through the day. On a bright afternoon, the colour on the floor can be as striking as the windows themselves.

There is one more survivor from the story of the fire. Steiner worked with the English sculptor Edith Maryon on a great carved wooden figure called the Representative of Humanity. It was meant for the first building, but it was still in a workshop when the fire came, so it escaped. Today it is kept inside the Goetheanum. It shows a central human figure standing between two opposing forces, a balance that sits at the heart of Steiner's thinking.

When you step back outside, do not leave straight away. The hillside around you is full of smaller buildings in the same unusual style. Look for the boiler house, whose chimney rises in curving shapes like stylised flames or leaves. Look for the private houses with their sloping roofs and odd, rounded windows. Wander the paths past the gardens and meadows. The whole slope was planned as a kind of village around the central building, and much of it is still in use today.

So, take one last look at the Goetheanum from where you are standing now. Notice how the light falls across those folded concrete planes, and how the shadows move as clouds pass over the Jura hills. Whatever you think of Steiner's ideas, this building is a record of an unusual conviction: that a house could be shaped like a living thing. As you walk back down the hill, listen again for the wind, the gravel, and the quiet.

Key facts

Designed by Rudolf Steiner, founder of anthroposophy.
The first wooden Goetheanum burned on New Year's Eve 1922.
The second Goetheanum, in concrete, was completed in 1928.
Steiner died in 1925, before the new building was finished.
Named after the poet and thinker Johann Wolfgang von Goethe.

What to look for

Nearby houses with sloping roofs and rounded windows.
Before you go

Practical tips

Take the tram or train from central Basel to Dornach.
Check the programme before visiting for Faust or eurythmy performances.
Performances usually require a separate ticket booked in advance.
Walk the surrounding hillside to see the related smaller buildings.
Visit on a sunny afternoon to see the coloured windows glow.
