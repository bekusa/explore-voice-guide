The guide

The story

Run your hand along the gatehouse wall. The stone is pale and slightly rough, like hardened sand. In the afternoon it glows a soft honey colour, and close up you can see tiny fragments of shell inside it. This is limestone, cut from the ground on this very hill. The men who cut it were prisoners. They quarried the stone, shaped it, and built the walls that would hold them.

Welcome to Fremantle Prison. It sits on a ridge above the port town of Fremantle, a short journey from the centre of Perth. From the outside, it looks calm. There is a clock above the gate, a neat lawn, and quiet terraced streets around it. But for well over a century, this was one of the most feared places in Western Australia. People who walked through this gate often did not walk out again for many years.

To understand why this prison exists, go back to the middle of the nineteenth century. The young Swan River Colony was struggling. It had land, but it did not have enough workers. So the colonists asked Britain for something most other Australian colonies were trying to stop — convicts. From 1850 until 1868, ships carried thousands of British and Irish prisoners to this coast. They needed somewhere to sleep, and somewhere to be watched.

The answer was the Convict Establishment, the original name for this place. Construction began in the early 1850s, and the convicts did the heavy work themselves. Royal Engineers designed the buildings and supervised the labour. Prisoners dug the limestone out of the hillside, dressed each block by hand, and laid it course by course. When you look at the walls, you are looking at thousands of hours of forced work. Each block was lifted into place by someone who had no choice.

Now look ahead at the Main Cell Block. It is very long and four storeys high, with rows of small windows running along its face. Inside, the cells are tiny. In the early years, many men slept in hammocks rather than beds. Later, two smaller cells were sometimes joined into one, but the space still feels close. Stand inside a cell on a tour and stretch out your arms. Most visitors are surprised by how quickly they touch both walls.

Here is a detail many people miss. This prison did not close in the convict era. It kept working. After transportation ended, it became a regular state prison, and it stayed in use until 1991. So the story here is not only Victorian. Men were locked in these cells during the world wars, through the moon landing, and into the age of colour television. Some guides here worked inside as officers, and some visitors once lived inside as prisoners.

That long life left marks you can still see. Look closely at the cell walls. Over many decades, prisoners covered them with drawings and paintings. Some are careful, detailed pictures. Others are rough sketches, names, or counted days. These images are some of the most personal things in the whole site. They are not official history. They are the private voices of the men who sat here, hour after hour, with only a wall to talk to.

Every old prison has its escape stories, and this one has two famous ones. The first belongs to a man known as Moondyne Joe, a bushranger — an outlaw who lived rough in the Australian bush. He escaped from custody several times. According to a popular tale, authorities built him a specially strengthened cell, and the governor declared that no one could escape it. Joe did get away again. The details have grown in the retelling, so enjoy it as a local legend.

The second escape is better documented, and it reaches across the world. In 1876, six Irish political prisoners, members of a movement called the Fenians, slipped away from work parties outside the walls. An American whaling ship named the Catalpa waited off the coast to collect them. They made it out to sea and sailed to freedom in the United States. In Irish communities, this story is still told with pride today.

The darkest part of the prison is quieter. This was the place of legal execution in Western Australia. The gallows room is small and plain, and visitors often fall silent when they enter. Executions continued here until the 1960s. The last person hanged in the state, Eric Edgar Cooke, died here in 1964. There is no decoration in that room, and none is needed. Take a moment there. It is a heavy place.

Another surprise lies under your feet. Beneath the prison runs a network of tunnels, cut deep into the limestone. Prisoners dug them to reach fresh water for the prison and the town. Today, some of these tunnels are flooded. On a special tour, visitors can climb down ladders and travel through them in small boats. Down there it is cool, dark, and very quiet. Only the sound of dripping water breaks the silence.

The prison's working life ended in drama. In 1988, on a very hot summer day, prisoners rioted and set fires inside the complex. Officers were taken hostage, and parts of the buildings were damaged. The riot sharpened a long debate about whether this old convict jail was fit for modern use. A few years later, in 1991, the last prisoners were moved out to a new facility. The gates closed, and then they opened again — for visitors.

In 2010, Fremantle Prison became part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing called the Australian Convict Sites. This group of eleven places in Australia tells the story of convict transportation. The listing recognises something important. This prison is not just a building. It is evidence of a system that moved people across the world as punishment, and used their labour to build a colony. The prison is one of the most complete convict sites still standing.

Before you leave, walk back to the gatehouse and turn around. Look again at that pale stone, now in whatever light the day has given you. Listen to the street outside — cars, voices, maybe gulls from the harbour below. For the people held here, that ordinary noise was just beyond reach. As you step back through the gate, notice how easy it is to walk out. Very few people inside these walls ever had that freedom.
