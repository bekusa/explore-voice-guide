The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Pine needles shift in the breeze, cicadas buzz in the trees, and somewhere nearby gravel crunches under another visitor's shoes. In front of you, pale columns stand in a curve on a low stepped base. The curve is the first surprise. Almost everything else in this sacred grove follows straight lines, with long temple walls and rectangular treasuries. This building is round. You are standing before the Philippeion, a circular monument that a Macedonian king raised among the gods of Olympia.

Let us place ourselves first. This is the Altis, the walled sanctuary of Zeus at the heart of ancient Olympia. Behind you, or just to the side, is the Temple of Hera, one of the oldest buildings in the sanctuary. The hill of Kronos rises beyond it, green and quiet. The Philippeion sits in this northwest corner, close to the old hearth and the dining hall where victorious athletes were honoured. The position was deliberate. Anyone walking to the oldest temples had to pass this round building.

The story begins with a battle. In 338 BC, Philip the Second of Macedon defeated an alliance of Athens and Thebes at Chaeronea, in central Greece. That victory made him the dominant power over the Greek cities. Soon after, he commissioned this monument at Olympia. The choice of place mattered. Olympia was not a city that belonged to one people. It was a sanctuary for all Greeks, and every four years they gathered here for the Games.

Why would that matter to Philip? Many southern Greeks saw the Macedonians as outsiders, only half Greek at best. Philip had already sent horses and chariots to compete here, and his teams had won. A building inside the Altis made a stronger statement. It said that the Macedonian royal house stood among the Greeks, and that it stood near the gods. Philip did not live to see it finished. He was murdered in 336 BC, and the work is usually thought to have been completed under his son, Alexander.

Now look at the shape again. A round building of this kind is called a tholos. Greek architects used it rarely, mostly for special shrines and memorials. Around the outside ran a ring of eighteen columns in the Ionic order. You can recognise Ionic columns by the scroll shapes at the top, which look a little like rolled paper. Inside, a circular wall carried half-columns in the Corinthian style, decorated with carved leaves. Two styles of column, in one small building, show a confident design.

Here is a detail most visitors walk past. The roof was held together at its peak by a bronze fitting shaped like a poppy head. We know this because Pausanias mentions it. Pausanias was a Greek traveller who wrote a detailed guidebook in the second century after Christ, about five hundred years after this building rose. He walked these same paths and recorded what he saw. Much of what we know about the interior of the Philippeion comes from his short description.

And the interior is the real reason this building matters. Pausanias says it held statues by the sculptor Leochares, one of the leading artists of the age. They showed Philip, his son Alexander, and his father Amyntas. They also showed two women — Olympias, the mother of Alexander, and Eurydice, the mother of Philip. Five members of one family stood in a circle, in a sanctuary of the gods. That was something new.

Think about what a visitor would have seen. Inside the Altis, statues normally showed gods, heroes, or athletes who had won at the Games. The Philippeion is the only building in the sanctuary dedicated to mortal people rather than to a god. Pausanias describes the statues as gold and ivory, the precious materials used for divine images. Many modern scholars think they were actually marble with gilded surfaces. Either way, the effect was clear. A royal family was presented in a setting that belonged to gods.

So this small round building carries a large idea. Some historians see it as an early step toward the worship of rulers. That practice grew strong after Alexander, when kings across the Greek world received honours close to those of gods. Others read it more simply, as dynastic display, a family portrait in stone. The building does not settle the debate. But it shows how power, family, and religion were starting to overlap in the late fourth century before Christ.

What happened to the statues? Nobody knows for certain. They are long gone, and no fragment has been securely identified with them. Over the following centuries, Olympia declined. Earthquakes, floods, and the river Alpheios slowly buried the sanctuary under silt and sand. Stones from many buildings were reused or scattered. For a very long time, the Philippeion existed only as a ring of foundations and a few lines in an old travel book.

The columns you see standing today are the work of modern archaeologists. The German Archaeological Institute has excavated at Olympia since the late nineteenth century. In the early two thousands, specialists re-erected part of the colonnade using original blocks found on the site, with new stone added where pieces were missing. This method is called anastylosis, which simply means putting a fallen building back together from its own parts. Look closely and you can often tell old stone from new by colour and texture.

Take a slow walk around the outside, if the path allows. Notice the stepped platform beneath the columns, which lifted the building above the ground of the grove. Notice how the column shafts are cut with shallow vertical grooves, called flutes, which catch shadow and make the stone look lighter. Look at the remains of the inner wall and imagine it closed, with a doorway facing you. Behind that door, five figures waited in the half-light.

The best light here comes early or late in the day. Then the low sun falls across the columns and the flutes stand out sharply. At midday the stone turns flat and white, and the heat in the Altis can be strong, because there is little shade between the ruins. This corner of the site is usually quieter than the temple of Zeus or the tunnel to the stadium. Many people pass it quickly. It rewards a few patient minutes.

Before you move on, look once more at the curve of columns against the pines and the hill of Kronos. A king who wanted to belong built this ring of stone. His son went on to cross Asia, and the family he placed here in marble and gold disappeared within a generation. What remains is quiet: a round platform, some restored columns, birds in the trees. As you walk toward the Temple of Hera, notice how the straight lines return... and how this one circle stays in your mind.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon for low raking light.
Walk the full circle to see the restored colonnade.
Bring water and a hat; shade is scarce in the Altis.
Pair your stop with the nearby Temple of Hera.
Read Pausanias beforehand for his firsthand description.
