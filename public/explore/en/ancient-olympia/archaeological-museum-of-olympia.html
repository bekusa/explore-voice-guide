The guide

The story

Step in from the Peloponnese heat, and the first thing you notice is the change in the air. It is cooler here, and quieter. Your footsteps soften on the stone floor. Outside, the cicadas keep up their dry, steady rattle in the pine trees. Inside, the light is calm and even, falling across bronze, marble and clay. Everything in these rooms came out of the ground a short walk from where you stand. This is the museum of the sanctuary of Olympia, home of the ancient Olympic Games.

It helps to know what this place was. Olympia was never a city in the usual sense. It was a sanctuary, a sacred ground dedicated mainly to Zeus, king of the Greek gods. Every four years, Greeks from across the Mediterranean gathered here for the games. They came to compete, but they also came to pray, to make offerings, and to show off. Over many centuries, the ground filled with gifts to the god. This museum holds what survived.

The modern story begins in the nineteenth century. German archaeologists began systematic excavations here in 1875, and the work has continued, on and off, ever since. Much of the sanctuary had been buried under thick layers of river silt and earthquake debris. That burial was a disaster for the ancient site, but a blessing for archaeology. It sealed objects away from looters and weather. As you walk through the galleries, the rooms move roughly forward in time, from the earliest offerings to the Roman era.

Start with the bronzes, because Olympia is famous for them. The collection here is among the richest of its kind in the world. In the early centuries, pilgrims offered small bronze figurines — horses, bulls, warriors with raised arms. Later came great bronze cauldrons set on three legs, called tripods. Look closely at the griffin heads that once decorated their rims. A griffin is a mythical beast, half eagle and half lion. Their open beaks and curled tongues still look alert, even after nearly three thousand years.

Here is something many visitors miss. A large share of this bronze is military. Helmets, shields and armour fill the cases. Greek city-states often dedicated weapons taken from defeated enemies to Zeus, as thanks for victory. So these are not just pieces of equipment. They are trophies, and often trophies of wars between Greeks. One helmet carries an inscription naming Miltiades, the Athenian general linked to the victory at Marathon. Its plain, battered surface makes history feel unexpectedly personal.

Now move to the heart of the museum — the great central hall with the sculptures from the Temple of Zeus. The temple was built in the fifth century before Christ, and its triangular gables, called pediments, were filled with marble figures. Those figures are now arranged here at eye level, in their original order. You can walk along them slowly, which no ancient visitor ever could. They stood high above the ground, seen only from below.

On one side is the east pediment. It shows the moment before a chariot race between the hero Pelops and King Oinomaos. Zeus stands at the centre. The figures are still and tense, as if everyone is holding their breath. Myth says the race ended in betrayal and death, and the sculptors chose to show the calm before it. Notice the seated old man near one end. His worried face is often read as a seer who already knows how the story will end.

On the opposite side, the west pediment explodes with movement. Here the Lapiths, a mythical Greek people, fight the Centaurs, creatures half man and half horse, at a wedding feast. Bodies twist and grapple. Hands pull at hair. At the centre stands Apollo, arm outstretched, calm amid the chaos. Art historians often describe this style as early Classical, sometimes called the Severe Style — a moment when Greek sculpture moved from stiff, smiling figures toward real weight and emotion.

Below the pediments, look for the metopes. These are square relief panels that once ran above the temple's inner porches. Together they told the twelve labours of Heracles, the hero said by some to have founded the games here. In one panel, Heracles holds up the sky while Atlas brings him golden apples. A goddess quietly helps take the weight. It is a small, almost tender detail — and a reminder that even heroes need support.

Nearby, rising on a tall base, is the Nike of Paionios. Nike was the winged goddess of victory. The sculptor shaped her as if she is landing from the sky, her thin garment pressed against her body by the wind. She once stood on a high triangular pillar in front of the Temple of Zeus, dedicated by the Messenians and Naupaktians after a military success. Even broken, without her face, she still seems to be descending through the air.

In a separate room waits the museum's most celebrated marble. Hermes, messenger of the gods, holds the infant Dionysus on one arm. It was found in 1877 in the ruins of the Temple of Hera, close to where an ancient travel writer named Pausanias had described it. Many link it to the famous sculptor Praxiteles, though scholars still debate whether it is his original. Look at the surface. The marble is polished so smoothly that it almost looks soft.

There is one more surprise, quieter than the rest. Excavators found the workshop where the sculptor Pheidias built the giant statue of Zeus, once counted among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world. That statue is long gone. But from the workshop came tools, clay moulds for shaping golden drapery, and a small, plain cup. Scratched on its base are words read as a note of ownership by Pheidias himself. The wonder has vanished, yet a craftsman's mug remains.

As you finish, the later rooms show Olympia under Rome. Here are statues of emperors and their families, set up when the sanctuary served new rulers. The games continued for centuries, until the late Roman period, when the old religious festivals faded away. Eventually floods and earthquakes buried everything. The objects around you waited in the silt until shovels found them again.

Take a moment before you leave. Stand once more in the central hall and look up at Apollo's raised arm. Listen to the soft murmur of other visitors, in many languages, all looking at the same stone. When you step back outside, the heat and the cicadas will return. Walk toward the ancient site, and look at the bare foundations of the Temple of Zeus. Now you know what once stood on them.
