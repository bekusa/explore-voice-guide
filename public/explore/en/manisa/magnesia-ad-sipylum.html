The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look south. A wall of grey limestone rises almost straight up behind the rooftops. In the afternoon it turns pale gold, and its shadow falls early across the streets below. That mountain is Sipylus, and for more than two thousand years people have built their lives at its foot. You are standing in Manisa, but under your feet lies an older name. This was Magnesia ad Sipylum.

That Latin phrase means "Magnesia by Sipylus." The label mattered. The ancient Greek world had more than one Magnesia. Another famous one lay to the south, on the Maeander River, and travellers needed to know which was which. So this city carried its mountain in its name, the way a person might carry a family name. The mountain was its address and its identity.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. You will not find a neat archaeological park with columns and a ticket gate. Ancient Magnesia was never abandoned. People kept living here, century after century, and each generation built on top of the last. Greek, Roman, Byzantine and Ottoman layers lie under the modern town. The old city is not lost. It is simply underneath, carrying the weight of the present.

So how do you see it? Look at the geography, because the geography has not changed. The mountain stands at your back. Ahead lies the wide, flat plain of the Hermus River, which Turks today call the Gediz. That plain is fertile and open. It made the region rich. It also made it a place where armies could meet, and one of the great battles of the ancient world was fought nearby.

In 190 BCE, or very close to it, a Roman army faced the forces of Antiochus the Third. He was king of the Seleucid Empire, one of the large states that grew out of Alexander the Great's conquests. The Romans were commanded by Lucius Cornelius Scipio. His more famous brother, Scipio Africanus, the man who had defeated Hannibal, travelled with the army. Ancient writers say he was ill and away from the field on the day itself.

Antiochus brought a large and varied army. Ancient historians describe war elephants and scythed chariots, chariots with blades fixed to their wheels. The battle went badly for him. Rome won, and the peace that followed pushed Seleucid power back beyond the Taurus Mountains in the south. That moment changed the map. After it, Rome was the power that decided the future of western Anatolia. And it began on this plain below Sipylus.

Now a darker chapter. In the year 17, during the reign of the Roman emperor Tiberius, a powerful earthquake struck this region at night. The Roman historian Tacitus records that it damaged twelve cities in the area, and Magnesia by Sipylus was among them. Tiberius sent relief and reduced taxes so the cities could rebuild. It was remembered as one of the worst disasters of its age. The mountain above you is beautiful, but this land moves.

Now look back up at the cliffs. Long before Rome, Greeks told stories about this mountain. Legend has it that Tantalus, a king punished by the gods, ruled from Sipylus. His daughter was Niobe. According to the myth, she boasted that she had more children than the goddess Leto. The gods killed her children, and Niobe wept until she turned to stone. Locals point to a rock face on the mountain called the Weeping Rock. When water runs down it, some people see a grieving woman.

Here is the second surprise. Some people claim that the word "magnet" comes from this city. The truth is more uncertain. The ancient Greeks called lodestone "the Magnesian stone," a stone that attracts iron. But scholars do not agree which Magnesia gave its name to it. It may have been this city, or the southern one, or the Magnesia region in Greece. So treat it as an open question. Still, it is pleasant to think that the word might begin here.

The city did not fade after Rome. In the thirteenth century, after crusaders captured Constantinople, Byzantine rulers in exile built a state based at Nicaea. Magnesia became one of their important centres. Historians associate it with the treasury of Emperor John the Third Doukas Vatatzes. He was a careful ruler who took a strong interest in farming and the local economy. For a time, the wealth of a Byzantine state in exile was kept in this town.

In the early fourteenth century, the Turkish principality of Saruhan took the city. Later it became part of the Ottoman Empire. Under the Ottomans, Manisa became a training ground for princes. Young heirs to the throne were sent here to govern and learn how to rule. Suleiman, who became known as the Magnificent, was one of them. The mosques and courtyards on the lower slopes come from these centuries. They stand on ground the ancient city once covered.

If you have time, the slopes of Sipylus hold traces even older than the Greek city. A short distance from the town centre, a seated figure is carved into the rock high on the mountainside. It is very old. Many scholars date it to the Bronze Age and link it to a mother goddess. It is worn, and you need to look closely. Standing below it, you understand why ancient people thought this mountain was sacred.

So take a last look around you. The traffic, the call to prayer, the smell of bread from a bakery, the plane trees moving in the breeze... All of this sits on top of Magnesia. The plain still stretches toward the river. The cliffs still catch the late light. As you walk away, look up at Sipylus one more time. It is the one thing here that every generation, from Niobe's storytellers to you, has seen in exactly the same way.

Practical tips

Walk uphill toward the mountain for wide views over the plain.
Visit in late afternoon when the cliffs turn gold.
Wear sturdy shoes if you plan to climb Sipylus paths.
Bring water for the walk to the rock-cut seated figure.
Pair this walk with the Ottoman mosques on lower slopes.
