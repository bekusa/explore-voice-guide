The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under your feet the cobbles are uneven, worn smooth by centuries of shoes and cart wheels. In front of you rises a long building of pale render and dark roof tiles. It looks calm, almost plain. But behind these walls, a monk changed the way half of Europe prayed, read, and argued. This is the Lutherhaus in Wittenberg, the house where Martin Luther lived for most of his adult life.

The building did not begin as a family home. It began as a monastery. In the early sixteenth century, the Augustinian friars started building a cloister here at the eastern edge of town. Locals called it the Black Cloister, after the dark habits the monks wore. A young friar named Martin Luther came to live here as a monk and teacher. He studied, he lectured at the new university, and he struggled with deep questions about sin and grace.

Those questions became public in 1517. Luther wrote his Ninety-Five Theses, a list of arguments against the sale of indulgences. Indulgences were church certificates that promised to reduce punishment for sins in the afterlife. Tradition says Luther nailed the theses to the door of the Castle Church. That church stands at the far end of this same street. So the walk between Luther's home and that famous door takes only a few minutes.

Here is the first surprise. As the Reformation spread, the monks left one by one. Within a few years, the great cloister stood almost empty. Luther remained, sometimes nearly alone, in a building made for a whole community. Then the Elector of Saxony, Luther's protector and the ruler of this region, gave the old monastery to him. The former monk now owned the house where he had once taken vows of poverty.

In 1525, Luther married Katharina von Bora. She was a former nun who had escaped from her convent with other sisters. Their marriage was a scandal to some and a model to others. Katharina did not simply move into Luther's world — she ran it. She managed the household, the gardens, the animals, and a brewery. Luther sometimes called her his lord, as a warm joke about who was really in charge.

The house became very full. The couple raised their children here. Relatives lived with them. Students rented rooms and ate at the family table. Guests arrived from across the German lands and beyond. Imagine the noise in these corridors: children running, dishes clattering, Latin and German mixed in loud debate. This was less a quiet scholar's retreat and more a busy boarding house with a famous host.

At dinner, the students did something unusual. They wrote down what Luther said. His remarks on faith, politics, food, illness, and his opponents were recorded in notebooks. Later these notes were collected and published as the Table Talk. Some remarks are wise. Some are rude, funny, or harsh. Historians treat them with care, because students wrote them from memory. Still, they give us a rare, human voice from five centuries ago.

Before you go inside, look at the entrance door in the courtyard. This carved sandstone doorway is known as the Katharina Portal. It was a gift from Katharina to her husband in 1540. On it you can find a portrait of Luther and his personal emblem, the Luther rose. The rose shows a cross inside a heart, set within a white flower. For Luther, it was a symbol of his faith.

Inside, the most moving space is the Luther Room. This wood-panelled room is where the family lived and where guests gathered. Much of the furniture and the panelling dates from Luther's lifetime. The room is low, warm, and surprisingly small. Here is the second surprise. Visitors are shown a signature cut into the woodwork, said to belong to Tsar Peter the Great. The Russian ruler visited in the early eighteenth century, and he left his mark like any tourist.

After Luther's death in 1546, the house passed through many hands. The university used parts of it. In the nineteenth century, admirers of the reformer restored the building and opened it as a museum. Today the Lutherhaus is often described as the largest museum of Reformation history in the world. Since 1996, it has been part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site. That listing covers the Luther memorials here in Wittenberg and in Eisleben.

The exhibitions inside follow Luther's life and his long influence. You can see early printed books, pamphlets, and Bibles. Printing was central to the Reformation. Luther's writings were cheap, short, and in German, so ordinary people could buy and read them. His German Bible translation helped shape the modern German language. When you look at these pages, notice the woodcut pictures. Many readers could not read well, so images carried the message too.

When you step back outside, walk a short way along the street toward the old city gate. There you will find an oak tree inside a small fenced area. It marks the spot where, in 1520, Luther publicly burned a papal document that threatened him with excommunication. The tree you see today is not the original. Even so, locals have kept a Luther oak at this place for generations, as a living reminder of that act.

Now turn back toward the house. Look at the plain walls, the courtyard, the stone portal glowing in the light. Hear the footsteps of other visitors on the cobbles. Five hundred years ago, this same courtyard was full of students, children, and arguments. As you walk on toward the Castle Church, follow the same street Luther walked every day... and imagine what he carried in his mind.
