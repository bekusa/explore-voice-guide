The guide

The story

Start with the wind. Up here on the plateau above the Urft valley, it moves steadily through the grass and the beech trees. Below you, the long dark water of the Urftsee reservoir curves between forested hills. The stone under your feet is rough, grey, and heavy. It is a quiet place. That quiet is part of what makes Vogelsang so strange, because these buildings were made to train men for one of the loudest and most destructive regimes in human history.

You are standing at Ordensburg Vogelsang, in the Eifel hills of western Germany, near the small town of Schleiden. The name needs a short explanation. An Ordensburg means an order castle. The word looks back to the medieval knightly orders, such as the Teutonic Knights, who built fortified monasteries. The Nazi party borrowed that image on purpose. It wanted its future leaders to see themselves as a sworn brotherhood, bound by loyalty and obedience.

Construction began in the mid-1930s, soon after the Nazis took power. Vogelsang was one of three such schools. The other two stood at Sonthofen in the Bavarian Alps and at Crössinsee in Pomerania, which is now part of Poland. Each site was chosen for its dramatic landscape. Here, the planners wanted the hills, the forest, and the water to frame the buildings like a stage set. Look around, and you can see that they knew exactly what they were doing.

The young men who came here were called Junker, an old German word for young noblemen. They were not nobles. Most came from ordinary families, selected for their political reliability and their physical fitness. The plan was to shape them into the next generation of party officials. Their days were filled with sport, ideological lectures, and military-style discipline. The racist worldview of the regime was not a side subject. It sat at the centre of everything taught on this hill.

Now look closely at the architecture. The walls are built from local quarry stone, laid in thick, solid courses. The roofs are steep and dark. The style mixes a rural, almost village-like simplicity with a monumental scale. This combination was deliberate. It was meant to look rooted in the soil, ancient, and permanent. In reality, these buildings were brand new, and they are barely older than the grandparents of many visitors walking here today.

Here is something many visitors miss. Vogelsang was never finished. The original plans included much larger structures, among them a vast building intended as a house of learning, which would have dominated the whole hillside. When war came in 1939, work slowed and the training programme was disrupted. Many of the Junker went to the front. So the place you see is, in a sense, a fragment. The full vision existed only on paper.

The second surprise comes after 1945. Vogelsang did not fall empty. British forces took over the site first, and soon afterwards it passed to the Belgian army. For about six decades, it served as a military training ground. Soldiers lived here, trained here, and left their own marks on the buildings. The Belgian presence is part of the story too, and traces of that long military chapter remain across the grounds.

The military years also changed the surrounding landscape. Not far from here lies the ruined village of Wollseifen. Shortly after the war, its residents were ordered to leave so that the area could serve as a training zone. The village was never rebuilt. Today, little remains apart from scattered foundations and the old church. It is a quiet reminder that the history of this hill touched ordinary families long after the regime itself had collapsed.

The Belgian army withdrew in the mid-2000s. Around the same time, the land around Vogelsang became part of Eifel National Park. Since then, the site has slowly opened to the public. The question was difficult. What do you do with buildings created to glorify a criminal ideology? Tearing them down would erase the evidence. Leaving them untouched would risk turning them into a shrine. The answer chosen here was education.

Today the site is known as Vogelsang IP, which stands for international place. It holds a documentation exhibition about the Ordensburgen, the men trained in them, and the crimes connected to that training. It also houses a national park centre about the forests and wildlife of the Eifel. That pairing feels unusual at first. But it means visitors meet two stories here — one about human power and its abuse, and one about nature slowly reclaiming a damaged place.

As you walk the grounds, pay attention to the sculptures and reliefs that survive. Some show idealised male bodies, strong and calm. This was a common style in Nazi public art. It presented a narrow image of the perfect German man. Seen today, with information panels beside them, these figures look less heroic and more like what they were: tools of propaganda, made to persuade young people that cruelty was a form of strength.

Take a moment, too, for the view itself. From the terraces, the land falls away toward the reservoir, and on clear days the hills fold into each other in layers of green and blue. The light changes quickly here, especially when clouds blow across from the west. Birdsong drifts up from the woods. It is easy to understand why the planners chose this spot. It is harder to accept that such a calm landscape was used for this purpose.

Before you leave, look once more at the heavy stone walls around you. They were built to last for centuries, as a monument to a future that never arrived. Instead, they now hold school groups, hikers in muddy boots, and families reading exhibition panels together. Listen to the voices around you, in German, Dutch, French, and English. As you walk back toward the forest paths, notice how the wind still moves through the grass, just as it did before any of this was built.
