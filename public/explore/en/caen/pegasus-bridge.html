The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Water moves slowly along the canal below you, flat and grey-green, and the steel of the bridge hums when a car rolls across. Gulls call from the direction of the sea. On a quiet morning this feels like an ordinary crossing in the Norman countryside, a short drive northeast of Caen. Yet on this exact spot, in the first minutes of the Allied landings in June 1944, one of the most daring operations of the Second World War began.

You are standing over the Caen Canal, at the village of Bénouville. The canal runs from the city of Caen out to the sea at Ouistreham, roughly parallel to the River Orne. A few hundred metres east, a second bridge crosses the river itself. In 1944, these two crossings mattered enormously. Whoever held them controlled the route between the Allied beaches to the west and the open ground to the east, where German armoured units could counterattack.

The Allied planners understood this. Their solution was bold. Instead of fighting toward the bridges from the beaches, they would drop soldiers directly beside them in the dark, hours before the main landings. The task went to D Company of the Second Battalion, the Oxfordshire and Buckinghamshire Light Infantry, reinforced with Royal Engineers. Their commander was Major John Howard. His men would not arrive by parachute. They would arrive in gliders — large wooden aircraft with no engines, towed across the Channel and then released.

The gliders were called Horsas. They were built mostly of plywood, and they carried around thirty soldiers each. Once a glider was released from its tow plane, there was no second chance. The pilots had to judge the approach in darkness, using stopwatches, compass bearings and their memory of models and aerial photographs. Shortly after midnight on the sixth of June, the first gliders came down in the field just beside this canal crossing.

Here is the first surprise. The landing was remarkably precise. The lead glider stopped very close to the bridge — so close that the defenders had almost no time to react. The impact was violent. Pilots were thrown through the cockpit glass, and many soldiers were stunned. But within minutes, the men were out and running toward the bridge. The whole assault on this crossing lasted only a few minutes.

Not everyone survived those minutes. Lieutenant Den Brotheridge led his platoon across the bridge in the first rush and was shot as he reached the far side. He is often described as one of the first Allied soldiers killed by enemy fire on D-Day. He lies buried in the churchyard at nearby Ranville. When you cross from the field toward the far bank, you are following the path he ran.

The success signal had a memorable code. The word for the canal bridge, and the word for the river bridge, were combined into a short phrase: Ham and Jam. When the radio operator sent it, it meant both crossings were in British hands and intact. Now the task changed. The soldiers had to hold the bridges against counterattack until relief arrived from the beaches, many hours later.

Relief came in stages. Paratroopers arrived during the night to strengthen the position. Then, in the early afternoon, commandos who had landed on Sword Beach reached the bridge. According to a much-loved account, their arrival was announced by the bagpipes of a piper named Bill Millin, who played as the commandos marched across. The story is told so often that it has become part of the bridge's identity — a sound of Scotland in a Norman village.

The name you hear today came from that night. The emblem of the British airborne forces shows Bellerophon, a hero from Greek mythology, riding the winged horse Pegasus. After the war, the canal crossing was renamed Pegasus Bridge in honour of the men who took it. The river crossing to the east became known as Horsa Bridge, after the gliders. Two names, one for the soldiers and one for their aircraft.

Here is the second surprise. The bridge in front of you is not the original. The first one was a lifting bridge from the 1930s, designed to rise so that ships could pass along the canal. By the 1990s, it was too small for modern traffic and shipping. It was replaced with a larger bridge of similar design. The original was saved. Today it stands in the grounds of the Memorial Pegasus museum, a short walk away, where you can see it up close.

Now look toward the café at the end of the bridge. This is the Café Gondrée. The Gondrée family ran it during the occupation, and it is widely described as one of the first houses in France to be liberated on D-Day. For decades afterward, the family welcomed returning veterans here. The walls carry photographs, badges and memorabilia left by those soldiers and their families. It is still a working café.

In the field near the canal bank, small memorial markers show where the gliders came to rest. Stand beside them and look back at the bridge. The distance feels short — and that is the point. It shows how close the pilots brought their fragile aircraft in total darkness. At the museum, a full-size replica of a Horsa glider gives a sense of scale. Most visitors are struck by how light and simple it looks.

Before you leave, take a moment on the bridge itself. Feel the vibration of the steel under your feet. Watch the canal stretch away toward the sea on one side and toward Caen on the other. Each year, in early June, veterans' families and visitors gather here, and the café fills with conversation in many languages. Today, though, it is just you, the water and the wind. As you walk away, look back once — and notice how small and ordinary this crossing appears.
