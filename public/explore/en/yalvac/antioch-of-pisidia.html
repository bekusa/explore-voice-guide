The guide

The story

Listen first. On most days the loudest sound here is the wind, moving across dry grass and loose stone on a low hill above Yalvaç. Underfoot, the ground is uneven. Worn paving slabs give way to packed earth and grey limestone blocks. To the east, the long ridge of the Sultan Mountains marks the horizon. This is Antioch of Pisidia, and for centuries it was one of the most important cities in the interior of Asia Minor.

The name can confuse visitors. There were several cities called Antioch in the ancient world, and the most famous one stood far to the southeast, in what is now Antakya. This Antioch took its name from the same royal family, the Seleucids, the Greek-speaking dynasty that ruled much of the Near East after Alexander the Great. Historians still debate which king founded it. Most agree it began as a Hellenistic settlement, a Greek-style town planted to hold a strategic plateau.

Pisidia, the region in the name, was rugged mountain country. Its people had a reputation in the ancient sources as hard to govern. A city on this hill, between lakes and mountain passes, gave rulers a place to watch the roads. That logic did not change when Rome arrived. If anything, the Romans valued the site even more.

Here is the first surprise. Around the late first century before Christ, the emperor Augustus refounded Antioch as a Roman colony. He settled veterans of his legions here, retired soldiers who received land in return for their service. For generations, Latin was heard on these streets in the middle of a Greek-speaking world. The city carried a new official name that honoured Caesar, and it became, in effect, a small piece of Italy on the Anatolian plateau.

You can still feel that Roman plan in the layout. Paved streets run between the ruins, and the city was organised around public squares. Walk toward the highest point of the site and you reach the most dramatic space of all. A wide, raised area leads to the remains of a temple, set against a curved wall of rock. Scholars generally identify this as a temple for the worship of Augustus himself and the imperial family.

Look closely at that curved backdrop. Much of it was cut directly into the natural bedrock of the hill. The builders shaped the living stone into a semicircle that framed the temple like a stage set. In front of it, a monumental gateway once led visitors up from the street. Imagine a crowd arriving for a festival, climbing toward the temple, with the whole curved façade rising behind it. The design was meant to impress, and to remind every citizen who held power.

Now the second surprise, and one that matters to historians across the world. Augustus wrote an account of his own achievements, a document usually known by its Latin title, Res Gestae, meaning things accomplished. Copies were set up in cities across the empire. Fragments of a Latin version were found here at Antioch. Together with the better-preserved copy from Ankara, they help scholars reconstruct what the first emperor wanted the world to remember about him.

Antioch also holds a special place in Christian history. The Acts of the Apostles, in the New Testament, describes Paul and his companion Barnabas coming to Antioch in Pisidia. According to that account, Paul spoke in the local synagogue, and his words drew both interest and hostility. Because of this story, the site has long drawn Christian pilgrims, and many visitors arrive here specifically to stand where Paul is said to have preached.

Among the ruins you will find the foundations of a large church, often called the Church of Saint Paul. It dates from later centuries, when the city had become Christian and an important seat of bishops. The outline of its walls is still clear on the ground. Pilgrims sometimes pause here quietly, and you may see small groups reading aloud together. Whether or not this exact spot connects to Paul, the church shows how central his memory became to the city.

A city of this size needed water, and the Romans solved that problem with an aqueduct. It carried water from springs in the hills to the north. Some of its stone arches still stand outside the main ruins, and they are easy to miss if you stay near the entrance. Seen from the right angle, the arches step across the landscape in a line, and they show the engineering confidence of the Roman colony.

There is more on the slopes. You will pass the remains of a theatre, baths, and stretches of street lined with fallen columns and carved blocks. On a separate hill some distance away stood a sanctuary of a local moon god called Men, worshipped in this region long before Roman rule. That older faith reminds us that the colonists did not arrive in an empty land. Anatolian, Greek, and Roman traditions lived side by side here.

The city declined in the Byzantine period, affected by raids, earthquakes, and shifting trade routes. Over time, people moved down to the plain, and the town of Yalvaç grew below. Modern study of the site began with travellers and scholars in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and excavations have continued in waves since then. Many of the finest finds, including inscriptions and sculpture, are kept in the Yalvaç Museum in town, which makes a good partner visit.

As you walk, notice how quiet the site usually is. Unlike the crowded ruins on the coast, Antioch of Pisidia often lets you stand alone on a Roman street. Lizards cross the warm stones. Shepherds may pass with their flocks on nearby slopes. The light changes quickly on this open plateau, turning the limestone from pale grey at midday to soft gold in the late afternoon.

Before you leave, stand once more near the rock-cut wall of the temple and look out toward the mountains. Soldiers, priests, merchants, and apostles all looked at this same horizon. The voices are gone, but the stones they placed are still here, under your feet. Take your time on the walk back... and listen again to the wind moving through the grass.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Yalvaç Museum to see sculptures and inscriptions from here.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven paving and loose stones.
Bring water and sun protection; the plateau offers little shade.
Come in late afternoon for warm light on the limestone.
Walk out to the aqueduct arches beyond the main ruins.
