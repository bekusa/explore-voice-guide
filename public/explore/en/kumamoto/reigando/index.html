The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water drips somewhere in the rock. Cool air moves out of the cave mouth, carrying the smell of damp stone and old incense. Under your feet, the path is uneven, worn smooth by many pilgrims. Around you, moss covers almost everything in shades of green and grey. This is Reigandō, a shallow cave in the hills west of Kumamoto city. You are standing in the place where Japan's most famous swordsman chose to spend his last years.

That swordsman was Miyamoto Musashi. In Japan his name carries the weight that a figure like Robin Hood or King Arthur carries elsewhere. The difference is that Musashi was a real man. He lived in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. He fought many duels and was said never to have lost one. Later he became a painter, a calligrapher and a writer. It was here, in this cave, that he worked on his most famous book.

The book is called Go Rin no Sho, usually translated as The Book of Five Rings. The five rings are Earth, Water, Fire, Wind and Void. Each ring is a chapter. Together they describe his approach to strategy, combat and discipline. Musashi wrote it in the early 1640s, near the end of his life. He died in 1645. Today the book is read far beyond Japan. Martial artists study it, and so do business leaders and sports coaches.

Why Kumamoto? In his final years, Musashi was invited here by the Hosokawa family. The Hosokawa were the lords who ruled this region from Kumamoto Castle. They gave the ageing warrior a home and a respected position. But Musashi seems to have wanted silence more than comfort. He came out to these hills to meditate and to write. The quiet you hear around you now is close to the quiet he looked for.

The cave belongs to Unganzen-ji, a Zen Buddhist temple of the Sōtō school. Zen, in simple terms, is a form of Buddhism that places sitting meditation at its centre. That fits Musashi well. His writing returns again and again to a calm, empty mind — a mind that does not freeze or hesitate. Many readers say the final chapter, the Void, reads more like a Zen teaching than a fighting manual.

Now look into the cave itself. It is not deep or dramatic. It is a hollow in the cliff, open to the light. Inside, a statue of Kannon is enshrined. Kannon is the Buddhist figure of compassion, loved across Japan. This one is known locally as Iwato Kannon, which means roughly the Kannon of the rock door. Legend says the statue was here long before Musashi arrived. The cave was already a sacred place when he came.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. The cave is not the only reason people climb this hill. On the way up, you pass hundreds of small stone figures. These are the Gohyaku Rakan, the Five Hundred Arhats. An arhat is a disciple of the Buddha who has reached enlightenment. The statues were carved in the eighteenth century, long after Musashi's death. A local donor paid for them, and the work took many years to complete.

Take a slow look at their faces. No two are the same. Some smile. Some frown. Some look lost in thought, and some seem almost to be laughing. Over the centuries, rain, earthquakes and time have worn them down. Many have lost their heads or their hands. Moss grows over their shoulders like green cloth. A Japanese tradition says that among the five hundred, you may find one face that looks like someone you know.

The damage to the statues is part of the story of this place. Kumamoto sits in an area with strong seismic activity. In 2016, a series of powerful earthquakes shook the whole region. Kumamoto Castle was badly damaged, and the hillside here was shaken too. When you see a broken figure, you are seeing the real history of the land. Nothing here is a museum piece behind glass. It lives outdoors, exposed to weather and to the ground moving.

It is worth thinking about the man who sat in this cave. By the time he arrived in Kumamoto, Musashi was in his late fifties or early sixties. He had travelled across Japan for decades. He had developed a two-sword style, holding a long sword and a short sword at once. His school is known as Niten Ichi-ryū. Yet the book he wrote here is not full of tricks. It is about seeing clearly, practising constantly, and adapting to any situation.

Musashi's art is another surprise for many visitors. He was a gifted ink painter. His works show birds, plants and simple figures, made with few brushstrokes and much empty space. Some of his paintings are now treated as important cultural treasures in Japan. Standing here, it is easy to understand that style. The view is spare. The light comes and goes between the trees. Everything unnecessary seems to fall away.

A word about the visit itself. The path up to the cave is steep in places. The stone steps can be wet and slippery, especially after rain. Wear shoes with good grip, and take your time. This is an active temple, so speak quietly and treat the statues and the shrine with respect. Photography of the landscape is generally welcome, but watch for any signs asking you not to photograph certain objects.

Before you leave, stand still once more at the mouth of the cave. Feel the cool air on your face. Notice the drip of water and the soft green of the moss. Look back down the slope at the rows of stone faces watching the path. Nearly four centuries ago, an old swordsman sat here and tried to put a lifetime of learning into words. As you walk down, you carry a small part of that silence with you.
