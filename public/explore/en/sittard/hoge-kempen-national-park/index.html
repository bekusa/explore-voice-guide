The guide

The story

Listen first. Under your boots there is a soft crunch, half sand and half pine needles. The air smells of resin, warm in the sun and sharp after rain. Somewhere above, a breeze moves through the tops of the pines, a long hiss like distant surf. Then the trees open, and the ground falls away into a wide sweep of heather. Welcome to Hoge Kempen National Park, just across the river Meuse from Sittard, on the Belgian side of the border.

The name tells you where you are. The Kempen is the sandy upland region of northern Belgium and the southern Netherlands. Hoge means high, and this is the high part of it. It is a plateau, raised above the surrounding land, with a steep edge that drops toward the Meuse valley in the east. From Sittard, the Meuse is the line you cross to get here. On a clear day, from the high points of the park, you look back over that valley toward the Dutch side.

This park holds a quiet distinction. When it opened in 2006, it became the first national park in Belgium. That surprises many visitors, who expect a country this densely built to have protected its wild places long ago. But wild is not quite the right word here. Almost everything you see has been shaped by people, and the story of this landscape is a story of work.

Start with the ground itself. The sand and gravel under your feet were laid down long ago by an ancient version of the Meuse. Before the river cut its present valley, it spread stones and sand across this whole plateau. That is why the soil here is so poor. Rain drains straight through it, and few nutrients stay behind. Poor soil, it turns out, is exactly what heather loves.

For centuries, the heath was common land. Local farmers grazed sheep on it, cut the turf, and gathered heather for animal bedding. That constant use kept trees from taking over. The open, purple landscape that people now come to admire was not a pristine wilderness. It was a working landscape, kept open by generations of shepherds and farmers. When that way of life faded, the heath began to shrink.

Then came coal. In the early twentieth century, deep coal seams were discovered beneath this part of Limburg, the Belgian province around you. Mines opened in places like Genk and Maasmechelen, on the edges of the park. They brought workers from across Europe and beyond, from Italy, Turkey, Greece, Morocco and elsewhere. The towns around you still carry that mix of languages, kitchens and family names.

Here is the detail most visitors miss. Many of the pine forests you are walking through were planted for the mines. Underground tunnels needed timber props to hold up the roof, and fast-growing pine was ideal. So large areas of heath were planted with neat rows of conifers. When you notice how straight and regular some stands of trees look, you are looking at an industrial crop, not an ancient forest.

The mines closed toward the end of the twentieth century, and the region had to find a new future. One of the park's main entrances, Connecterra in Genk, sits on the site of a former mine. The old industrial buildings now welcome walkers instead of miners. It is a fitting doorway. You pass through the memory of the coal industry before you step into the landscape that supplied it.

The second surprise lies in the water. As you walk, you may come across deep blue lakes with pale sandy shores. They look natural, but many are the result of sand and gravel extraction. Companies dug into the plateau for building materials, and when the digging stopped, the pits filled with water. Today these lakes give the park some of its most dramatic views. Bright sand, dark pines and clear water sit side by side, more like a southern coastline than inland Belgium.

Nature has moved back into these human-made spaces. The open heath and sandy ground suit specialists that struggle elsewhere. On summer evenings, listen for the nightjar, a well-camouflaged bird with a long, mechanical, churring call. Lizards bask on warm sand. Dragonflies patrol the edges of the lakes. Park managers now cut trees and use grazing animals to keep parts of the heath open, doing on purpose what the old farmers once did by necessity.

Timing changes everything here. In late summer, usually around August, the heather flowers and the plateau turns a soft purple. Bees work the blossom, and the air carries a faint honey smell. In autumn, birches turn yellow against the dark pines. In winter, the heath goes brown and bronze, and mist settles in the hollows. Early morning and late afternoon bring the best light, low and golden across the open ground.

The park is made for walking and cycling. Like much of Flanders, it uses a network of numbered junctions. You follow posts from one number to the next, which makes it easy to build a route of any length. Distances on the map can be deceptive, because sand is slow underfoot. The terrain is mostly gentle, but the edge of the plateau has real climbs, especially where it falls toward the Meuse.

Take a moment now, wherever you are standing. Look at the ground, and notice whether it is sand, heather or needles. Look at the trees, and ask whether they grow in rows or at random. Look for water, or for the pale scar of an old quarry. Each of these tells you something about the people who used this place. As you walk on, the quiet around you is not an absence of history. It is what history left behind.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cross the Meuse from Sittard to reach the Belgian park.
Start at a gateway such as Connecterra in Genk.
Follow numbered junction posts to plan your walking route.
Wear sturdy shoes, since loose sand slows walking.
Visit in late summer to see the heather flowering.
