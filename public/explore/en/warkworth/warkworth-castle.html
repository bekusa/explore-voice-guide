The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across the grass of the outer bailey, and somewhere below the walls the River Coquet runs over stones. The ground here is soft and slightly uneven under your feet. Above you, the honey-coloured sandstone of the great keep rises against the Northumberland sky. On a grey day it looks almost brown. When the sun breaks through, it turns gold. Take a moment and let your eyes adjust to the size of it.

You are standing on a strong defensive site. The river curls around the village of Warkworth in a tight loop, almost like a horseshoe. The castle sits across the open end of that loop, on the high ground. Water protects most of the town, and the castle blocks the only easy way in by land. Medieval builders looked for exactly this kind of position, and this one is very good.

The first castle here was much simpler than what you see now. It began as a fortification of earth and timber, probably in the twelfth century. This was border country. Scotland is not far to the north, and for centuries raids and wars moved back and forth across this land. A castle here was not decoration. It was a working tool for holding the north of England.

The name most closely tied to Warkworth is Percy. The Percy family took control of the castle in the fourteenth century, and they became one of the most powerful families in England. Later they held the title Earls of Northumberland. Look for their emblem as you explore. The Percy lion appears in carved stone around the site. It was a clear message to every visitor about who was in charge here.

Now turn to the keep itself, because this is the surprise most visitors feel without being able to explain it. Most castle keeps are simple square or round towers. This one is different. Its plan is shaped like a cross, with wings that project from a square core. The design is unusual in England, and it was built in the late fourteenth century. It was both a fortress and a statement of wealth and good taste.

Inside, the keep was carefully planned around comfort and display. There were halls, kitchens, chambers and a chapel, all fitted together inside one compact building. At the centre, a narrow light well runs down through the middle of the tower. It brought daylight and air into rooms that would otherwise be dark. It also collected rainwater. When you walk inside, look up through it and notice how the light falls through the floors.

The second surprise comes from literature. William Shakespeare used Warkworth as a setting in his history plays about King Henry the Fourth. The Percy family appear as major characters. The most famous of them is Harry Percy, better known as Hotspur, a young warrior with a reputation for a hot temper and great courage. In the play, Shakespeare even calls this castle a worm-eaten hold of ragged stone, which is a rather unkind description of such a fine building.

The real Percys did rebel against Henry the Fourth in the early fifteenth century. The rebellion went badly for them. Hotspur died in battle, and the family lost much of its power for a time. The castle passed through several hands before the Percys eventually recovered it. That pattern repeats across English history. Great families rose, fell and rose again, and their stone buildings stayed in place through every change.

Walk back toward the gatehouse and the long curtain wall. These walls enclose the bailey, the large open courtyard where much of daily life once happened. Picture stables, workshops, storerooms and the noise of horses and people. Today you mainly see foundations in the grass and the outlines of lost buildings. Look closely at the walls and you can see changes in the stonework, where builders repaired, extended or rebuilt sections across several centuries.

Over time, the castle became less important as a fortress. Wars with Scotland ended, and great families wanted more comfortable houses. Parts of the castle fell into ruin. In the nineteenth century, some rooms in the keep were restored, and these are often called the Duke's Rooms. The castle still belongs to the Duke of Northumberland, a descendant of the Percy line. English Heritage looks after it and opens it to the public.

If you have time, the castle is only part of the story here. A short distance upriver, cut into the rock beside the water, there is a medieval hermitage, a small chapel and living space carved into the cliff. It is usually reached by a short boat crossing, which runs only at certain times of year. Even if you cannot visit it today, the walk along the riverbank gives you a quiet, green view of the castle above.

The village of Warkworth is worth a slow walk as well. The main street runs downhill from the castle gate toward the church and the river. At the bottom you will find an old bridge with a fortified gatehouse tower, built so that the town could control who crossed into it. Between the castle at the top and the bridge at the bottom, the whole medieval town plan is still easy to read.

Before you leave, find a spot on the high ground and look back at the keep. Notice the weathered sandstone, the dark gaps where windows once held glass, and the way the cross-shaped walls catch the light at different angles. Listen again to the wind and the river. These are the same sounds that the Percys, their soldiers and their servants heard every day, long before anyone thought of this place as a ruin.
