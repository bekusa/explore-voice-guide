The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through pine and birch. Gravel shifts under your shoes. Somewhere a bird calls across open heath. The silence here is not empty. It feels held, as if the land itself is waiting for you to pay attention. This is the site of Westerbork, a camp in the quiet province of Drenthe, in the northeast of the Netherlands.

Today the landscape looks peaceful. Low heath, sandy soil, tall trees at the edges. It is hard to imagine that this flat, gentle ground was once the last stop in the Netherlands for tens of thousands of people. From here, trains carried them east. Most of them never came back. As you walk, keep that contrast in mind. It is the heart of this place.

The story begins before the German occupation, and this surprises many visitors. In 1939, the Dutch government built Westerbork as a camp for Jewish refugees who had fled Nazi Germany. It was meant as a place of shelter, though a strict and isolated one. The government chose this remote location on purpose. And the Dutch Jewish community was asked to help pay for its construction. That uncomfortable detail is part of the history here.

Then came the war. Germany invaded the Netherlands in May 1940. In July 1942, the German authorities took control of the camp and changed its purpose completely. It became a Durchgangslager — a transit camp. The word means a place you pass through. Westerbork was never designed as a killing site. Instead, it was a gathering point, where people were held, counted, and then put on trains.

The first transport left in July 1942. After that, trains departed with terrible regularity, usually on Tuesdays. Prisoners lived each week in dread of the lists that were read out the night before. Over the following years, more than one hundred thousand people were deported from this camp. Most were Jews. Sinti and Roma were deported from here too. The trains went mainly to Auschwitz and Sobibor, and also to Theresienstadt and Bergen-Belsen. Only a few thousand survived.

Here is something many people do not expect. Inside the camp, daily life could look almost ordinary. There was a large hospital, a school, workshops, and even a cabaret with skilled performers. The commandant, Albert Konrad Gemmeker, encouraged this appearance of calm. A quiet camp was an efficient camp. The order and routine helped keep people from resisting, and helped keep the trains full. The normality itself was part of the machinery.

The main road through the camp had a bitter nickname among prisoners: the Boulevard des Misères, the Boulevard of Miseries. A railway line was extended right into the camp, so the trains could stop inside the fence. Families walked along that track to the waiting wagons. Many of those wagons were cattle and freight cars. As you look across the open ground today, try to picture that line of carriages stretching across the heath.

Two names connect many visitors to this place. Anne Frank and her family were brought here after their hiding place in Amsterdam was discovered. On 3 September 1944, they were put on the last train from Westerbork to Auschwitz. The writer Etty Hillesum also lived here, first as a worker for the Jewish Council. Her letters describe the camp with honesty and deep compassion. She was deported in 1943 and did not survive.

One image from this camp has travelled around the world. In 1944, a prisoner named Rudolf Breslauer filmed life in Westerbork on the commandant's orders. His footage includes a young girl looking out through the gap of a wagon door, a white scarf around her head. For decades she was unidentified. Later, researchers found her name: Settela Steinbach, a Sinti girl. She was murdered at Auschwitz. Her face is now one of the best-known images of the persecution.

Canadian troops liberated the camp on 12 April 1945. But the site's story did not end there. After the war, it held people suspected of collaborating with the Nazis. Later, it housed families from the Moluccan Islands, in what is now Indonesia, who came to the Netherlands after decolonisation. They lived here for many years, until the early 1970s. Most of the original barracks were then removed, and the land grew quiet.

Walk on and you will reach the national monument. A section of railway track ends abruptly, and the rails bend upward toward the sky, like broken hands. Nearby, you will see a large field of small stones of different heights. There is one stone for each person deported from this camp. Many carry a Star of David. Some carry a flame, marking Sinti and Roma victims. Take a moment to walk among them.

One more thing may surprise you. On the horizon, you can see large white radio telescope dishes. They belong to a scientific observatory built near the camp site, and they listen to signals from deep space. Some visitors find the contrast strange, even uncomfortable. Others find it quietly meaningful — a place of loss now also turned toward the stars. You can decide for yourself what it means to you.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Look at the outline of the old camp, the bent rails, the rows of small stones. Notice the wind again, and the light on the heath. The people who passed through here also saw this sky. As you walk back toward the memorial centre, carry one name with you, or one face. That small act of remembering is why this place still stands.

Practical tips

Keep voices low near the monuments and stone field.
Bring a rain layer; the open heath offers little shelter.
