The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind moves across the grass and the sheep call from the next field. Somewhere below, a car changes gear on the lane to Keswick. Then you turn, and the mountains are all around you. Grey peaks and green fells rise on every side, and in the middle of this wide, open bowl stands a ring of rough stones. The turf is springy underfoot. The air smells of wet earth and wool. Welcome to Castlerigg.

This is one of the oldest stone circles in Britain. Archaeologists generally place it in the late Neolithic period, the New Stone Age, around five thousand years ago. That makes it older than the great sarsen stones of Stonehenge. When people raised these stones, there was no metal in Britain. There was no writing either. Everything you see here was moved, set and balanced by human hands, rope, timber and enormous patience.

Count the stones as you walk the ring. Most sources list around forty, and they vary a great deal in size. Some barely reach your knee. Others stand taller than a person. Locals say that you can never count them twice and get the same number. It is an old piece of folklore told about several British circles. Try it yourself... the low, half-buried stones make a perfect count surprisingly difficult.

Now look at the shape. It is not a perfect circle. It is slightly flattened, closer to an oval, and that seems to be deliberate. Prehistoric builders across Britain made rings like this one, and researchers still debate how they laid them out. Some think they used ropes and stakes. Others suggest they simply judged it by eye. Either way, the result has lasted for fifty centuries on an exposed hilltop.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. Walk to the eastern side of the ring, toward the slopes beyond. Inside the circle, you will find a separate rectangle of smaller stones, set against the main ring. People call it the Sanctuary. Nobody knows what it was for. It is very unusual among British stone circles, and that makes Castlerigg special even among its ancient neighbours. Stand inside it for a moment and look outward.

Next, find the northern edge of the circle. Two larger stones stand there with a clear gap between them. Many experts read this gap as an entrance, a formal way into the ring. Imagine a group of people walking up the hill and passing between those two stones together. We do not know the ceremonies they performed. But the gap tells us that coming in mattered, and that the builders thought carefully about how people moved through this space.

Why here? Look around you and the answer starts to appear. The circle sits on a low, flat-topped hill, almost like a stage. To the north, the long bulk of Skiddaw fills the sky. To the northeast is Blencathra, with its ridged face. To the southeast, the Helvellyn range runs along the horizon. On a clear day, the mountains form a complete wall around you. It is hard to believe that this setting was an accident.

The second surprise is about trade. In the same era, people in the central Lake District made stone axes from a hard volcanic rock found high in the Langdale fells. These axes travelled across Britain. Some archaeologists suggest that circles like Castlerigg could have been meeting places where these tools were exchanged. This is a theory, not a proven fact. Still, it reminds us that this lonely field may once have been a busy crossroads for travelling people.

The stones themselves are local rock. They are rough and grey, often spotted with pale lichen in shades of yellow and green. Run your eyes along their surfaces. You will see cracks, hollows and smooth patches where rain has worked on them for thousands of years. Many are thought to be boulders that already lay in the landscape, possibly left by glaciers. The builders did not carve them into neat blocks. They chose them, moved them and stood them upright.

For centuries, people did not know who built this place. Earlier writers often linked stone circles with the Druids, the priests of Iron Age Britain, and this ring was once known as a Druids' circle. We now know that the stones are far older than the Druids. But the old name shows how this place has filled people's imaginations for a very long time, long before modern archaeology could offer any answers.

That imagination drew writers here. In the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, the Lake District became a destination for poets and artists of the Romantic movement, who looked for strong feeling in wild landscapes. Several of them came to Castlerigg. The poet John Keats is said to have visited during his walking tour of the north, and a gloomy circle of Druid stones later appeared in his poetry. The circle became part of the way Britain imagined its own deep past.

Today, the site is in the care of the National Trust and English Heritage. It is still an open field, with grazing sheep and a stone wall around the edge. There is no visitor centre, no glass case and no barrier between you and the stones. That openness is rare at monuments this old. It also brings responsibility. The turf here is fragile, and thousands of feet pass through each year, so treat the ground and the stones gently.

The weather shapes every visit. Clouds can sweep down from the fells in minutes and swallow the peaks completely. Then the sun breaks through, and light races across the slopes like a searchlight. Early morning and the hour before sunset are often the quietest times. Mist on an autumn morning can make the circle feel very still. In every season, though, the view changes from minute to minute, so it pays to stay a little longer than you planned.

Before you leave, stand still in the centre of the ring. Turn slowly, and let the mountains move past you one by one. Feel the wind on your face and the soft ground under your boots. Five thousand years ago, people stood on this same hill and saw these same shapes against the sky. The stones are still here. The fells are still here. And now, for a few quiet minutes, so are you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring waterproofs because weather changes fast on the hilltop.
Visit early morning or near sunset for fewer people.
Wear sturdy shoes as the field is often muddy.
Stand in the centre and turn slowly to see every fell.
Stay on firm ground to protect the fragile turf.
