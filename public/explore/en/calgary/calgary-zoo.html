The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the chatter of families and the crunch of gravel paths, there is a softer sound: the Bow River, moving fast and cold around this patch of land. The water comes down from the Rocky Mountains, and on a clear day you can feel that mountain chill in the air. Cottonwood trees lean over the banks. Somewhere ahead, a peacock calls, or a gibbon starts its long, rising song. Welcome to the Calgary Zoo.

First, a detail many visitors miss. Much of the zoo sits on an island. Saint George's Island lies in the Bow River, just east of downtown Calgary, and bridges link it to the city on both sides. Look back toward the west and you will see office towers rising above the trees. That contrast shapes the whole visit. You are a few minutes from the city centre, yet the river wraps around you like a moat.

The zoo's story begins in 1929, when a group of Calgarians formed a zoological society. In those early years it was a modest place. A few enclosures held local animals and some more exotic arrivals. Over the decades it grew into one of the largest zoos in Canada. In 2021 the organisation took a new name, the Wilder Institute, and it now runs the zoo alongside its conservation work. You will still hear most locals simply call it the Calgary Zoo.

Now for a surprise. Long before modern exhibits on Africa or the Arctic, this zoo was building dinosaurs. In the 1930s, workers began making life-size concrete figures of prehistoric animals. The most famous was a huge long-necked sauropod that Calgarians named Dinny. For generations, children climbed around these figures, and many adults here still remember them fondly. Today the Dinosaur Park continues that tradition, with replicas set among rock and pine that recall ancient Alberta.

That choice was not random. Alberta is one of the richest places on Earth for dinosaur fossils. The badlands east of Calgary have produced remarkable finds, and paleontologists still dig there today. So when you stand beside a concrete giant in this park, you are looking at a creature that may once have walked across this very province. It makes the dinosaur area feel less like a theme park and more like a local history lesson.

From prehistory, walk into the present-day landscapes. The zoo is organised into regions rather than simple rows of cages. In Destination Africa, you can find gorillas, hippos, giraffes, and other animals of that continent. Inside the warm building, the air feels heavy and humid. That is a welcome shock in winter, when the temperature outside can fall far below freezing. Watch the gorillas closely. Their family dynamics often play out in quiet glances and small gestures.

In contrast, the Canadian Wilds area celebrates animals from this country. Here you may see grizzly bears, moose, wolves, and other species that share the land beyond the city. For many international visitors, this is the best chance to see these animals safely and up close. Notice how the enclosures use boulders, grasses, and conifers. They aim to echo the foothills and forests that begin only a short drive west of Calgary.

Some of the zoo's most popular residents wear black and white. The Penguin Plunge holds several species of penguins, including kings and gentoos. Through the underwater windows you can watch them swim. On land they waddle and hop; underwater they become fast and graceful. In the colder months, the zoo has sometimes held penguin walks, when certain birds stroll outside along a path. For a city with long winters, it feels right that penguins have become local favourites.

The island also carries a harder memory. In June 2013, severe flooding struck southern Alberta. The Bow River rose sharply, and water poured across Saint George's Island. Staff moved many animals to safety, often working through dangerous conditions. Several buildings suffered heavy damage, and the zoo stayed closed for some time afterward. When you look at the calm river today, it is worth remembering how quickly it can change. Much of what you see was repaired or rebuilt after that flood.

Another chapter made international news. From 2018, the zoo hosted two giant pandas on loan from China. Crowds came to watch them eat bamboo. Then, in 2020, the pandemic disrupted the flights that carried fresh bamboo to Calgary. Without a reliable supply, the zoo decided to send the pandas back to China. It was a reminder that keeping animals well depends on long, fragile chains of care that most visitors never see.

That care continues behind the scenes. The Wilder Institute runs conservation programmes for endangered Canadian species. Its work has included breeding whooping cranes, among the rarest birds in North America, and helping other threatened animals return to the wild. Most of this happens away from public paths, at facilities outside the zoo. As you walk, look for signs that explain these projects. They show a side of the zoo that goes far beyond a day out.

Before you leave, consider the plants as well as the animals. The zoo has gardens throughout the grounds, and an indoor conservatory with tropical greenery offers a warm break in cold weather. In summer, flower beds line the paths and the cottonwoods drop their soft seed fluff, which drifts through the air like light snow. In winter, the zoo often lights its grounds with a seasonal festival, and the paths glow against the dark.

So take a last look around you. Behind the enclosures, the river keeps running toward the prairie, carrying water from distant glaciers. The towers of downtown stand to the west, and the trees move in the wind. On this small island, concrete dinosaurs, swimming penguins, and Canadian bears share the same ground. As you cross the bridge back into the city, listen once more for the river. It was here long before the zoo, and it shapes everything you just saw.

Before you go

Practical tips

Use the Zoo C-Train station to skip downtown parking.
Start with indoor exhibits on very cold winter days.
Visit the gorillas early when they are often active.
Wear layers because indoor halls feel warm and humid.
Check the zoo website for seasonal light festival dates.
