The guide

The story

Listen first. Beneath the traffic on the coast road there is another sound... water. Springs rise from the ground near the village of Myloi, and the air here smells of reeds, damp earth and salt from the gulf. Ahead of you, under a wide modern roof, sits a low mound of pale stone and packed clay. It does not look like much at first. But you are standing on one of the most important Bronze Age sites in Greece.

This is Lerna. It sits on the western shore of the Argolic Gulf, a short drive around the bay from Nafplio. On a clear day you can look east across the water toward the town you came from. People chose this spot for a simple reason. Fresh water. In a dry land, a coastline full of springs was worth more than gold. Human beings settled here again and again for thousands of years.

The oldest layers go back to the Neolithic, the late Stone Age, when the first farmers worked this plain. Later came the Early Bronze Age, which archaeologists in Greece call the Early Helladic period. Helladic simply means mainland Greek. Over the centuries, each new village was built on the ruins of the last. The result is the mound in front of you — a hill made of human life, one layer on top of another.

Most of what you see was uncovered in the 1950s. The excavations were led by the American archaeologist John Caskey, working with the American School of Classical Studies at Athens. His team dug carefully through the layers and recorded what each period left behind. Their work changed how scholars understood early Greece. Lerna showed that the mainland already had organised, wealthy communities long before the famous palaces of Mycenae.

Now look toward the centre of the site. The large rectangular building is known as the House of the Tiles. It takes its name from the many baked clay roof tiles found in its ruins, along with flat stone slabs. In the Early Bronze Age, a tiled roof was a sign of real wealth and effort. The walls stood on stone foundations, and the upper parts were made of mud brick. Narrow corridors ran along its sides — scholars call this type of structure a corridor house.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Inside this building, excavators found clay sealings — small lumps of clay pressed with stamped designs. People used them to close jars, boxes and doors. A broken seal showed that someone had opened a container without permission. Archaeologists found many different seal patterns here. That suggests a system of control over stored goods... an early form of administration, long before writing reached this part of Greece.

Then something dramatic happened. The House of the Tiles burned. The fire baked the clay and helped preserve what lay inside. And what followed is the second surprise. The people who came after did not simply build over the ruins. They raised a low, round mound of earth on top of them, edged with stones. They appear to have treated the place with respect, perhaps as a memory of something important. For a long time, nobody built on that spot again.

Around the settlement, the excavators also traced the remains of defensive walls with towers. These belong to the same early period as the House of the Tiles. Walls like these tell you that this coastline was not always peaceful. A rich village beside fresh water and a good harbour would attract trouble. Look along the edges of the site, and you can still follow the lines of stone where the defences once stood.

Lerna did not end with the fire. Later Bronze Age people lived here too. Their houses were smaller, and their pottery was different. Graves were found among the ruins, including deep shaft graves from a later stage of the Bronze Age. These connect Lerna to the wider story of the Argolid, the region of Mycenae, Tiryns and Argos. You can see the walls of Tiryns, in fact, from the hills behind Nafplio.

Now the story changes from archaeology to myth. For the ancient Greeks, Lerna was the home of the Hydra. This was the many-headed water serpent that Heracles fought in one of his famous labours. Every time he cut off a head, two more grew back. In the end, his companion burned each neck so no new head could grow. Myths are not history. But it is easy to see why storytellers placed a monster here, in a world of marshes, springs and hidden water.

The ancients told other stories about this coast. According to the traveller Pausanias, who wrote in the Roman period, there was a lake here thought to have no bottom. Legend said that the god Dionysus went down through it into the underworld, to bring back his mother. Mysteries — secret religious rites — were held at Lerna in his honour. Today the marshes are much smaller, and the springs feed channels and fields. But the feeling of a watery edge between worlds is still easy to imagine.

As you walk the site, take your time. The paths are short, and the information panels help you read the layers. Much of the most delicate material, including pottery and those clay sealings, is kept in the archaeological museum in Argos and in other collections. What remains here is the structure itself — the outline of rooms, the thickness of walls, the shape of the mound. It asks you to use your imagination a little.

So stand still for a moment. Look at the foundations of the House of the Tiles, at the stone edges of rooms where people once stored oil, grain and wine. Look beyond the roof to the blue line of the gulf. And as you walk back toward the road, listen again for the water. It is the same water that brought the first farmers here, and the same water that gave the Greeks their monster. It is still running.
