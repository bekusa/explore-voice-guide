The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through the grass, and somewhere a mockingbird runs through its borrowed songs. The ground under your feet is red Georgia clay, soft after rain and hard as brick in summer. It is a quiet field now, wide and open, with low white markers in the distance. But in 1864 this same ground held one of the most crowded places in America. Tens of thousands of men lived here with no roof, no walls of their own, and very little hope.

The official name was Camp Sumter. Soldiers on both sides simply called it Andersonville, after the small railway stop nearby. The Confederacy built it in the last year and a half of the American Civil War. Prisons closer to Richmond, the Confederate capital, had become crowded and hard to supply. Southwest Georgia seemed safer, far from Union armies, and close to a rail line. The first Union prisoners arrived in February 1864. The camp was meant to be temporary... it did not stay that way.

The prison was a stockade — a tall wall of pine logs set upright in a trench, sharpened, and packed tight. Inside, there were no barracks. Men built their own shelters from whatever they had: blankets, scraps of canvas, sticks, even holes dug into the clay. They called these shelters shebangs. Look around this field and try to picture it covered, edge to edge, with ragged little tents and smoke from tiny cooking fires.

The numbers are hard to hold in your head. The stockade first enclosed roughly sixteen acres, and was later enlarged to about twenty-six. That sounds generous until you hear how many men were inside. By the summer of 1864 the population passed thirty thousand. At its peak, Andersonville held more people than most cities in the Confederacy. Over the camp's whole life, about 45,000 Union soldiers passed through the gates. Close to 13,000 of them died here.

The main causes were not battle wounds. They were hunger, exposure, and disease — scurvy, dysentery, and infections that spread fast in crowded, filthy conditions. Rations were small and often raw, and many men had nothing to cook with. The South itself was short of food and transport by this point in the war. Historians still argue about how much was neglect and how much was collapse. For the men inside, the result was the same.

A small creek ran through the middle of the stockade. It was called Stockade Branch, and it was the prison's only water supply. It was also used for washing and, downstream, as a latrine. Guard camps and a cookhouse upstream fouled it before it even reached the prisoners. In summer heat the low ground near the stream became a swamp. Survivors wrote about the smell more than almost anything else. It reached far beyond the walls.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. A short distance inside the outer wall ran a light rail fence called the deadline. Any prisoner who crossed it, or even reached over it, could be shot by guards in the sentry boxes above. The word was literal. A man might cross it by accident, or reach toward cleaner water. Some crossed it on purpose because they had given up. That grim word, born in prisons like this one, is the same word we now use casually for work.

The prisoners did not only face their captors. A gang of fellow prisoners, known as the Raiders, robbed and beat other men for food, money, and blankets. In July 1864, other prisoners organised against them. With the camp commander's permission, they held their own trial with a judge and a jury drawn from new arrivals. Six leaders were found guilty and hanged inside the stockade by the prisoners themselves. Their graves still lie apart from the others in the cemetery here.

Now a story that sits between history and faith. In August 1864, during a heavy storm, a spring of clean water began to flow near the western wall. Prisoners believed it was an answer to prayer, and they named it Providence Spring. A geologist might say the rain opened a buried water channel. The men who drank from it did not care about the explanation. Today a small stone pavilion stands over the spot, built decades later by veterans and their supporters.

The officer in charge of the inner prison was Captain Henry Wirz, a Swiss-born doctor's assistant who had joined the Confederate army. After the war he was arrested, tried by a military commission in Washington, and convicted of war crimes. He was hanged in November 1865. He was one of very few people executed for crimes connected to the Civil War. His trial remains debated, and a monument to him stands in the nearby town. You will hear different opinions about him here.

The second surprise begins with a young prisoner named Dorence Atwater. He worked as a clerk recording the dead. Fearing the official list would be lost, he secretly made his own copy and smuggled it out. In 1865 he returned with Clara Barton, the nurse who later founded the American Red Cross. Together with a team, they matched names to numbered graves. Because of that hidden list, most of the men buried here have names on their headstones today.

The national cemetery lies just beyond the prison site. Walk through it and notice how close together the stones stand. The dead were buried in long trenches, shoulder to shoulder, and the markers follow those lines. Later, monuments from northern states rose across the prison ground, each honouring that state's soldiers. In 1970 the whole area became a National Historic Site, and it now also houses the National Prisoner of War Museum. That museum tells the story of American prisoners of war from every conflict, not only this one.

Before you leave, find one of the corner posts or the rebuilt section of log wall. Stand beside it and look across the open field. Feel the sun on your neck, the same sun the prisoners had no shelter from. Listen again to the birds and the wind in the grass. It is peaceful now, and that peace is part of the memorial. As you walk back toward your car, notice how long the walk feels... and remember that for thousands of men, this field was the whole world.
