The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your shoes as you climb toward the walls. The path rises through old trees, and the air smells faintly of resin and warm stone. Above you, battlements of pale limestone stand against the sky. Below, the red roofs of Tomar spread out along the river. It feels like a castle, and for good reason. Before this hilltop held monks and cloisters, it held soldiers. What you are walking into is a fortress that slowly turned into a monastery, and then into something much harder to name.

The story begins in 1160. A knight named Gualdim Pais, master of the Knights Templar in Portugal, began building a castle here. The young Portuguese kingdom was still pushing its frontier south against Muslim rulers. This hill was a strong point on that frontier. The Templars were a military religious order, monks who took vows but also carried swords. They had been founded to protect pilgrims travelling to Jerusalem. Here, far from the Holy Land, they guarded a different border.

As you move through the castle walls, notice how thick and plain the defensive stone is. There is very little decoration in this oldest layer. These walls were built to be useful, not beautiful. Look for narrow openings and the heavy rhythm of the towers. Later builders added extraordinary carving elsewhere on this site, but the outer walls remind you of the original purpose. This was a place made to survive attack.

At the heart of the complex stands the Charola, the round church of the Templars. From outside, it has many sides, almost like a stone drum. Inside, a central structure of eight sides rises in the middle, with a walkway circling around it. Round churches like this one recall the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem, the place Christians honour as the tomb of Christ. For the Templars, building in a circle connected this hill to the city they had sworn to defend.

Step inside and let your eyes adjust. The walls and the central core are covered with painted figures and gilded carving, added in later centuries. Saints, angels and scenes from the Bible look down from every surface. The effect is close, rich and slightly overwhelming. Locals often repeat a popular tale that the church was built round so knights could attend mass on horseback. It is a lovely image... but treat it as legend rather than documented fact.

The Templars did not last. In the early fourteenth century, the order was suppressed across Europe, accused of heresy under heavy pressure from the French king. Many members elsewhere were arrested, and some were burned. Portugal took a quieter path. King Dinis created a new order in 1319, the Order of Christ, with the approval of the pope. It received much of the Templars' property and many of their people. In effect, the knights changed their name and kept going.

This new order became deeply tied to Portugal's voyages of discovery. Prince Henry, remembered in English as Henry the Navigator, led the Order of Christ in the fifteenth century. Its wealth helped support the early sea expeditions down the coast of Africa. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The red cross of the Order of Christ was painted on the sails of Portuguese ships. So the emblem you see carved on this convent once crossed oceans on canvas.

That ocean connection explodes into stone on the western side of the church. Under King Manuel the First, around the start of the sixteenth century, a long nave was added to the old round church. Its most famous feature is a great window, usually credited to the architect Diogo de Arruda. This is the Manueline style, a late Gothic Portuguese style full of maritime and royal symbols. To see it properly, you usually look at it from a small cloister nearby.

Take your time with this window, because it rewards slow looking. Carved ropes twist and knot around the frame. You may pick out coral, seaweed, chains, cork, and the armillary sphere, a globe of rings that was the personal emblem of King Manuel. A cross of the Order sits near the top. At the base, a bearded figure seems to hold the whole structure on his shoulders. Interpretations differ about who he is. Some see a sailor, others the designer himself.

Now walk into the Great Cloister, and the mood changes completely. Gone are the knotted ropes and the sea creatures. Instead you find calm columns, balanced arches and clean classical proportions. This is the Italian Renaissance arriving in Portugal, built under King João the Third. By then the order itself had changed too. The king reformed it so the brothers lived a more enclosed, contemplative religious life. The architecture follows that shift, from crusading energy toward quiet order.

This cloister also witnessed a turning point in Portuguese history. In 1581, after a crisis over the succession, King Philip the Second of Spain was formally accepted as king of Portugal at an assembly held in Tomar. For the next sixty years, the two crowns were joined under one ruler. Standing here, among these restrained stone arches, you are close to the moment when Portugal lost its independent monarchy for a time. It is a surprising thing to learn in such a peaceful space.

The convent grew into a maze of cloisters, dormitories, kitchens and corridors. As you wander, notice the long hallways lined with small cells where the brothers slept. Notice the worn stone steps, shaped by centuries of feet. Outside the main walls, an aqueduct carried water to the convent across the valley, built in the early seventeenth century. Next to the complex lies a walled woodland, once the monks' enclosed grounds, now a shaded place for walking. UNESCO listed the convent as a World Heritage Site in 1983.

Before you leave, find a spot on the walls or in a quiet courtyard, and simply stand still for a moment. Around you are many centuries layered one on top of another. A Templar fortress. A round church shaped by Jerusalem. A carved window full of the sea. A calm Renaissance cloister. As you walk back down the hill toward the town, look back once. The battlements you saw on the way up will look different now, because you know what they hold inside.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least two hours to explore the many cloisters.
Wear comfortable shoes for the steep climb from town.
View the Manueline window from the adjoining small cloister.
Walk the shaded woodland beside the convent afterwards.
Visit the Charola first while your eyes are fresh.
