The guide

The story

Listen first. Inside the museum halls, the air is cool and quiet, and the lights are kept low. Then a face looks out of the dark. It is green-brown bronze, wide as a doorway, with eyes that push out from the head like short tubes. Its ears spread like wings. The face does not look like any culture you learned about in school. That is the point of this place. You are about to meet a civilization that almost nobody knew existed until the twentieth century.

You are in Guanghan, in Sichuan Province, a short drive north of the city of Chengdu. The name Sanxingdui means Three Star Mound. It comes from three earthen mounds that once rose from the plain here. Locals linked them with stars in the sky. Around this flat, fertile land, the people of the ancient Shu kingdom built a large walled settlement during the Bronze Age, more than three thousand years ago. Today, the excavation site and the museum stand side by side.

The story of its discovery starts with an accident. In 1929, a local farmer was digging a ditch for irrigation. His tools struck a cache of jade and stone objects. Word spread slowly, and some pieces passed into private hands. Archaeologists carried out small digs over the following decades. They found walls, pottery, and signs of a large town. But nobody was ready for what came next. The real surprise stayed underground for almost sixty more years.

In 1986, workers at a local brick factory were digging for clay. They broke into a pit packed with treasure. A few weeks later, a second pit appeared nearby. Inside were bronze heads, bronze masks, gold objects, jade blades, and dozens of elephant tusks. Archaeologists were astonished. Nothing like these bronzes had been found anywhere in China. The news changed how historians thought about early Chinese civilization. It was no longer one story told from the Yellow River valley in the north.

Here is the first detail that most visitors miss. The objects were not simply buried. Many of them were broken on purpose, and many were burned before they went into the ground. Scholars still debate why. Some think the pits held offerings from a great ritual. Others think a new ruler, or a new belief, made the old sacred objects unwanted. One thing is clear. These pits were created with intention, layer by layer, and the act of burial itself was important.

Now look closely at the bronze masks. The largest ones have those famous protruding eyes and huge, pointed ears. Some historians link them to an ancient Shu legend. According to old texts, an early Shu ruler called Cancong had eyes that stuck out from his head. Whether the masks show him, a god, or an ancestor, nobody can say for certain. The masks are too large and too heavy to wear. They were probably fixed to wooden structures or statues in a temple.

Walk on to the bronze standing figure. It is a tall, slim man on a high pedestal, and together they rise well above the height of an adult visitor. His hands form two large rings in front of his chest. They once held something, but that object is lost. Perhaps it was an ivory tusk, perhaps a staff. His robe carries fine patterns of dragons and other shapes. Many scholars see him as a priest or a priest-king, a person who stood between people and the spirit world.

The second surprise is the bronze sacred tree. It stands almost four metres tall after careful restoration. Its branches curve downward, and birds perch on them. A dragon runs down the trunk. Some researchers connect it with Chinese myths about a world tree, where sun birds rested before crossing the sky. It was cast in many separate parts and then joined together. Stand back from the glass and take in the whole shape. Then step close and look at the small birds.

Gold is another part of the story. The pits held thin gold foil, gold masks that once covered bronze heads, and a long gold covering for a staff or scepter. That covering shows fish, birds, arrows, and human faces. In this region, at this time, gold was rare and precious. Its use here suggests a powerful ruling group. It also suggests skilled craftspeople who could hammer metal into sheets thinner than paper. Look for the faint marks of tools on the surface.

The discoveries did not stop in the 1980s. Starting around 2019, archaeologists found six more sacrificial pits near the first two. They worked inside climate-controlled shelters, with scientists from many fields on site. The new pits produced fresh bronzes, more ivory, silk traces, and a large fragment of a gold mask. Some of these finds now appear in the newer exhibition hall. So the site is not a closed chapter. Research is still going on in the fields just outside.

Perhaps the deepest mystery is silence. No writing has been found at Sanxingdui. There are symbols on some objects, but no texts that tell us who these people were or what they believed. We also do not know exactly why the city declined. Later, a related culture appeared at the Jinsha site, inside modern Chengdu. Many scholars see it as a successor. Still, the people who made these faces left no words. Their bronzes must speak for them.

As you walk through the museum, notice how the displays change pace. Some halls hold rows of small items, such as jade blades, pottery, and bronze animals. Others give one object a whole room. The lighting is low to protect the metal and to keep focus on the faces. Families often gather at the large masks, and children like to copy their wide stares. Take your time. Many visitors rush to the famous pieces and miss the smaller animals and birds.

Before you leave, look at the bronze faces one more time. Their eyes have watched this plain for more than three thousand years, most of that time in the dark under the soil. Outside, the Sichuan air is soft and often hazy. The fields around the site still grow crops, as they did when that farmer dug his ditch. As you step back into the light, remember that more of this lost world may still lie beneath your feet.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early in the day to see masks before crowds.
Allow at least half a day for both exhibition halls.
Combine your visit with the Jinsha site museum in Chengdu.
Bring a light jacket for the cool, air-conditioned halls.
Turn off camera flash to protect the ancient bronzes.
