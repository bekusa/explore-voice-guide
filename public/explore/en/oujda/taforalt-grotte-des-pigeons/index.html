The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here in the Beni Snassen mountains, the wind moves through scrub and wild olive, and somewhere nearby, pigeons clatter off a rock ledge. The air smells of dry earth and warm limestone. In front of you, a pale cliff opens into a wide, shadowed mouth. It does not look like much at first. But this cave holds one of the deepest human stories anywhere in North Africa.

This is Taforalt, also known as the Grotte des Pigeons, the Cave of the Pigeons. The name comes from the rock doves that still nest in the cliffs. The cave sits near the small village of Taforalt, in the hills north-west of Oujda, not far from the Algerian border. The drive up from the plain climbs through switchbacks, almond trees and terraced slopes. By the time you arrive, the city feels very far away.

The cave is formed in limestone, the soft grey-white rock you see around you. Over long ages, water dissolved the stone and carved out this large chamber. Its broad entrance faces outward over the valley. For people living here thousands of years ago, that mattered. A wide opening lets in light and air. A raised position lets you watch the land below. And the rock overhead keeps off rain, snow and the hard summer sun.

Archaeologists have studied this cave for decades. The French archaeologist Jean Roche excavated here in the mid-twentieth century. Later, from the early 2000s, a joint team from Morocco's national archaeology institute and the University of Oxford returned with modern methods. They dug slowly, layer by layer, through deposits several metres deep. Each layer is like a page. Together, those pages cover tens of thousands of years of human life in this one place.

Here is the first surprise. Deep in the older layers, researchers found tiny shells of a small sea snail called Nassarius. Many were pierced, and some carried traces of red ochre, a natural iron pigment. These were beads. People had strung them to wear. The shells were dated to roughly 82,000 years ago. That makes them among the oldest known personal ornaments made by humans anywhere on Earth. And remember — the sea is a long walk from here.

Think about what that means. Someone carried those shells inland, across hills and valleys, then pierced and coloured them. Beads are not tools for survival. They are about identity, belonging, and perhaps beauty. So this cave shows that, very long ago, people here already cared about how they looked to one another. In a small way, they were saying who they were. That instinct still feels familiar when you look at the jewellery people wear today.

The cave is best known, however, for a much later chapter. Around 15,000 years ago, people of a culture archaeologists call Iberomaurusian lived across this part of North Africa. They were hunter-gatherers who made small, finely worked stone blades. At the back of this cave, they buried their dead. Excavators uncovered the remains of many individuals. Taforalt is now counted among the oldest known cemeteries in the world.

These people had one striking custom. Many of the adults had their upper front teeth removed during life. Archaeologists can tell because the bone of the jaw had healed afterwards. Nobody knows exactly why they did it. It may have marked a passage into adulthood, or membership in a group. Whatever the meaning, it was clearly important. It was painful, deliberate, and shared by many members of the community.

The teeth hold a second surprise. Researchers found an unusually high rate of tooth decay among these hunter-gatherers. Cavities are usually linked with farming and starchy food. But these people did not farm. Plant remains from the cave suggest they ate large amounts of acorns and pine nuts, which are rich in starch. So painful toothache arrived here thousands of years before agriculture. It is an unexpected link between their lives and ours.

In 2018, scientists published ancient DNA from several of the people buried here. It was some of the oldest human DNA ever recovered from Africa. The results showed a mixed ancestry. Part of it was related to people living in the Near East at that time. Another part was related to populations of sub-Saharan Africa. So this quiet cave in the Moroccan hills helped researchers rethink how people moved and met across the ancient world.

As you stand here, try to picture the cave in use. Smoke from cooking fires would rise toward the ceiling. People would sit near the entrance, shaping stone and sorting nuts. Animal hides might hang to dry. Children would run along the ledge. Further back, in the dimmer part of the cave, the dead lay close to the living. Here, home and memory shared the same rock shelter.

Today the site is protected, and the deep excavation areas are not open for wandering. That protection matters. The deposits are fragile, and every centimetre of soil can still hold information. So look, but leave the ground as it is. The wider landscape is worth your time as well. The Beni Snassen hills hold other caves, river gorges and mountain villages. Shepherds still move flocks across these slopes, and the hills turn green after winter rain.

Before you leave, turn and look out from the cave mouth, the way its first residents did. You see the same ridges, the same sky, and probably the same kind of pigeons wheeling below. The people who wore shell beads and buried their families here are long gone. But the view they knew is still in front of you. As you walk back down the path, listen again for the wind and the wings.
