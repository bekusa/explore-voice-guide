The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere above you, branches creak and leaves shiver as a small body leaps from limb to limb. The air is cool and damp here, noticeably cooler than the street you just left. It smells of wet earth, moss, and the faint sweetness of incense drifting from a temple you cannot yet see. Underfoot, the paved path is often slick with fallen leaves. Welcome to the Sacred Monkey Forest Sanctuary, in the heart of Ubud, Bali.

Its Balinese name is Mandala Suci Wenara Wana. Roughly, that means the holy sanctuary of the monkey forest. The word wenara simply means monkey. This is not a zoo, and it is not a national park run by a distant government. The forest belongs to the people of Padangtegal, the village community that surrounds it. Local villagers manage it, maintain it, and care for its temples. That fact shapes everything you will see today.

At the centre of that care is a Balinese idea called Tri Hita Karana. It is a philosophy of three sources of wellbeing. The first is harmony between people and the divine. The second is harmony between people themselves. The third is harmony between people and the natural world. Here, all three meet in one place. The temples serve the gods, the village works together, and the trees and monkeys are protected. Keep that idea in mind as you walk.

Now, the residents. The monkeys you see are long-tailed macaques. They are also called crab-eating macaques, although you will not see them hunting crabs here. Look at their faces. Many have pale whiskers that sweep back like a small grey beard. Their tails are often as long as their bodies. They live in several separate groups, each with its own part of the forest. Researchers who study them have found that these groups defend their territory and sometimes clash at the borders.

Here is something most visitors miss. The monkeys are not one big crowd. Each troop has its own social order, its own favourite trees, its own daily routine. If you stand still for a few minutes, you may notice that mothers keep their babies close, while young males test each other in short, noisy chases. Grooming is constant. One monkey picks carefully through another's fur. It is not only about cleanliness. Grooming builds friendships and keeps the peace inside the group.

As you walk deeper, the trees take over. The forest holds many different species, but look especially for the banyan trees. Their aerial roots drop from the branches like thick ropes and slowly grow into new trunks. In Balinese belief, the banyan is a sacred tree. You may see some wrapped in black-and-white checked cloth, called poleng. That pattern marks something as spiritually important, and it represents the balance between good and evil, light and dark.

The sanctuary is not only a forest. It is also a sacred site with three temples. The largest is Pura Dalem Agung Padangtegal. A pura dalem is a temple linked to death and to the god Shiva in his role as destroyer and transformer. The temple is believed to date back several centuries. Its gateways are carved from grey volcanic stone, now softened with moss. Look for the stone figures guarding the entrances, with wide eyes and bared teeth.

The second temple is Pura Beji, a bathing temple. It sits beside a holy spring. Before major ceremonies, worshippers come here to purify themselves with the water. The third is Pura Prajapati, which is connected to the village cemetery nearby. In Balinese Hindu tradition, the dead are often buried for a time before a later cremation ceremony. So these three temples follow the cycle of life itself: purification, worship, and death.

A gentle reminder. The inner courtyards of these temples are places of active prayer. Visitors are generally not allowed inside unless they come to worship and wear proper temple dress. You can still admire the carved walls and gateways from the paths. If a ceremony is taking place, you may see villagers in white and gold, carrying offerings of flowers, rice, and fruit stacked high on their heads. Please give them space and keep your voice low.

Now, the forest's most dramatic corner. Follow the path down towards the ravine, where a stream runs through a narrow gorge. Here you will find a stone bridge guarded by carved serpent-dragons, called naga in Balinese and Hindu tradition. Their long bodies curl along the railings, green with moss. Giant trees lean over the water, and their roots grip the rock walls. The light down here turns soft and green, and the sound of running water fills the air.

Why monkeys, in a holy place? In Hindu storytelling, monkeys hold a respected role. The epic called the Ramayana tells of Hanuman, a brave monkey hero who helps the god Rama rescue his wife. Balinese dance and carving often show Hanuman and his monkey army. You may spot monkey figures among the stone sculptures here. Local tradition holds that the forest and its animals are protected precisely because the place is sacred.

A few practical words, for your sake and theirs. These are wild animals, quick and very clever. They know that bags often hold food. Keep snacks, plastic bags, and loose items out of sight. Hold tight to sunglasses, hats, and phones. Do not stare directly into a monkey's eyes, because many primates read that as a challenge. If one climbs onto you, stay calm and stand still. Usually it will simply climb off again.

As you head back towards the exit, notice the edges of the forest. Beyond the trees, Ubud returns: scooters, shop fronts, the smell of frying garlic and coffee. Yet the boundary is thin. Monkeys sometimes sit on the walls, watching the traffic go by. Pause for a moment under the last tall trees. Feel the cooler air on your skin one more time, and listen. Leaves are still rustling above you. Somewhere, a small body is still leaping from branch to branch.

Before you go

Practical tips

Hide all food and plastic bags before entering.
Secure sunglasses, hats, and phones against curious hands.
Avoid direct eye contact with the monkeys.
Stay still and calm if a monkey climbs on you.
Visit early morning for cooler air and calmer paths.
