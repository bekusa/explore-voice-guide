The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below the stone steps, water runs over rock, a steady sound. The air turns cooler and damper as you go down. Moss covers the edges of the paving, and incense drifts in from a small shrine. This is Goa Gajah, the Elephant Cave, a short drive from the centre of Ubud. It sits in a green hollow where an old sanctuary meets a stream valley.

Before any history, look at the cave itself. It is cut straight into a wall of soft volcanic rock. The doorway sits inside a huge carved face. The wide eyes bulge, and the mouth is the entrance. All around it, the stone is carved with waves of leaves, rocks, animals and small human figures. Over many centuries, the rain has softened every edge, so the carving looks almost alive.

Who is the face? Nobody knows for certain. Many guides call it Bhoma, a guardian figure in Balinese art whose face often appears above temple gateways. Others link it to Kala, a figure of time and destruction. Either way, the idea is the same. A fierce face guards a sacred space and frightens away harm. You walk into the mouth of the guardian, and you leave the ordinary world behind you.

Here is the first surprise. There are no wild elephants in Bali, and there probably never were. So why the name? One common explanation points to the statue of Ganesha inside the cave. Ganesha is the Hindu god with an elephant's head, the remover of obstacles. Another explanation links the name to an older name for the nearby river. A fourteenth-century Javanese court poem mentions a place with a similar name, and many scholars connect it to this site.

Now step inside, if you are ready to bend your head a little. The cave is small, dark and close. A short passage leads to a T-shaped space. Niches are cut into the walls, and these were probably places for meditation or rest. The air smells of smoke and wet stone. When your eyes adjust, you can see the soot from countless offerings on the walls and ceiling.

At one end of the passage stands Ganesha. Families often leave flowers, rice and small woven baskets in front of him. At the other end you will find a set of stone lingams, the upright forms that represent the god Shiva, set on a base. These objects are part of daily worship in Balinese Hinduism. Please treat them gently. To many people here, this is not a ruin. It is a working holy place.

When did people carve all this? Most historians place the sanctuary around the eleventh century. This was a time when Hindu and Buddhist ideas both shaped life on Bali, often side by side. Balinese kingdoms had close links with Java at that time. Royal courts supported religious sites, and hermits and monks used caves like this one for meditation, away from the villages.

Walk back into the open courtyard and look at the large rectangular pool. This is the second surprise. For a long time, these bathing pools were hidden under soil and debris. Archaeologists uncovered them only in the mid-twentieth century. The cave itself had already been described by Dutch visitors earlier in that century. So the site you see today came back into view in stages.

The pools are a quiet masterpiece. Along the back wall stand carved female figures. Each one holds a vessel at her waist, and water flows out of the vessel into the basin below. The water comes from springs, and it has flowed through these figures for hundreds of years. In Bali, water from a spring carries spiritual power. People still come to sacred springs to purify themselves before ceremonies.

Now follow the path down toward the stream. The steps are steep and often wet, so take your time. The forest grows thicker here. Huge trees spread their roots over the rocks, and the sound of water grows louder. Many visitors stop at the cave and the pools and turn back. That is a mistake, because the southern side shows a different part of the story.

Across the small bridge, you find Buddhist remains. Here are broken pieces of carved stone, including parts of a stupa, a dome-shaped Buddhist monument. There are also weathered Buddha figures. Some of the carvings fell from the cliff long ago and now lie half-hidden among the roots and ferns. This mix of Hindu and Buddhist elements in one place is the real heart of Goa Gajah. It shows two traditions sharing one valley.

Near the pools, look also for a small figure of a woman surrounded by children. Many people identify her as Hariti, a figure from Buddhist tradition. Legend says she was once a demon who stole children, and she became their protector after a lesson from the Buddha. In Bali, she is known locally by another name and appears in folk stories about a mother with very many children. It is a gentle image in a place guarded by a fierce face.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment by the water. Look up at the green slope, the moss on the steps, the smoke rising from a fresh offering. A woman in a white sash may be placing flowers on a shrine. A rooster may call from the village above. As you climb back up the stairs, listen again to the stream behind you. It has been running through this valley much longer than the stone has been carved.

Key facts

What to look for

Female figures pouring spring water into the bathing pool.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear a sarong to enter; one is usually provided.
Wear shoes with good grip for the wet stone steps.
Come early to see the cave without big tour groups.
Continue down to the stream to see the Buddhist ruins.
Bring a small torch for the dark cave interior.
