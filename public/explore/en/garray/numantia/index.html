The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. Up here on this low hill above the village of Garray, it rarely stops. It moves through dry grass and over pale stones, and it carries a cool edge even in summer. Below you, rivers wind through open farmland. Look around slowly. This quiet hilltop was once one of the most stubborn places in the ancient world... a town that Rome could not take by force, only by patience.

The people who lived here are known as Celtiberians. The name describes a mix of Celtic and Iberian cultures that grew up across the high plateau of central Spain. They were farmers, herders and skilled metalworkers. They built their town on this rise because it was easy to defend. Rivers protected it on several sides, and the slopes gave a clear view of anyone approaching. That choice of ground would matter enormously.

In the second century before Christ, Rome was pushing deeper into the Iberian Peninsula. The Celtiberians resisted, and the conflict that followed is now called the Numantine War. For years, Roman armies came to these walls and went away again. Some commanders were defeated. Some made treaties that the Roman Senate later refused to honour. For Rome, this small town on a windy plateau became an embarrassment — and a problem that had to be solved.

The man sent to solve it was Scipio Aemilianus, one of the most celebrated generals of his age. He had already destroyed Carthage. He arrived here around 134 BC, and he did something different. He did not attack the walls head-on. Instead, he decided to surround the whole town and starve it out. Here is the first surprise for many visitors... the siege of Numantia is one of the best-studied siege operations of the ancient world.

Scipio's soldiers built a continuous wall right around the hill, with towers and a ring of fortified camps. Ancient writers describe it, and archaeologists have traced it on the ground. The ring is usually estimated at around nine kilometres long. The Romans even blocked the river, so that no boat or swimmer could slip supplies inside. As you look out across the fields, try to imagine that line of earth, stone and timber running across the landscape.

Inside the walls, food ran out. The ancient historian Appian, writing long after the event, describes terrible hunger in the final months. When surrender finally came in 133 BC, many of the defenders had already chosen death rather than capture. The survivors were sold into slavery, and the town was destroyed. It is a hard story. Standing here, in this silence, it is worth letting it settle for a moment.

That ending turned Numantia into a symbol. In Spain, the word "numantino" still describes fierce, stubborn resistance against impossible odds. Writers took up the story too. Miguel de Cervantes, the author of Don Quixote, wrote a play about the siege, sometimes known in English as The Siege of Numantia. So this hill has lived a second life — not only in stone, but in language and literature.

Now look at the ground around you. Here is the second surprise. Much of what you see is not the Celtiberian town that Scipio besieged. After the war, a Roman town grew up on the same hilltop. Many of the street lines and house foundations visible today belong to that later Roman settlement. Layers of history sit on top of each other here, and archaeologists have worked carefully to separate them.

Walk along the streets and notice their pattern. They cross each other in a fairly regular grid, with paving stones and raised edges in places. Some stones are worn smooth. The low walls of houses show how the space was divided into rooms and yards. Rain and frost have shaped everything here, so look closely at the joints between the stones. You can see where old walls have shifted, settled, and been carefully consolidated by conservators.

The serious study of this site began in the early twentieth century. A German archaeologist named Adolf Schulten became closely linked with Numantia. He searched for the Roman siege camps in the surrounding hills and published his findings. Spanish teams also excavated the town itself across many seasons. Their work helped turn ancient written accounts into something you can see on the ground and on a map.

On the site, look for the reconstructed buildings. They are modern rebuilds, made to help visitors picture daily life. One shows a Celtiberian house, low and practical, with thick walls against the cold. Another shows a Roman house, with a different layout and a different sense of space. Stepping between them is a simple way to feel the change from one culture to the next on the very same hill.

Many of the finest objects from these excavations are now in the Numantine Museum in the nearby city of Soria. Celtiberian painted pottery is especially striking there, with bold patterns, birds, horses and human figures. If you have time after this visit, the museum is a natural next stop. Seeing the pottery after walking these streets makes both experiences richer, because you can picture the hands that used them here.

This is also a place of weather and sky. The plateau sits high, so clouds move fast and light changes quickly. Morning and late afternoon bring long shadows that make the low walls stand out clearly. In summer, the heat can be strong and there is little shade. In colder months, the wind cuts through. Either way, the open horizon gives you the same wide view the defenders once watched.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look across the fields toward the distant hills where Roman camps once ringed this town. Listen to the wind in the grass, and maybe a bird overhead. Nothing here is loud or dramatic now. That is part of its power. As you walk back toward Garray, notice how small this hill looks from below — and remember how long it stood firm.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a windproof layer; the hilltop is exposed and breezy.
Visit early morning or late afternoon for softer, angled light.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven paving and loose stones.
Carry water and sun protection; shade is very limited.
Pair your visit with the Numantine Museum in Soria.
