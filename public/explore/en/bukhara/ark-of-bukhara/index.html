The guide

The story

Start at the bottom of the ramp. Feel the slope under your feet, the worn brick and packed earth rising toward a gate set between two heavy towers. On a warm day the walls throw back the heat. Swallows cut across the sky above the battlements. The fortress in front of you is not just a building. It is a hill of human making, raised and rebuilt by people over more than a thousand years.

This is the Ark of Bukhara, the old citadel of one of the great cities of the Silk Road. For centuries, whoever ruled Bukhara lived up there, behind these walls. Kings, governors, khans, and finally the emirs of Bukhara all held court on this artificial plateau. The city spread out around it. The citadel stayed at the centre, looking down on markets, mosques and caravan routes. If you wanted to understand who held power here, you only had to look up.

The walls are the first thing to study. Notice how they lean inward as they rise. That slope is not decoration. Walls built of rammed earth and mud brick are stronger when they taper, because the weight settles toward the base. In places you can see fired brick laid over older earth, the marks of repair after repair. Look closely and the colour shifts from honey to grey to pale sand. Each patch is a different century of builders.

Nobody knows exactly when the first fortress stood on this spot. Archaeologists have found layers of settlement deep inside the mound, and medieval writers already described a citadel here more than a thousand years ago. Legend offers a more romantic answer. According to one popular tale, the hero Siyavush, a prince from Persian epic poetry, built the first fortress here. It is a story, not a record. But it shows how old this place felt, even to people living in the Middle Ages.

Now turn around and look at the open ground behind you. This is the Registan, which simply means a sandy place. It was the main public square of old Bukhara. Here, traders set up stalls, crowds gathered for ceremonies, and the ruler's decisions were announced. It was also a place of punishment. Executions happened in this square, in full view of the gate. Today it is open and quiet, but for centuries it was the stage where Bukhara watched its rulers act.

One of those moments became famous far beyond Central Asia. In 1842, two British officers, Charles Stoddart and Arthur Conolly, were executed in front of the Ark on the orders of Emir Nasrullah. Both men had come to Bukhara during the long rivalry between Britain and Russia for influence in Central Asia, a contest often called the Great Game. Before their deaths, they spent long months in harsh captivity. Their fate shocked Victorian Britain and gave Bukhara a fearsome reputation in Europe.

Across the square, you may notice a mosque with a tall porch held up by slender wooden columns. That is the Bolo Hauz Mosque, built in the early eighteenth century. Its name refers to the pool in front of it. The emirs prayed there on Fridays, walking from the citadel across the square. Here is a detail many visitors miss: the columns are carved from single trunks of wood, and their reflections in the water seem to double the porch.

Walk up the ramp toward the gate. The passage turns dark and cool, and the sound of your footsteps changes as the walls close in. The entrance was designed to slow down anyone who came uninvited. A covered corridor climbs steadily, with small rooms opening off it. In earlier times, guards and officials worked here. Every visitor to the ruler had to make this same slow walk upward, under watching eyes.

At the top, the space opens up — and this is the first surprise. Much of the interior is empty ground. Once this plateau held a dense town of its own: palaces, offices, a treasury, stables, a mint, homes for servants and soldiers. Most of it is gone. In 1920, the Red Army attacked Bukhara, and the citadel was badly damaged in the fighting and fires. The last emir, Alim Khan, fled the city. With him, the long era of the emirs came to an end.

What survives is still worth slow attention. Look for the courtyard where rulers received guests and held ceremonies. A raised porch with carved wooden columns faces the open yard, and from there the ruler watched the court below. Nearby you will find a small Friday mosque with a painted wooden ceiling. Tilt your head back. The colours are soft now, reds, blues and greens, but the patterns are still sharp and precise.

Several rooms inside the citadel now hold museum displays. You can see coins, manuscripts, weapons, textiles and objects from daily life in old Bukhara. Old photographs show the city before the damage of 1920, and they are worth finding. Compare them with what you see from the walls today. That second surprise comes quietly: you are standing inside a place that has lost most of its buildings, yet still holds the shape of its power.

From the edge of the walls, look out over the city. The rooftops are flat and brown, broken by turquoise domes and the tall minaret of the Kalon complex rising in the distance. This old centre of Bukhara is protected as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. From up here you can see how the streets and markets grew outward from this mound. The citadel was the anchor, and the city formed around it.

Before you leave, pause at the gate once more and look back down the ramp. Imagine the sound of drums, horses on the stones, the voice of a herald calling across the square. Then listen to what you hear now: tour groups, a vendor calling out, wind moving along the walls. As you walk down into the Registan, turn once and watch the walls change colour in the light. They have done this every evening for a very long time.
