The guide

The story

Listen first. A dog barks somewhere behind a mud-brick wall. A bicycle bell rings in the lane. Somebody is sweeping a doorstep, and the dry scratch of the broom carries a long way in this quiet corner of Bukhara. Then you turn the last corner, and four towers rise in front of you. Each one carries a small dome of turquoise tile. They stand in a tight square around a central block, and they look almost playful after the heavy mosques of the old town.

This is Chor Minor. The name comes from Persian, and it means simply four minarets. But here is the first surprise. These are not really minarets at all. Nobody ever climbed them to call the faithful to prayer. They are decorative towers set at the corners of a gatehouse. The building in front of you was the entrance to a madrasa, a school for Islamic learning. What you see today is the doorway to a place that has almost entirely disappeared.

The gatehouse dates to the early nineteenth century, generally given as 1807. Its builder was a wealthy merchant, usually named as Khalif Niyaz-kul. He was not a ruler and not a great religious figure. He was a man who had made money in trade and chose to spend some of it here. That gives the building a different character from the royal monuments elsewhere in the city. It feels personal, a little eccentric, and made to one man's taste.

Look at the proportions for a moment. The central block is a cube, topped by a low dome. The four towers rise from its corners and stand slightly above it. They are built of fired brick, the warm honey colour that covers so much of Bukhara. The bands of glazed blue tile sit near the top, just below the domes. In strong sun, the brick turns almost golden and the blue looks deeper. On a cloudy day, the whole building looks softer and more grey.

Now walk slowly around the outside, and compare the towers one by one. This is the detail many visitors miss. The towers are not identical. The patterns in the tilework and brickwork differ from tower to tower. Some people have tried to read meaning into these designs. Popular interpretations link the motifs to different faiths or ideas. These readings are interesting, but they are interpretations, not documented facts. The simple truth is that each tower was given its own decoration.

Why four towers? Here we move into legend. One popular local tale says the merchant had four daughters, and each tower stands for one of them. Their different patterns, the story goes, reflect four different personalities. Another tale says the towers represent four philosophies or religions. No record confirms either story. Still, the daughters' legend is the one locals like to tell, and it fits the gentle, family feeling of the building.

Another idea you may hear is that the merchant saw a similar four-towered gate on his travels in India. People often compare Chor Minor with the Charminar in Hyderabad, whose name also means four minarets. The resemblance in the name is real. Whether the Bukhara builder actually took his idea from India is a matter of guesswork. It is better to treat it as a pleasant possibility than a proven link.

So what was inside? The central domed room above the entrance is traditionally described as a library for the madrasa. Small rooms in the towers served practical purposes, and a stair leads up inside the structure. Beyond the gate, there was once a courtyard with student cells, a mosque, and a pool of water. Very little of that survives. You may notice low traces of old walls nearby. The gate outlived the school it was built to serve.

That pool deserves a word. In old Bukhara, these pools were called hauz, and the city once had many of them. They held drinking and washing water, and people gathered around them in the heat. They were also a source of disease, because the water sat still. Most were drained in the twentieth century. When you stand here, try to imagine water beside the gate, shade from trees, and students coming and going.

Here is the second surprise. The building you see is not exactly the one the merchant built. In the 1990s, one of the four towers partly collapsed. It had to be rebuilt, and restoration work followed. If you look closely, you may sense small differences in the brick or tile between the towers. Old buildings in Bukhara have always been repaired, patched, and rebuilt. Chor Minor is part of that long cycle of care, not a frozen object.

The gate is part of the historic centre of Bukhara, which is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. Yet it sits apart from the main monuments. You came here through narrow residential lanes, past wooden doors and children playing. This is a living neighbourhood, called a mahalla in Uzbek. People hang washing here. They carry bread home. Please remember that the houses around you are homes, and keep your voice gentle.

You will likely find a small souvenir stall inside the gatehouse or beside it. Families have sold postcards, embroidery, and small crafts here for years. Some visitors are allowed up to the roof, depending on the day and the people looking after the building. If you go up, the view shows the old town as a sea of flat roofs and small domes. It is a rare chance to see Bukhara from above, close to the scale of ordinary life.

Before you leave, step back across the lane and look once more at the whole front. Four towers, four blue domes, one quiet doorway. A merchant built it, a school grew behind it, and then the school faded away. The gate is still here, still in honey-coloured brick, still catching the light. As you walk back toward the centre, listen again for the broom, the bicycle, and the voices behind the walls.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in early morning when the lanes are quiet.
Ask at the stall about climbing to the roof.
Walk all four sides to compare the towers.
Keep voices low in the residential lanes around it.
Combine with a walk from the Lyabi-Hauz area.
