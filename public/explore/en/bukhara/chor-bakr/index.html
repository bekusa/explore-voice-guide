The guide

The story

Listen to the quiet first. Out here, a short drive west of Bukhara's old town, the traffic noise fades. You hear sparrows in the mulberry trees and footsteps on packed earth. Somewhere a broom moves across baked brick. The light is pale and dusty, and it settles on rows of tan walls that look, at first, like a small walled town. In a way, that is exactly what this is... a town built for the dead.

This is Chor-Bakr, a memorial complex and family necropolis on the edge of the village of Sumitan. The name is usually translated as "Four Bakrs." It refers to four men who each carried the name Abu Bakr. Local tradition holds that they were brothers, and that all four were respected religious figures. They are remembered as descendants of the Prophet Muhammad. People came here to honour them, and over the centuries the site grew.

The central figure of the story is Abu Bakr Said, a holy man of the tenth century. His grave became a place of pilgrimage long before most of the buildings you see today existed. For many generations it was a modest site — a tomb, a few graves around it, and the steady arrival of visitors who came to pray. The real transformation came several hundred years later, with one powerful family.

That family was the Juybari sheikhs. They were leaders of the Naqshbandi Sufi order — a mystical tradition of Islam that has deep roots in Bukhara. The Juybaris claimed descent from the Abu Bakrs buried here. In the sixteenth century they became very rich and very influential. They owned land, they advised rulers, and they shaped the religious life of the city. Chor-Bakr became their family seat in death.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The Juybari sheikhs were not just spiritual guides — they were political partners of the Shaybanid dynasty, the rulers of Bukhara at that time. They were especially close to Abdullah Khan the Second, one of the strongest rulers of the sixteenth century. Royal support flowed into this site. The large buildings at the centre of the complex date mostly from that period of patronage.

Walk toward the main ensemble and look at its three key buildings. There is a mosque for prayer. There is a madrasa, a religious school where students studied the Quran and Islamic law. And there is a khanaka, a lodge where Sufi followers gathered, prayed, and sometimes lived. Together they made Chor-Bakr more than a cemetery. It was a working centre of faith, teaching, and community life.

Notice the tall portals, called pishtaqs in Central Asian architecture. A pishtaq is a high rectangular frame around a deep arched entrance. It works like a stage set, making the doorway feel monumental. Look at the brickwork. Much of the surface is plain, warm brick, with patches of glazed tile in turquoise and deep blue. Compared with the tiled facades in central Bukhara, the decoration here feels restrained and calm.

Now turn away from the big buildings and walk into the lanes between the walls. This is the second surprise. The necropolis is laid out like a real town, with narrow streets and walled courtyards. Each courtyard is a family enclosure called a hazira. Inside, members of the Juybari clan and their relatives were buried together. Behind each wall is a private garden of graves.

Peek through the openings. You will see gravestones set in rows, some carved with flowing Arabic script, some simple and worn smooth. Many enclosures have small niches or covered porches facing the graves. Families sat here during visits, to pray and to remember. The idea is gentle and practical. If the dead live on a street, the living can come and visit them, almost like neighbours.

The atmosphere is part of the experience. Chor-Bakr is still a place of pilgrimage. You may see local families walking slowly between the tombs. Some tie small strips of cloth to branches as a sign of a wish or a prayer. Others touch a wall, then touch their face. Please move quietly here. Dress modestly, keep your voice low, and ask before you photograph people who are praying.

The site went through hard times. In the Soviet period, religious buildings across Central Asia were neglected or used for other purposes. Many lost their communities and their caretakers. After Uzbekistan became independent in 1991, attention returned to places like this one. The complex has since been restored, and you can see the difference between newer repair work and older, weathered surfaces. Look closely, and the stone shows both layers.

Take a moment in the open courtyard near the main buildings. The trees here give real shade, and that matters in a Bukhara summer. Old Central Asian builders understood heat. They used thick walls, deep arches, and shaded porches to keep spaces cool. Stand under one of the arches and feel the air temperature change. It is a simple lesson in desert architecture that still works today.

Think about what this place meant to Bukhara. The city was famous across the Islamic world as a centre of learning and faith. Its saints and scholars were buried in sites like this one, just outside the city walls. Pilgrims visited them as part of a wider circuit of holy places. Chor-Bakr is one stop on that old route, and it gives you a quieter view of Bukhara than the crowded centre.

Before you leave, stand still once more and look down one of the narrow lanes. Brick walls, a blue patch of tile, a strip of sky above. A bird lands on a dome and flies off again. Four men named Abu Bakr gave this place its name, and one family built it into a town. As you walk back to the gate, listen again... the quiet is the same quiet that pilgrims have heard here for centuries.
