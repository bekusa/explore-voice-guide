The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the grass on the slope across the valley. Sheep call somewhere out of sight. Then your eye settles on the hillside, and the shape comes together: a huge pale figure cut into the green turf. It is a man with a raised club in one hand, outlined in white chalk. He has watched over this small Dorset village for centuries. Yet nobody can say for certain who made him, or why.

This is the Cerne Abbas Giant, one of the best known hill figures in Britain. A hill figure is a design made by cutting away the turf to expose the pale rock underneath. Here in Dorset that rock is chalk, so the lines show white against the grass. The giant is roughly fifty-five metres tall, taller than many church spires. He stands on the side of what locals call Giant Hill, just north of the village.

Look closely and you will see that the figure is made only of lines. There is no solid shape, only an outline. The head, the eyes, the ribs, the nipples and the knotted club are all drawn with narrow trenches. Those trenches are packed with crushed chalk. And yes, as you can probably see from here, the giant is very clearly male. Generations of visitors have giggled at that detail, and many still do.

Now the puzzle. For a long time, the oldest written mention of the giant came from 1694. In that year, the churchwardens of Cerne Abbas recorded a payment for repairing the figure. That is a useful clue, but it only proves he existed by then. It says nothing about when he was first cut. Strangely, earlier surveys of this land, and the records of the nearby abbey, seem to stay silent about him.

That silence produced several competing theories. One idea held that the giant was ancient, perhaps a Celtic god or a Romano-British image of Hercules, the club-carrying hero of Greek and Roman myth. Another theory pointed to the seventeenth century. Some historians suggested the figure was a political joke, mocking Oliver Cromwell, the army leader who ruled England after the civil war. Each theory had supporters, and the arguments ran for many years.

Then science stepped in. In 2021, the National Trust, which looks after the site, published the results of a long study. Researchers took samples of soil from the deepest layers beneath the chalk lines. They used a technique that measures when grains of sand were last exposed to sunlight. The results pointed to the late Saxon period, roughly a thousand years ago. That surprised almost everyone, because it fitted none of the favourite theories neatly.

There was a second surprise in the soil. The team also found tiny snail shells in the samples. Some of these belonged to species that are thought to have arrived in Britain only in the medieval period. That detail supported a medieval date for at least part of the figure. Even so, the study left questions open. The giant may have been cut, abandoned, grown over and recut more than once. He may even have looked different in the past.

This matters because of what stood in the valley below. Cerne Abbas grew up around a Benedictine abbey, a community of monks, founded in the late tenth century. Today only fragments remain, a short walk from the village centre. It is odd to picture a large naked figure on a hill above a monastery. Some writers suggest the giant may be older than the abbey, or connected to pagan beliefs the monks tried to replace. Nobody knows for sure.

Look just above the giant's head. You may make out a small, rectangular earthwork, a set of low banks in the grass. Locals call it the Trendle. Its age and purpose are also uncertain. According to local tradition, villagers once raised a maypole here for May Day celebrations. Folk customs like these leave few written records, so treat the story as tradition rather than proven history. Still, it shows how long this hill has been part of village life.

The giant also carries a reputation as a fertility symbol. Local folklore says that couples hoping for a child would visit the figure. Some tales claim that sitting or sleeping on him helped. These are legends, passed from one generation to the next, and they are hard to date. But they help explain why this hillside draws people who come for more than history. Today, though, visitors are asked to stay off the figure to protect the fragile lines.

The giant only survives because people keep him. Grass and weeds spread quickly over chalk, and an untended hill figure can vanish within a few decades. So the outline is cleaned and rechalked from time to time, often with help from volunteers. They carry fresh chalk up the steep slope and tamp it into the trenches by hand. During the Second World War, the figure was covered over. The concern was that enemy pilots could use it as a landmark.

The giant has a playful modern life too. In 2007, a huge image of the cartoon character Homer Simpson, holding a doughnut, was painted on the grass beside him. It was a promotion for a film, and it washed away with the rain. Some local people disliked the stunt, while others found it funny. Either way, it showed how strongly this old figure still catches public attention, far beyond Dorset.

Before you leave, take one more slow look at the hillside. Notice how the white lines change with the light. In soft morning or evening sun they look raised and sharp. Under a grey sky they flatten into the grass. Behind you, the village carries on with its stone cottages, its church and its quiet lanes. As you walk away, glance back once more. The giant will still be there, club raised, keeping his secrets for a little longer.

Before you go

Practical tips

Use the roadside viewpoint for the clearest full view of the giant.
Visit in low morning or evening light for sharper outlines.
Wear sturdy shoes if you walk the steep footpaths on Giant Hill.
Stay off the figure itself to protect the chalk lines.
Combine your visit with a walk to the abbey remains.
