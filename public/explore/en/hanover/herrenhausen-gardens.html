The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your shoes, a soft and even sound, as if the path has been raked this morning. Ahead, hedges stand in perfect walls of green, trimmed so sharply that the edges look drawn with a ruler. Somewhere a fountain hisses and falls. The air smells of cut leaves and damp stone. This is Herrenhausen, on the northwest side of Hanover, and the first thing it teaches you is order.

Herrenhausen is not one garden but a family of them. The Great Garden is the formal baroque heart, where you are likely standing now. Beside it lies the Berggarten, a botanical garden full of plants and glasshouses. Across the road stretch the Georgengarten and the Welfengarten, softer landscape parks in the English style. Together they show two very different ideas of nature. One idea controls it. The other lets it wander.

The Great Garden took its shape in the late seventeenth century, under the rulers of Hanover. The person most connected with it is Sophia, Electress of Hanover. She loved this place deeply. Popular accounts often say she called the garden her life, and the phrase has followed her ever since. The layout was carried out with the help of a French gardener, Martin Charbonnier, who brought the geometry of French court gardens to northern Germany.

Look at the plan around you. Baroque means a style from roughly the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries that loved symmetry, drama, and grand effect. Here, that means straight axes, square beds, and patterns of flowers and gravel laid out like embroidery on cloth. Paths cross at exact angles. Statues mark the corners. Everything points somewhere. The garden was designed to be seen from above, from palace windows, as one great living picture.

Now, a surprise that many visitors miss. Sophia came very close to becoming Queen of Great Britain. Under an English law of 1701, she was named heir to the British throne. But she died in 1714, only weeks before Queen Anne. Many accounts say she collapsed while walking here, in her own garden. Her son became King George the First of Great Britain. For more than a century afterwards, Britain and Hanover shared the same ruler.

That link explains a lot about the city. From 1714 until 1837, the Hanoverian kings mostly lived in London. The court in Hanover grew quieter. Some historians believe this absence helped the Great Garden survive in its old baroque form. Elsewhere in Europe, fashion changed, and formal gardens were torn up and replaced with romantic parks. Here, with the rulers far away, the old geometry simply stayed.

Walk toward the water, and you will hear the Great Fountain before you see it. Its jet of water climbs high above the hedges, many storeys into the air. It is often described as one of the highest garden fountains in Europe. Getting water to rise like this was a serious engineering problem for the baroque age. Builders worked on it for many years before the fountain could perform as the rulers wished.

Notice also the long canal that surrounds the Great Garden. It is a moat of still, dark water, framing the whole rectangle like the edge of a mirror. Swans and ducks drift along it. The canal does more than look elegant. It marks the boundary between the controlled world inside and the wider landscape outside. Stand on one of the bridges, and you can see both worlds at once.

One of the quietest corners is the hedge theatre. Here, the walls, wings, and stage are made from clipped green hedges instead of wood or stone. Gilded figures stand among the greenery and catch the light. It is considered one of the oldest hedge theatres in Europe. In summer, performances and concerts still take place here, so this space has kept its original purpose for centuries.

Now for the second surprise. Find the grotto, a small cave-like building at the edge of the garden. In the baroque age, grottoes were cool, shadowy rooms decorated with shells and stones. In the early 2000s, the French artist Niki de Saint Phalle redesigned this one. Today its walls glitter with mirrors, coloured glass, and mosaic. The mix of old garden and modern art feels playful, almost like stepping inside a jewel box.

At the head of the garden stands Herrenhausen Palace. The original building was destroyed by bombing during the Second World War. For decades, there was only an empty space where the palace had been. In the 2010s, the palace was rebuilt with its historic exterior. Today it holds a museum and rooms for conferences and events. When you look at it, remember that its stone is new, even if its face is old.

Cross into the Georgengarten, and the mood changes completely. The straight lines disappear. Paths curve gently, trees stand in loose groups, and meadows open toward the water. This English landscape style tried to look natural, although it was carefully designed too. Inside this park stands a small round temple honouring Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, the great philosopher and mathematician who worked for the Hanover court and spoke often with Sophia.

The Berggarten offers yet another world. It began as a kitchen garden and grew into a botanical collection with glasshouses full of rare plants. Here also stands a mausoleum of the House of Hanover, a quiet place where members of the ruling family were laid to rest. The calm of the Berggarten makes a good contrast to the show of the Great Garden. It feels like a place for study rather than display.

Before you leave, stop for a moment. Look down one of the long straight paths and let your eye follow it to the end. Listen to the fountains, the gravel, the birds in the hedges. Three centuries of gardeners have clipped these same lines, year after year. As you walk out, notice how the city returns slowly around you, first the trees, then the traffic, then Hanover itself.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come on a summer evening for concerts in the hedge theatre.
Wear comfortable shoes for long walks on gravel paths.
Visit the Berggarten glasshouses on a cold or rainy day.
Cross to the Georgengarten for a quieter, shadier walk.
Check whether the Great Garden requires a separate ticket.
