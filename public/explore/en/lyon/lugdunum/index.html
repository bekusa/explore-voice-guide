The guide

The story

Start with the stone under your feet. On the slope of Fourvière, the old seats of the Roman theatre are worn smooth and slightly uneven, rounded by two thousand years of weather and people. Sit for a moment. Below you, the stage area opens toward the city, and beyond it the roofs of Lyon run down to the river. On a quiet morning, you can hear birds in the trees and the faint traffic of the city below. This is where Lyon began.

The Romans called this place Lugdunum. A Roman colony was founded here in 43 BCE by Lucius Munatius Plancus, a general and politician from the circle of Julius Caesar. The location was carefully chosen. From this hill, you can see the meeting of two rivers — the Rhône and the Saône. Roads, boats, and trade all passed close by. The colony grew quickly, and within a few generations it became one of the most important cities in Roman Gaul.

The name itself is a small puzzle. Most scholars agree that the second part, dunum, is a Celtic word for a fort or a hill settlement. The first part is debated. One popular explanation links it to Lugus, a Celtic god. Another reads it as a word for light or for a raven. Locals sometimes tell a legend about ravens flying over the hill at the founding. It is a good story, but treat it as folklore, not history.

What made Lugdunum special was its role. Under the emperor Augustus, the city became the capital of the Three Gauls — the three large Roman provinces that covered much of modern France and beyond. Governors lived here. Coins were minted here. Each year, delegates from the Gallic tribes gathered at a federal sanctuary on the opposite hill, now called Croix-Rousse, to honour Rome and the emperor. So this was not a provincial town. It was a centre of power.

Now look at the theatre around you. The large theatre was first built in the time of Augustus and later enlarged, probably in the second century. At its largest, it could hold around ten thousand spectators. People came for plays, music, and public events. Notice how the seating follows the natural curve of the hill. The Romans used the slope to save effort and to carry sound. Even today, a voice from the stage reaches the upper rows surprisingly well.

Just beside it sits a second, smaller building — the odeon. It is easy to miss on a first visit. An odeon was a covered or semi-covered hall for music, poetry readings, and speeches, so it was more intimate than the main theatre. Look at its floor near the stage. Parts of it are paved with coloured stone in geometric patterns. Having two such buildings side by side was rare in the Roman world, and it shows how wealthy and ambitious this city was.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The emperor Claudius was born in Lugdunum, in 10 BCE. He was the first Roman emperor born outside Italy. Years later, in 48 CE, he gave a speech in the Roman Senate. He argued that leading men from Gaul should be allowed to become senators. The people of Lugdunum were proud of this. They had the speech engraved on a large bronze tablet and displayed it in their city.

That tablet survived. It was found in 1528, buried in a vineyard on the Croix-Rousse hill. Today it is one of the most important objects in the museum next to the theatres. The bronze is dark and heavy, covered in neat Latin letters. You do not need to read Latin to feel its weight. It is the voice of an emperor, speaking about who belongs, written down by the city where he was born.

The museum itself is worth a moment of attention before you go inside. It is built into the hillside, so from the theatre you barely see it. The building is made of raw concrete. Inside, a long ramp spirals down through the hill, level by level. Large windows look out onto the ruins. The idea is simple and clever: you walk down through the layers of the city's past, and the ancient theatre stays in view beside you.

Inside, look for the mosaics. One of the best known shows a chariot race in a Roman circus. You can see the horses, the drivers, and the long central barrier of the track. It is full of movement and colour. Another object is very different and very rare — a bronze calendar written in the Gaulish language, found in the late nineteenth century. It records months and lucky or unlucky days. Few texts in Gaulish survive anywhere, so this one is precious.

Lugdunum also has a darker chapter. In 177 CE, a group of Christians in the city was persecuted and killed, probably in the amphitheatre on Croix-Rousse. Among them was a young enslaved woman named Blandina. She later became a saint and is still remembered in Lyon today. The story comes from an early Christian letter, so it is one of the earliest written accounts of Christianity in Gaul. The ruins here are not only about entertainment and power.

The theatres are not just ruins to look at. Every summer, they come back to life. A festival called Les Nuits de Fourvière brings concerts, theatre, and dance to these same stone seats. On those evenings, thousands of people sit where Roman audiences once sat. The scale feels different when the rows are full and the stage is lit. If you can return on a summer night, the place makes much more sense.

Before you leave, stand at the top of the theatre and look out once more. The red roofs of the old town lie below. The Saône curves past them, and the newer city spreads beyond. Behind you, the white basilica of Fourvière rises on the hill. All of this grew from the small colony that started here. As you walk away, listen to the gravel under your shoes, and notice how close the ancient city still is to the modern one.

Before you go

Practical tips

Sit in the upper rows to judge the theatre's acoustics.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven, sloping stone seats.
Combine your visit with the nearby Fourvière basilica.
Return on a summer night for Nuits de Fourvière.
Take the funicular up to avoid the steep climb.
