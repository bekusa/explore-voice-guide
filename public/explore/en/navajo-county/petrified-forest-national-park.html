The guide

The story

Crouch down and lay your palm on one of these logs. It is cool in the morning and warm by afternoon. It is heavier and harder than any wood you have ever touched. Run your fingers along it and you can feel bark ridges, knots, and the split ends where a trunk once broke. Then look closer. Inside, the grain glows red, orange, yellow, and violet. This is a tree, and it is also stone. Welcome to Petrified Forest National Park.

You are in northeastern Arizona, on high, dry ground where the wind rarely stops. The park reaches across parts of Navajo County and neighbouring Apache County. A road runs north to south through it, connecting the colourful badlands of the Painted Desert with the log-strewn hills to the south. Interstate 40 crosses the park, and so does the old path of Route 66. Few national parks let you drive past roadside diners and then, minutes later, walk among trees older than the dinosaurs that most people picture.

So how old are these trees? Geologists date them to the Late Triassic period, roughly 225 million years ago. At that time, this land was not desert at all. It sat much closer to the equator, as part of the supercontinent called Pangaea, where all of Earth's continents were joined together. Rivers wound across a wide, humid floodplain. Tall conifers stood along the banks. Ferns, horsetails, and early cycads grew in the shade. The air would have been warm, wet, and loud with water.

Now comes the part most visitors never quite picture. The trees fell, perhaps in floods, and the rivers carried them downstream. They became buried in mud, sand, and volcanic ash. That burial cut them off from oxygen, so they did not simply rot away. Over long ages, groundwater rich in silica soaked into the wood. The silica slowly hardened inside every cell, turning into quartz. The shape of the tree remained, but the tree itself had become rock.

The colours come from small traces of other minerals in that water. Iron gives the warm reds, oranges, and yellows. Manganese adds darker purples, blues, and blacks. Pure quartz stays white or clear. That is why one log can look like a slice of sunset, while the log beside it looks pale and grey. Because quartz is so hard, this stone wood resists scratching better than steel does. Many of the logs here sit exactly where erosion has slowly uncovered them.

The rock layers that hold these trees have a name: the Chinle Formation. You can see it everywhere in the park, as bands of soft clay in grey, blue, lavender, and rust. The clay swells when it rains and shrinks as it dries. This repeated cycle cracks the surface into a crust that looks a little like popcorn. Keep that in mind when you walk out to Blue Mesa. The striped hills there are this same ancient mud, now lifted high and worn by the wind.

The Chinle layers hold more than trees. Paleontologists have found fossils of animals that lived beside those Triassic rivers. Among them were phytosaurs, long-snouted reptiles that looked a lot like crocodiles but were only distant relatives. There were armoured plant-eaters, large amphibians with broad, flat heads, and some of the earliest dinosaurs. Those early dinosaurs were small and lightly built — nothing like the giants that came later. Research here continues, and new fossils still come to light as the hills erode.

People have lived on this land for thousands of years. Near the middle of the park, Puerco Pueblo holds the low stone walls of a village built by ancestral Puebloan people. Close by, at Newspaper Rock, hundreds of petroglyphs cover dark boulders. These are images pecked into the rock surface — spirals, human figures, animals, and shapes whose meanings are not fully understood. Some markings line up with the sun on certain days of the year. For many Native communities in the region, this remains a living landscape, not just an archaeological site.

In the late nineteenth century, the railroad reached this part of Arizona, and the petrified wood began to disappear. Collectors carted it away by the wagonload. Some people even dynamited logs to search for crystals inside. Public concern grew, and in 1906 President Theodore Roosevelt protected the area as a national monument. It became a national park in 1962. Later, much more land was added to the park. Today, it is illegal to take even a small chip of wood away with you.

That rule has inspired a curious tradition. Locals and rangers like to talk about the so-called curse of the petrified wood. According to the popular tale, anyone who pockets a piece will have bad luck. Over the years, the park has received many packages of stolen fragments mailed back with letters of apology. Some letters blame the stone for car crashes, broken bones, or lost jobs. Whether or not you believe in curses, the returned pieces cannot go back to their original spot, so they are kept separately.

At the northern end of the park, the Painted Desert Inn stands on the rim, built from adobe and timber. Workers from the Civilian Conservation Corps — a Depression-era program that gave young men jobs on public land — rebuilt the inn in the 1930s. In the following decade, the architect and designer Mary Colter helped reshape its interiors, and the Hopi artist Fred Kabotie painted murals on its walls. Look out the windows here. The desert below shifts from red to mauve as the light changes.

Further south, near the Rainbow Forest, short paved trails lead you right among the largest logs. Some trunks lie broken into neat sections, as if someone had sliced them on purpose. In fact, quartz is brittle, and the logs cracked cleanly as the ground shifted and settled. At Crystal Forest, the cut ends sparkle with small quartz crystals. At Agate Bridge, a log stretches across a small ravine. In the early twentieth century, people added a concrete support beneath it to stop the log from falling.

Before you go, stand still for a moment and listen. You will likely hear wind moving across the grass, perhaps a raven calling, and maybe the distant sound of a passing train. Look down at the log at your feet. The rings you see marked years of rain and sun in a forest that vanished long before the first flowers bloomed. As you walk back, watch the ground. Small stone splinters glint in the soil everywhere — and every one of them will still be here tomorrow.

Before you go

Practical tips

Drive the park road one way to see desert and forest.
Walk the short Blue Mesa trail for close views of banded hills.
Bring plenty of water and sun protection; shade is scarce.
Visit near sunrise or sunset for the richest desert colours.
Buy petrified wood souvenirs only from shops selling legally sourced pieces.
