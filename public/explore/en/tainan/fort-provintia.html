The guide

The story

Run your hand along the low wall near the entrance. The bricks are a deep, weathered red, softened at the edges, and the mortar between them is pale and lumpy. Above you, curved roofs of grey and green tile turn up at the corners. Incense drifts across the courtyard. Somewhere a scooter hums along the street outside. You are standing in the middle of Tainan, but those old red bricks were laid by Dutch hands more than three and a half centuries ago.

This is Fort Provintia, known locally as Chihkan Tower. The local name means something close to the Red-Haired Tower. In the seventeenth century, people in this region called the Dutch the red-haired people, and the name stayed with the building long after they left. So two stories sit on this small plot of land. One is Dutch and European. The other is Chinese and Taiwanese. You can see both of them at once.

The Dutch East India Company built the first fort here in 1653. At that time the company already held Fort Zeelandia, out on the coast at Anping. But in 1652, Chinese settlers in the area rose up in a revolt led by a farmer named Guo Huaiyi. The Dutch crushed the uprising. Afterwards they wanted a stronger position close to the settlers' farmland. Fort Provintia was the answer — a brick stronghold and trading post facing the water.

And here is the first surprise. Look around you. There is no sea in sight. But when the fort was built, it stood near the shore of a wide, shallow lagoon called the Taijiang Inner Sea. Ships could sail between this fort and Zeelandia across open water. Over the following centuries, rivers carried silt into the lagoon, and it slowly filled with land. The city grew over it. Today the old waterfront lies under streets, shops, and homes.

The Dutch did not hold this place for long. In 1661, the Ming loyalist commander Zheng Chenggong, known in the West as Koxinga, crossed from the mainland with a large fleet. He was fighting the Qing dynasty, which had taken power in China. He needed a new base. Fort Provintia surrendered to him within days of his landing. Fort Zeelandia held out for months, but it also fell, early in 1662. Dutch rule in Taiwan was over.

In the courtyard you will find statues that show this moment. Dutch officers bow before Koxinga as they hand over the fort. It is worth pausing here. Koxinga is a hero to many people in Taiwan and in China, and he is honoured in temples across Tainan. Under his family's rule, this fort became an administrative centre for the new government. Tainan itself became the capital of the island, a role it kept for a very long time.

Now look again at the old brickwork. Very little of the Dutch fort still stands. Earthquakes and long neglect brought down most of the original structure. What remains is mainly foundations and fragments of wall. It is often said that the Dutch builders mixed their mortar with sugar water, sticky rice, and crushed oyster shells. Treat that as a popular account rather than a proven fact, but it is a good story to think about as you study the joints.

The graceful buildings above you came much later. In the late nineteenth century, during the Qing dynasty, Chinese builders raised new halls directly on top of the Dutch foundations. That is the second surprise. The pavilions that look so completely Chinese are resting on a European fort. The red-tiled roofs, the carved beams, and the swallowtail ridges all sit on bricks from a very different world.

The two main buildings are the Sea God Temple and the Wenchang Pavilion. The Wenchang Pavilion honours the god of literature and learning. For generations, students have come here before exams to ask for help. You may still see notes, offerings, or copies of exam admission slips left by hopeful students and their parents. It is a small, everyday ritual, and it gives this old military site a very gentle present-day purpose.

Near the front of the grounds stands a row of stone tablets. Each one rides on the back of a large stone creature that looks like a turtle. In Chinese tradition this creature is called a bixi, a son of the dragon, and it is famous for carrying heavy loads. The tablets were ordered by the Qing emperor Qianlong. They record the defeat of a major rebellion in Taiwan in the 1780s, led by a man named Lin Shuangwen.

There is a well-known legend about these turtles. Locals say that when the stones were shipped across from the mainland, one of them fell into the water as it was being unloaded. According to the tale, it was lost for many years before it was found again. Some versions say people still rub the turtles' heads for luck. Whether every detail is true or not, you will notice that some of the stone is polished smooth by many hands.

Take a moment to walk slowly through the small garden paths. Koi move in the pond. The light changes as it passes through the old trees and falls on the stone. In the afternoon, the walls glow a warmer red. Many visitors climb to the upper floor of the pavilions. From there you can look out over the rooftops and imagine the lagoon that once spread to the west, all the way toward Anping.

This is a small site, and you can see it in less than an hour. But it holds a large part of Taiwan's story in a very compact space. A colonial trading company, a Ming loyalist general, a Qing emperor, students praying for good grades — they all left a mark here. Tainan is a city of temples and old lanes, and this courtyard is a good place to understand why it feels so layered.

Before you leave, stand once more beside the red Dutch bricks and look up at the Chinese roofs above them. Listen to the traffic outside the walls and the quiet sound of footsteps inside them. As you step back onto the street, remember that you are walking on land that was once water. The shoreline of the seventeenth century is somewhere beneath your feet.
