The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. In Waterton, it rarely rests. It comes down off the peaks, runs across the long blue lake, and pushes against your jacket, your hair, and the flags by the shore. Underfoot there may be gravel, or short prairie grass bent flat by that same wind. Ahead, the mountains rise almost without warning. There are no gentle foothills here to ease you in. The plains simply stop, and the Rockies begin.

That sudden meeting is the key to this place. Waterton Lakes National Park sits in the far southwest corner of Alberta, pressed against the border with Montana and close to British Columbia. Parks Canada often describes it as the place where the prairies meet the mountains. You can see it with your own eyes. Turn one way, and the land rolls out flat and golden toward the east. Turn the other, and grey and red peaks stand shoulder to shoulder above the water.

The park is small by the standards of the Canadian Rockies, but it is old. It was first protected in 1895 as a forest reserve, which makes it one of the earliest protected areas in Canada. Over the following decades its boundaries changed several times, and it grew into the national park you are standing in now. For a place that feels so remote, it has been watched over for a long time.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The lakes are named after Charles Waterton, an English naturalist of the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. He was known for his travels in South America and for his interest in birds. But as far as historians know, he never came here. The name was given by Thomas Blakiston, a member of a British survey expedition in the 1850s, who admired Waterton's work. So the park carries the name of a man who never saw it.

Now look south, down the length of Upper Waterton Lake. The far end of the lake lies in another country. In 1932, Canada and the United States joined this park with Glacier National Park in Montana to create the Waterton-Glacier International Peace Park. It was the first park of its kind in the world. The idea was simple: wildlife, water, and mountains do not stop at a border, so the two nations would care for them together.

That shared spirit earned wider recognition later. In 1995, the two parks were listed together as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, valued for their scenery and for the unusual mix of plant and animal life. Prairie species, mountain species, and species from the Pacific side of the continent all overlap here. In early summer, the meadows fill with wildflowers, and botanists come from far away to study them.

The rocks themselves hold a surprise. Many of the layers here are very old — sedimentary rock laid down in shallow seas more than a billion years ago. They are some of the oldest rocks you can see in the Canadian Rockies. And through a slow, enormous movement of the Earth's crust called the Lewis Overthrust, these ancient layers were pushed up and over much younger rock. In other words, the old sits on top of the new... the reverse of what you might expect.

You can see this ancient stone up close in the village. At Cameron Falls, at the edge of the townsite, water pours over ledges of that billion-year-old rock. The layers look like stacked pages, in shades of grey, cream, and rust. Farther up the Red Rock Parkway, Red Rock Canyon shows the same story in bolder colour. There, the stone is a deep red and green, coloured by iron in the rock. Ripple marks from those ancient seas are still pressed into some of the surfaces.

Above the townsite, on a windy hill, stands the Prince of Wales Hotel. It was built in the 1920s by the Great Northern Railway, an American company that wanted to bring tourists north from Glacier. Its steep roofs and wooden balconies were designed to look like a mountain chalet. Locals say the wind was so strong during construction that the frame was pushed out of line more than once. Whether that tale grew in the telling or not, stand on that hill and you will understand why people believe it.

As you move around, keep an eye out for animals. Mule deer often walk calmly through the village streets and graze on lawns. Bighorn sheep sometimes appear on the slopes nearby. Black bears and grizzly bears live in the park too, especially in berry season. A few kilometres from the park entrance, a paddock holds a small herd of plains bison, a reminder of the great herds that once crossed these grasslands.

Not every story here is gentle. In 2017, the Kenow Wildfire swept through the park and burned a large part of it. Flames came close to the townsite, but crews saved the village. As you drive or hike, you may still pass slopes of grey, standing trunks. Look closer, and you will often see new green growth beneath them. Fire is part of the natural cycle of these forests, and the land is already rebuilding itself.

There are many ways to spend your time. A tour boat runs down Upper Waterton Lake, and in some seasons it crosses into the United States, to Goat Haunt in Montana. Hikers come for trails like the Bear's Hump, a short, steep climb with a wide view over the village and lakes. The Crypt Lake trail is longer and more demanding, and it is famous for a ladder and a narrow tunnel through the rock. For a slower day, the shoreline paths around the townsite are easy and quiet.

Before you leave, take a moment to stand still by the water. Notice how the colour of the lake changes as clouds move over the peaks — slate grey one minute, deep blue the next. Listen for the wind again, and for the small sounds under it: waves on the stones, a raven calling, a flag snapping on its pole. Look south, toward the border you cannot see. The mountains you are looking at now have no idea which country they belong to. And perhaps that is exactly the point of this place.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a windproof layer, even on sunny summer days.
Carry bear spray and learn how to use it.
Reserve the lake cruise early in peak summer season.
Climb the Bear's Hump for a view over the townsite.
Check border rules before taking the boat into Montana.
