The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below you, the Yangtze moves against the rock. It is a low, steady sound, more felt than heard. The air here is damp and green. It smells of wet stone, of moss, and faintly of incense drifting from the temple halls above. Ahead, stone steps climb through trees toward curved tiled roofs. This is Baidicheng, the White Emperor City, on a small hill above the mouth of one of China's great gorges.

Look east, toward the place where the mountains seem to close together. That narrow opening is Kuimen, the western gate of Qutang Gorge. Qutang is the first and shortest of the Three Gorges of the Yangtze. Here the river squeezes between cliffs that rise almost straight from the water. For centuries, boatmen feared this passage. Many travellers saw it as the true doorway between the Sichuan basin and the lands of central China downstream.

That view may look familiar, even if you have never been here. A picture of Kuimen appears on the back of the Chinese ten yuan banknote. If you carry one in your pocket, take it out now. Hold it up against the real gorge. Many visitors do exactly this. It is a small ritual, and a strange pleasure, to compare the engraved cliffs on the note with the living ones in front of you.

Now, the name. Why is this a city of a White Emperor? The story goes back about two thousand years, to the troubled years at the end of the Western Han dynasty. A regional warlord named Gongsun Shu took control of this region. He built a fortified town on this hill. Legend has it that a white mist, shaped like a white dragon, rose from a well nearby. Gongsun Shu took this as a sign from heaven. He declared himself the White Emperor, and the town took his title.

His rule did not last. The armies of the restored Han dynasty defeated him, and his short empire ended in the first century. But the name stayed on the hill. Locals say that people here remembered him kindly, and for a long time they built shrines in his honour. Keep that in mind as you walk. The figure you will meet most often inside these halls is not Gongsun Shu at all.

The central story of Baidicheng belongs to the Three Kingdoms period, in the third century. This was an age when the old Han empire broke into rival states. One of those states was Shu Han, ruled by Liu Bei. After a heavy military defeat downstream, Liu Bei fell back to this place. He was old, ill, and grieving. In the year 223, he died here, at Baidicheng.

Before his death, Liu Bei called for his chancellor, Zhuge Liang. Zhuge Liang is one of the most admired figures in Chinese history — a strategist and minister known for loyalty and wisdom. Liu Bei placed his young son and heir into Zhuge Liang's care. This scene is known in Chinese as entrusting the orphan. It became a lasting symbol of trust between a ruler and a loyal servant. Inside the temple, you can see this moment shown in a group of painted statues.

Here is something many visitors miss. The temples on this hill once honoured Gongsun Shu. During the Ming dynasty, roughly five to six hundred years ago, local officials changed the focus of worship. They felt that a rebel who called himself emperor was not a proper figure for a shrine. So the halls turned to Liu Bei, Zhuge Liang, and their companions. The name of one emperor stayed on the hill, while the statues of another filled the rooms.

Baidicheng has a second nickname — the City of Poetry. Many of China's greatest poets came through here, often on boats heading down the river. The most famous verse comes from Li Bai, a poet of the Tang dynasty. In a short poem, he describes leaving the White Emperor City at dawn, under clouds lit with colour. He writes of racing a vast distance downstream in a single day, while monkeys call from both banks, and his light boat passes ten thousand mountains.

Another Tang poet, Du Fu, had a deeper connection to this area. He lived in this region, then known as Kuizhou, for about two years. He was poor, unwell, and far from home. Yet this was one of his most productive periods. He wrote hundreds of poems here, many about autumn, the gorge, and the sorrow of a wandering life. As you walk the grounds, watch for stone tablets carved with poems. Many of them speak about this exact landscape.

The hill itself has changed in your own lifetime. Before the Three Gorges Dam was finished downstream, the river ran much lower here. Baidicheng stood on a high hill, joined to the land. When the great reservoir filled in the early twenty-first century, the water rose many metres. The low ground around the hill disappeared under the river. Today the site sits almost like an island, reached by a long bridge across the water.

So the gorge in front of you is calmer than the one Li Bai knew. The rapids that frightened boatmen for centuries now lie deep below a wide, slow surface. Large cruise ships pass where small wooden boats once fought the current. It is worth thinking about this as you look at Kuimen. The cliffs are the same cliffs. The poems are the same poems. But the river that inspired them has been reshaped.

As you move through the halls, take your time with the details. Notice the dark timber, the red painted columns, the carved roof ridges, and the worn stone of the steps. Notice how the courtyards open suddenly onto views of the water. The builders placed these halls to frame the gorge. Every turn of the path seems to offer one more look east, toward the narrow gate in the mountains.

Before you leave, stand for a moment at the edge of the hill. Let the sound of the river return. Look again at the two great walls of rock and the light moving across them. An exiled poet stood near here. A dying king spoke his last wishes near here. Now it is your turn to look... and then, like so many travellers before you, to carry this view downstream.
