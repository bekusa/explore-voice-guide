The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below you, the Baltic is moving against a narrow strip of sand. Above you, the wind runs through the crowns of old beech trees. The air smells of salt and damp leaves at the same time. There are not many places where sea and forest press this close together. Here, on the island of Wolin, just east of Świnoujście, they meet along a wall of pale, crumbling cliff.

Wolin National Park was created in 1960. It covers a large part of the island, from the high Baltic shore down to the quieter waters of the Szczecin Lagoon. Wolin itself is one of two big islands that frame the mouths of the Oder River. The town of Świnoujście sits mostly on the other one, Usedom, and on the islands between them. So when you travel here from the town, you cross water to reach the park. The crossing is a small reminder that this whole corner of Poland is made of islands, channels and shifting sand.

The park is really several landscapes laid side by side. On the northern edge, there are the cliffs and the open sea. Inland, there are forested moraine hills, low ridges of sand and gravel left behind by retreating glaciers at the end of the last Ice Age. To the south and west, the land flattens into reed beds, marshes and lagoon shoreline. Walk for an hour here, and you can pass through all three. The light changes as you go, from the hard glare of the coast to the green shade under the trees.

The cliffs are the part most visitors come to see. The highest point is Gosań, near the village of Wisełka, and it rises close to one hundred metres above the beach. That makes it one of the tallest sections of cliff anywhere on the Polish coast. From the top, you look straight out over the water, with no land between you and the horizon. On a clear day, the sea turns from grey-green near the shore to a deep, steady blue further out.

Here is something many people miss. These cliffs are not fixed. They are made of soft glacial material, not solid rock, and storms cut into them every year. Chunks of the edge slide down to the beach after heavy rain or winter waves. Fallen trees often lie at the foot of the slope, roots in the air, washed by the surf. The coastline you see today is not quite the coastline of ten years ago. For that reason, the park closes some paths near the edge, and it is wise to respect every barrier you meet.

Behind the cliffs stands the forest, and much of it is beech. Beech woods have a special quality of light. The trunks are smooth and silver-grey, and the canopy is thick, so the ground below often stays open and bare. In spring, the new leaves glow an almost electric green. In autumn, they turn copper and fall in drifts that crunch under your boots. Mixed with the beech, you will also find pine and oak, especially on the sandier soils.

Now, a second surprise. Wolin National Park does not stop at the beach. It includes a strip of the Baltic Sea itself, which makes it unusual among Polish national parks. The protection reaches out into the water along the coast. So when you stand on the sand and look at the waves, you are still inside the park. The sea is treated here as part of the same living system as the forest and the lagoon, not as a separate world.

The symbol of the park is the white-tailed eagle, one of the largest birds of prey in Europe. You may see its image on signs and maps as you walk. These eagles nest in the quiet forests and hunt over the lagoon and the lakes. An adult has broad, dark wings, a pale head and a short white tail. If you see a very large bird gliding low over the water, with wings held flat like a plank, it is worth stopping and watching for a while.

The park is also home to European bison. Near the resort town of Międzyzdroje, there is a bison show enclosure run by the park. The bison here live in a large fenced area of forest, and visitors can watch them from a viewing area. European bison are the heaviest land animals in Europe. They once disappeared completely from the wild, and today they survive thanks to careful breeding programmes. Seeing their dark, shaggy shoulders move between the trees is a quiet, powerful moment, especially for children.

South of the coast, the mood of the park changes. Here, small lakes sit in hollows between the wooded hills. Their water is often still and dark, reflecting the trees around the shore. Further west, toward Świnoujście, lies the delta of the Świna River, a maze of low islands, channels and reed beds. This is bird country. In the migration seasons, flocks of ducks, geese and waders stop here to rest and feed. The sounds are different too — reeds rustling, water birds calling, very little else.

If you want to understand the park before you walk it, the park museum in Międzyzdroje is a good place to start. It presents the animals, plants and landscapes of the island in one place. Many marked trails also begin from Międzyzdroje and lead up into the forest and along the cliff line. One popular viewpoint is Kawcza Góra, a hill near the town that looks out over the sea. The paths can be steep in places, with roots and loose sand underfoot, so comfortable shoes make a real difference.

The best time to visit depends on what you want to feel. Early morning is the quietest. Mist sometimes hangs in the beech woods, and animals are more active. Summer brings long evenings and warm sand, but also many visitors from the resorts along the coast. Autumn may be the most beautiful season, with colour in the forest and a cool, clean wind off the Baltic. Winter is grey and raw, but the empty beaches and storm-cut cliffs have their own strong, simple drama.

Whatever season you come, remember that this is a protected place first and a visitor attraction second. Stay on marked paths. Leave flowers, shells and fallen wood where they are. Keep your voice low in the forest, and you will hear far more — woodpeckers, wind, the distant rhythm of the waves. The park works because people agreed, more than sixty years ago, to let this island stay wild.

So take a moment where you are. Feel the ground under your feet — sand, or roots, or soft leaf litter. Notice the smell of the air, and which way the wind is coming from. Somewhere nearby, the sea is still working at the cliffs, slowly changing the shape of the coast. As you walk on, listen for the moment when the sound of the waves fades behind the trees... and the forest takes over.
