The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through the trees, a dog barks somewhere down the lane, and boards creak softly in the sun. You are standing in front of a small wooden building painted green. It sits at the edge of a quiet village in eastern Poland, a short distance from the Belarusian border. From here, it could almost be a country chapel. Then your eye climbs to the top of the towers, and you see crescents instead of crosses.

This is the Kruszyniany Mosque. It is one of the oldest surviving wooden mosques in Poland. It was most likely built in the second half of the eighteenth century, and it is still in use today. It belongs to the Lipka Tatars. They are a Muslim community whose ancestors have lived in these lands for around six hundred years. Their story is not one of recent migration. It is woven deep into the history of Poland and Lithuania.

To understand why a mosque stands in a Polish farming village, we need to go back to the late Middle Ages. Tatar warriors came from the Golden Horde, a powerful Mongol-led state on the steppes to the east. From the fourteenth century, groups of them settled in the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. They served the Lithuanian rulers as soldiers, especially as skilled cavalry. Over generations, they became part of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, a large shared state that once stretched across much of Central and Eastern Europe.

Kruszyniany has its own particular beginning. In the late seventeenth century, King Jan the Third Sobieski granted land in this area to Tatar officers. According to the accepted account, the land was given in place of military pay that the royal treasury owed them. Sobieski is the same king remembered for leading the relief of Vienna in 1683. So these quiet fields were, in a sense, a soldier's wage. Families settled, farmed, married, and built a place to pray.

Now look closely at the building itself. It is made of timber, built in the same carpentry tradition as the wooden churches of this region. Local craftsmen knew how to work wood, and they used those skills here. That is why the mosque looks so familiar to Polish eyes. The walls are of horizontal and vertical boards. The roof is steep, made to shed snow in hard winters. Small towers rise above the entrance and the main hall. It is an Islamic house of prayer, built in the language of the Polish countryside.

Here is something many visitors miss. Turn slowly and notice which way the building faces. Mosques are oriented toward Mecca, the holiest city in Islam. Inside, a niche in the wall called the mihrab marks that direction for prayer. Even in a small timber mosque in a Polish forest, that ancient rule shapes the whole layout. The line of prayer runs from this quiet village toward Arabia, thousands of kilometres to the south-east.

If you go inside, you will be asked to take off your shoes. The floor is covered with carpets, and the space feels warm and domestic. The interior is divided into two parts. One part is for men, and a smaller section is set aside for women, separated by a partition. Near the mihrab stands the minbar, a raised pulpit with steps. The imam speaks from it during Friday prayers and on holy days. Look up at the wooden ceiling and the simple decoration around you.

On the walls you may see framed texts called muhirs. These are decorative panels with verses from the Koran or religious inscriptions in Arabic script. Making them is a traditional craft among the Polish Tatars. For centuries, the Lipka Tatars gradually lost their original Turkic language. They came to speak Polish, Belarusian, and Lithuanian. Yet they kept the Arabic script for prayer. Some even wrote Slavic languages in Arabic letters, in handwritten religious books known as kitabs. That is a surprising piece of European cultural history.

Now walk a little way from the mosque, toward the trees. There you will find the mizar. That is the word the Tatars use for their Muslim cemetery. It lies in a grove, and it is still an active burial ground. The older stones lean in the grass, often covered in moss. Look at the inscriptions. On some graves you will find Arabic, on others Cyrillic letters, and on many the Latin alphabet of modern Polish. Some stones carry a crescent. The languages on these stones tell the story of a community that adapted again and again.

The mizar is a sacred place. Walk quietly on the paths, and do not step on graves. Notice the family names carved into the stone. Many Tatar surnames in this region sound Polish, but they come from Tatar roots. Over generations, Tatar families served in Polish armies and fought in Polish uprisings. They took part in many of the country's struggles. Here, faith and patriotism did not compete. For many Tatar families, being Muslim and being Polish were simply both true.

The twentieth century brought great change. Borders moved, wars passed through this region, and many Tatar communities were scattered. Today, the Polish Tatar community is small. Many members live in larger cities such as Białystok, Gdańsk, and Warsaw. But they return to Kruszyniany for important holidays, weddings, and funerals. On those days the village fills with cars and voices. The mosque becomes a gathering place again, not just a monument.

Kruszyniany also keeps the Tatar table alive. In the village, you may find places serving traditional Tatar food. Look for pierekaczewnik, a layered pastry rolled with sweet or savoury filling. It is often baked for celebrations and takes great skill to make. Families also serve dumplings and meat dishes with long roots in the community. Food is one of the ways a small culture carries its memory. Sharing a meal here is a quiet way to meet that history.

Before you leave, stand back from the mosque once more. Look at the green boards, the sloping roof, and the crescents against the sky. Listen to the wind in the trees around the mizar. This small building has stood through many winters and many changes of government. It carries the memory of soldiers, farmers, and families who made a home at the far edge of Europe. As you walk back down the lane, notice how ordinary the village looks. That ordinary calm is the real surprise of Kruszyniany.
