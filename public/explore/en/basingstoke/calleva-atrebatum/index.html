The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through the hedgerows. Somewhere a rook calls, and sheep shift in the grass. Under your feet there is only farmland, soft and green. But you are standing inside a Roman town. Its streets, its houses, its marketplace all lie just below the turf. Around you, a ring of grey flint wall still marks where the city ended and the countryside began.

This is Calleva Atrebatum, just outside the village of Silchester, a short drive north of Basingstoke. The name is usually taken to mean something close to the town of the Atrebates in the woods. The Atrebates were a British tribe, a people of this region. Long before Roman soldiers arrived, they had already made this gravel ridge a place of power, trade and authority.

Here is the first surprise. Calleva did not begin as a Roman town. It began as an Iron Age settlement, and an ambitious one. Excavations by the University of Reading found evidence that people here laid out streets on a planned grid before the Roman conquest of 43 CE. That is unusual in Britain. It suggests the local rulers already knew Roman ideas and Roman goods, and wanted to look like part of that wider world.

Coins help tell this story. Rulers of the Atrebates issued their own coinage, and some coins carry abbreviated forms of the town's name. One of the best known names linked to the tribe is Commius, a leader who appears in the writings of Julius Caesar. Later rulers followed him. The details of their politics are still debated by historians... but the coins show that Calleva mattered, and that its people were connected to the Continent across the Channel.

After the conquest, the town grew into a Roman administrative centre. Builders added a forum and a basilica at the heart of the grid. The forum was an open square for markets and public business. The basilica was a large hall for courts and council meetings. Around them stood houses, shops, workshops and temples. Over the following centuries, the town was enclosed first by earth defences, and later by the stone wall you can still walk beside today.

Take a closer look at that wall. It is built mostly from flint, the hard, glassy stone found in the chalk of southern England. At intervals you may notice flatter courses of stone running horizontally. These bonding layers helped hold the rough flint core together. The facing has been robbed and repaired over the centuries, so the surface changes as you walk. In some stretches the wall still stands well above head height. The full circuit runs for roughly two and a half kilometres.

Now the second surprise — and perhaps the most important one. Most Roman towns in Britain became modern towns. London, Winchester, Chichester, Colchester... they were built over again and again. Calleva was not. Sometime after the end of Roman rule, in the fifth or sixth century, the town was abandoned and never reoccupied. Nobody knows exactly why. Because no later city rose on top of it, the Roman town plan survives almost untouched beneath the fields. For archaeologists, that makes this place extraordinarily valuable.

People have been digging here for a long time. In the nineteenth century, the Reverend James Joyce excavated parts of the site with the support of the Duke of Wellington, whose estate lay nearby. One of his finds became famous. It was a bronze Roman eagle, found in the 1860s in the area of the basilica. Its wings are missing. That small, damaged bird inspired the novelist Rosemary Sutcliff to write The Eagle of the Ninth, a children's adventure loved by generations of readers.

In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, larger campaigns uncovered much of the town plan. Then, from the 1990s onwards, the University of Reading ran a long excavation on one block of the town, known as Insula Nine. The team worked slowly and carefully, season after season. They found food remains, pottery, tools and traces of daily life across several centuries. Among the finds was a stone carved with Ogham, an early Irish script made of lines and notches. It is a rare and puzzling object for this part of Britain.

Many of the objects from Calleva are now displayed at Reading Museum, including the eagle. Here on site, you will not find a visitor centre full of glass cases. Instead, the experience is open air. You walk the wall, read the information panels, and imagine the streets below. After excavation, the trenches were filled in again to protect the remains. So the grass hides more than it shows. That is part of the quiet strength of this place.

Beyond the walls, to the north-east, lies the amphitheatre. It is a grassy oval bowl, ringed by banks and walls. Here crowds once gathered for public spectacles. These may have included animal shows, displays of combat, and other entertainments. Today the arena floor is calm and open. Stand in the centre and look up at the banks. You can feel how the shape gathers sound and attention towards the middle.

Inside the walls, near the east side, stands the small medieval Church of Saint Mary the Virgin. It is a reminder that this land did not become completely empty. The church sits within the Roman circuit, and its builders likely reused Roman materials from the ruins around them. Its quiet churchyard and old stonework make a gentle contrast with the wide, open fields of the abandoned town. Two worlds touch here: the Roman city and the English village parish.

As you walk on, look again at the line of the wall against the sky. Notice where trees grow from its top and where ivy covers the flint. Watch for changes in the grass. In dry summers, buried walls and streets can sometimes show as pale lines in the fields. Listen for the wind, the birds and your own footsteps on the path. Beneath them lies a complete town, resting in the dark. You are walking along its edge right now.
