The guide

The story

The air is thin here, and cold, even in the middle of summer. You stand on a wide plateau of green grass. Bare brown mountains rise on every side. The wind carries the smell of earth and horses. This is Shandur Top. It is one of the highest places in the world where people gather to play a game.

Shandur sits at about three thousand seven hundred meters above the sea. That height shapes everything. The sky feels close. Thin clouds drift below the peaks around you. When you walk even a short way, you feel it in your chest. The body needs time to learn this thin mountain air. Move slowly here, and rest often.

People sometimes call this region the roof of the world. Standing here, you understand the name. There are no trees on the plateau. There is only grass, water, rock, and sky. The mountains around you are bare and worn smooth by wind and ice. Nothing softens the view. The land feels old and patient and very wide.

The plateau lies on a high mountain pass. This pass joins two regions of the north. On one side is Ghizer, which belongs to Gilgit-Baltistan. On the other side is Gilgit. For a very long time, traders and herders have crossed this ground with their animals. The summer grass feeds their horses and their sheep.

At the center of the plateau is a long, flat field. This is the polo ground. People call it the highest polo ground in the world. The surface is plain grass and packed earth. There are no painted lines. There are no tall stands of seats. For most of the year, the field lies empty and quiet.

But each July, the field comes alive. The Shandur Polo Festival begins. Two teams ride onto the grass. One team comes from Gilgit-Baltistan. The other comes from Gilgit. They play a wild form of the game called freestyle polo. No umpire stops the action. The play runs hard from one end to the other.

The polo here is older than any written rulebook. It grew from the riding life of these mountains. Drummers and pipers sit beside the field. They beat a fast rhythm as the riders charge. Think how hard this game must be. The men gallop at full speed. The thin air gives them little to breathe. The horses strain on the heights. When a player scores, the music climbs higher, and the crowd shouts back from the slopes.

People travel a long way for these few days. They raise tents across the open plateau. Families cook their meals over open fires. Small traders sell hot tea, fresh bread, and warm woolen shawls. At night, the cold comes back hard. The fires glow in lines across the dark and crowded field.

Near the polo ground lies a quiet body of water. This is Shandur Lake. Its flat surface holds the picture of the sky and the peaks above. In the early morning, before the games begin, the water lies calm and clear. A thin mist sometimes rests on it. A few birds wait along the shore. The silence here runs very deep, and the cold air feels clean in your lungs.

Come when there is no festival, and you find another place entirely. The plateau is nearly empty. A herder moves slowly with his animals. The wind crosses the grass and meets no answer. You can walk the whole length of the silent field. You will hear only the sound of your own steps.

The way to Shandur is long and slow. The road climbs up from the green valleys far below. From Gilgit, the distance is about two hundred kilometers. Parts of the track are rough, narrow, and steep. The drive tests both the vehicle and the traveler. Many people break the journey across two days. The slow climb also gives the body time to meet the height.

Stand at the edge of the field now. Picture the riders at full charge, the drums loud, the slopes packed with people. Then picture this same ground in deep winter. It lies buried under snow, with no one for many miles. Both of these are Shandur. It holds the noise of summer and the long silence that follows.

The mountains do not follow the seasons of people. They watched the first horsemen cross this pass. They will watch the last one too. You are here for only a short while, high on the roof of these lands, where the grass reaches up and seems to touch the sky.

Before you go

Practical tips

Carry warm layers; even summer nights turn very cold
Drink water often and climb slowly to adjust
Book transport and lodging early for festival days
Bring enough cash; there are no machines here
