The story

On a network of narrow canals, wooden boats heaped with fruit, vegetables, and steaming food jostle for space, their owners paddling and calling out, cooking and selling from the water itself. This is Damnoen Saduak, the most famous floating market near Bangkok, a glimpse of an older way of trade carried on the water.

The market lies well outside the city, a drive of around an hour and a half to the south-west, in a region laced with canals dug long ago for transport and farming. Reaching it means an early start, but the journey is part of the experience.

Floating markets are a survival of the days when the canals were the roads and trade moved by boat. Vendors brought their produce to the water to sell, and although roads have long since taken over, this market keeps the old form alive, partly for visitors and partly out of habit.

The canals fill with boats: some piled with tropical fruit and vegetables, others fitted out as floating kitchens grilling, frying, and boiling food to order, and still others carrying visitors paddled through the crowd. The colour and bustle are the whole point.

Eating is one of the great pleasures here. You can buy noodle soup, grilled meats, fresh fruit, and sweets straight from the boats, handed up to you as you sit at the water's edge or in a hired boat among the sellers.

Most visitors hire a boat, either a paddled one or a noisy long-tailed boat, to move through the market and the surrounding canals. Drifting among the vendors, with the boats pressing close, is the classic way to see it.

It is worth being clear: the market is now heavily geared to tourists, crowded, and commercial, and prices are high and aimed at visitors. The authentic trading market of the past has become as much a show as a market, which disappoints some.

The way to enjoy it is to go in with open eyes, early, before the biggest crowds, and to treat it as a colourful, lively spectacle rather than a place for bargains. Seen that way, it is genuinely memorable.

Coming early, soon after the market opens, makes a real difference: the light is soft, the canals are less crowded, and the vendors are fresher and more relaxed before the tour buses arrive in force.

The canals and countryside around the market, with their stilt houses, temples, and orchards, are worth seeing too, and many boat trips take in some of the quieter waterways beyond the busiest market stretch.

A few practical notes. Go early and ideally with your own transport or a small tour rather than a big bus; agree boat and food prices before you commit, and bring cash. Expect crowds and a commercial atmosphere, and enjoy it for what it is.

Sit in a boat among the floating kitchens, with the smell of grilling food and the press of vendors around you, and for all the tourism, you catch an echo of the old water-borne trade that shaped this whole region. When you drive back toward the city, the canals give way to roads, and the past slips behind you.
