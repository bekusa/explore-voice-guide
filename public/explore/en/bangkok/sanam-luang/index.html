The story

In front of the grand palace spreads a vast open field of grass and bare earth, ringed by old trees and grand buildings, empty and open in a city packed with traffic and temples. This is Sanam Luang, the royal field at the ceremonial heart of Bangkok, a great open space with a special place in the life of the nation.

The name means, roughly, the royal field or royal ground, and it has long been reserved for important state and royal occasions rather than everyday use. Its openness, rare in the crowded old city, is itself part of its dignity.

For centuries the field has been the setting for the grandest royal ceremonies, including royal cremations, when elaborate structures rise on the grass and vast crowds gather to mourn or celebrate. At such times it becomes the focus of the whole country.

In ordinary times it is a great public lawn, used for gatherings, and historically famous as a place to fly kites in the windy season, a simple pleasure carried on in the shadow of the palace.

The field is bordered by some of the most important buildings in the country, with the walls and spires of the grand palace on one side and old institutions and temples around it, so it sits at the symbolic centre of the old royal city.

Its bare, ceremonial openness can surprise visitors expecting a manicured park; it is a working ceremonial ground, sometimes green, sometimes dusty, depending on the season and recent events, rather than a garden.

Walking its edge gives some of the best wide views of the grand palace and the surrounding monuments, the open ground letting you see the spires and walls from a distance, unusual in the tight old city.

The field is bound up with the monarchy and with great national moments, so it carries a weight of meaning for Thai people that is not always obvious to a passing visitor, and it is treated with respect.

Around its edges lie the grand palace, important temples, the national museum, and the old university, so the field is the natural centre of a day exploring the historic royal quarter on foot.

Because it is open ground, it offers little shade, so crossing or skirting it is best done in the cooler hours, and during major ceremonies parts of it may be closed to the public.

A few practical notes. The field is free and open ground; dress respectfully near the palace, check whether ceremonies are underway, and use the cooler morning or late afternoon to cross it in comfort. It anchors the whole royal quarter.

Stand at the edge of the great open field, with the spires of the grand palace rising beyond the grass, and the ceremonial heart of the country lies before you. Sanam Luang is the stage for the nation's grandest moments. When you turn back toward the palace gates, the open space gives the monuments room to breathe.
