The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Above you, there is a steady hum, a low concrete thunder that never quite stops. Tyres pass over expansion joints with a soft, rhythmic knock. Beneath that sound, the street-level traffic of Bang Na honks, idles, and inches forward. Look up and you see it — a long grey ribbon of concrete, lifted high on a single line of pillars, running east until it fades into the haze.

This is the Bang Na Expressway. Its official name is the Burapha Withi Expressway, and Burapha means the east. That name tells you its purpose. The road leaves the southeastern edge of Bangkok and runs toward the eastern seaboard. That coast is Thailand's industrial heartland, home to deep-water ports, factories, and beach towns. For decades, the journey east was slow and crowded. This elevated road was built to lift that traffic into the air.

Here is the first surprise. The expressway is not built beside the old highway. It is built directly on top of it. Below the deck runs Bang Na–Trat Road, the national highway that carried the eastern traffic for generations. Instead of buying new land and clearing homes and shops, the engineers placed their pillars along the existing road. The result is a city of two floors. The fast traffic travels above. The local life of markets, petrol stations, and bus stops continues below, in the shade.

The scale is hard to grasp from the ground. The elevated section stretches for more than fifty kilometres. It passes through Bang Na in Bangkok, then Bang Phli in Samut Prakan province, and on toward Bang Pakong in Chachoengsao province. It opened around the turn of the millennium. For a time, it was counted among the longest bridges in the world. Many people who drive it every day have no idea. To them, it is simply the road to work.

Now look closely at the shape of the deck. It is one wide concrete box, carrying several lanes in both directions. It rests on a single row of central columns, not two separate rows. This design keeps the footprint on the old highway as narrow as possible. Watch how the deck widens out from each pillar, like the arms of a letter T. That shape lets the busy road below keep most of its space.

How do you build something this long without closing a major highway for years? The answer is repetition. The deck is made from concrete segments cast in advance, away from the road. These pieces were carried into place and joined together, span after span, like beads on a string. If you look under the deck, you can sometimes see the faint lines where segments meet. Each line marks one piece of a very long puzzle.

The expressway is run by the Expressway Authority of Thailand, the state body that operates many of Bangkok's toll roads. It is a toll road, so drivers pay to use it. Along the route you will see toll plazas and lanes for electronic payment. Pedestrians and bicycles are not permitted on the elevated deck. For most visitors, the experience comes in two forms. You can watch it from below, or you can ride it as a passenger.

From below, the Bang Na area gives you the best sense of the structure. This district sits where Sukhumvit Road meets the start of the eastern route. The Skytrain runs through here too, on its own elevated line. In some places, you can see layers of concrete stacked across the sky — the train, the expressway, the flyovers. It is a very Bangkok view. The city has grown upward because the ground is crowded, flat, and often wet.

That wet ground is the second surprise. Bangkok sits on soft clay in the delta of the Chao Phraya River. This land was once rice fields, canals, and marshes. Building heavy structures here is difficult. Foundations must reach deep below the soft surface to find firm support. So the pillars you see are only part of the story. Below the street, the foundations go much further down than the columns rise above it.

Ride east as a passenger, and the view changes quickly. Shopping malls and housing estates give way to warehouses and logistics centres. Off to one side lies the area around Suvarnabhumi, Bangkok's main international airport. On a clear day, you may see aircraft climbing out over the flat land. Further east, there are fish ponds, shrimp farms, and glimpses of water near the Bang Pakong River. From this height, the landscape opens up in a way the old road never allowed.

The expressway also changed the communities beneath it. The shade of the deck became a place for daily life. Motorcycle taxi drivers wait in the shadows. Food stalls set up beside the columns. Small shops face the highway, and buses stop under the concrete roof. In the rainy season, the deck gives some shelter from sudden storms. It is not a beautiful space in the usual sense. But it is a working space, shaped by millions of ordinary days.

It is worth thinking about what this road means for Thailand. The eastern seaboard grew into one of the country's most important economic regions. Cars, electronics, and chemicals move from factories to ports. Holiday traffic flows toward Pattaya and the beaches beyond. Much of that movement passes along this single concrete line. When you watch the trucks overhead, you are watching a small part of the national economy roll past.

Look up one more time. Notice how the late light catches the underside of the deck, turning the grey concrete warm for a moment. Notice the stains left by years of monsoon rain, the dust on the columns, the steady pulse of traffic above. This is not a temple or a palace. It is the kind of structure most people pass without a thought. Yet it carries the daily movement of a whole region.

As you walk on, listen again to the hum overhead. It will follow you for a long time along this road, quieter as you move away. Somewhere above you, someone is driving home, or heading to the coast, or rushing to catch a flight. The concrete holds them all, high above the old highway, as the city keeps moving east.
