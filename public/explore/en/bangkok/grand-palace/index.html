The guide

The story

Gold spires catch the sun above white walls, mythical giants guard the gates, and the air is thick with incense and the press of crowds. Beyond the walls lies a dazzling complex of throne halls, pavilions, and the most sacred temple in the country. This is the Grand Palace, the ceremonial heart of Bangkok and of Thailand itself.

The palace was begun in 1782, when a new king founded the city of Bangkok as his capital and raised a royal seat to match. For a century and a half it was the home of the kings of Thailand, and though they no longer live here, it remains the spiritual and ceremonial centre of the monarchy.

The complex is not one building but a walled city of them, courts opening onto courts, mixing Thai temple architecture with later European-influenced halls. The result can feel overwhelming, a glittering maze, so it helps to fix on a few key sights rather than try to absorb everything.

The jewel within is the temple of the Emerald Buddha, the holiest Buddhist site in the land. Its courtyards blaze with gold, coloured glass, and mosaic, and mythical guardian figures stand watch, making it one of the most ornate religious spaces anywhere.

The image itself is small and easy to miss, high on its golden altar: a seated Buddha carved from a single piece of green jade. Despite its modest size it is deeply revered, and the king himself changes its golden robes with the seasons in an important ritual.

Around the temple courtyard runs a long painted gallery telling a great epic, the Thai version of an ancient Indian tale, in vivid scenes of gods, demons, and battles. Walking its length is a slow tour through the country's most beloved story.

Beyond the temple stand the royal halls, where kings were crowned and where ceremonies and state occasions still take place. One grand hall blends a European-style building with a traditional Thai tiered roof, a striking sign of an age when the kingdom looked outward.

Towering demon guardians, painted and gilded, stand at gateways throughout the complex, drawn from the same epic as the murals. Their fierce faces and bright armour make them favourites for photographs and a good introduction to Thai mythology.

The Grand Palace enforces a strict dress code, the strictest of any site in the city: shoulders and knees must be covered, for both men and women, and bare feet or flip-flops may be questioned. Wraps can be borrowed at the gate, but it is far easier to arrive dressed appropriately.

It is the busiest sight in Bangkok, and the combination of crowds, heat, and glare off the gold can be tiring. Come as early as possible, carry water, and pace yourself; the morning is cooler and a little less crowded than the middle of the day.

A few practical notes. Entry is ticketed, and the ticket also covers some sites beyond the palace. Beware anyone outside claiming the palace is closed for the day and offering a tour elsewhere; it is a long-running scam, so go to the official entrance and check for yourself.

Stand in the temple courtyard for a moment, with the gold spires above and the smell of incense around you, and watch Thai visitors kneel and pray before the small green Buddha. For all the crowds, this remains a living sacred place at the heart of a nation. When you step back out through the gates, the ordinary city feels almost plain.
