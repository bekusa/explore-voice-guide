The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and tilt your head back. Two tall red pillars rise above the traffic, joined at the top by a carved crossbeam. The paint is a deep, warm red, like lacquer on a temple door. Around you, motorbikes hum, tuk-tuks rattle past, and the air carries incense smoke from the temple just behind. There is no swing hanging here today. There is only the frame, standing in the middle of the square like a gateway to nowhere.

This is the Giant Swing, known in Thai as Sao Chingcha. It is one of the oldest public structures in Bangkok. And it is also one of the strangest. Most visitors walk past it on the way to the temple and wonder what it was for. The answer takes us back to the founding years of the city, and to a ceremony that has much older roots in India.

The first swing was raised here in 1784, during the reign of King Rama I, the founder of the current Chakri dynasty. Bangkok was a very young capital then. The king was building temples, palaces, and city walls, and he was also restoring court rituals from the older Siamese kingdom of Ayutthaya. The swing was part of that effort. It gave the new city a stage for a ceremony that kings had sponsored for centuries.

That ceremony was Brahmin, not Buddhist. This surprises many visitors, because Thailand is so strongly Buddhist. But Brahmin priests have served at the Thai royal court for a very long time. They perform rituals connected to Hindu gods, especially for state occasions. Nearby, a small Brahmin shrine called the Devasathan still stands, and its priests still keep many of these traditions alive.

The swinging festival was linked to the god Shiva. According to Hindu belief as practised here, Shiva came down to visit the earth once a year, during the second month of the old lunar calendar. The festival welcomed him. One popular explanation says the swing honoured a story about Shiva testing whether the world was stable. The details vary depending on who tells it, so think of that part as tradition rather than fixed doctrine.

Now picture the ceremony itself. Teams of young men climbed onto a wooden seat hanging from the crossbeam. They swung higher and higher, in long, wide arcs over the crowd. Out to one side, a tall pole held a small bag of coins. The goal was to swing close enough to grab that bag — with their teeth. Crowds gathered to watch, and the king or his representative presided.

As you can imagine, this was dangerous. The swing reached a great height, and the men had no safety equipment. Over the years, there were falls, injuries, and deaths. In 1935, the swinging ritual was stopped. The Brahmin ceremony for Shiva continued in other forms, but nobody has swung from these pillars for public spectacle since then. The frame you see now is a monument to a ritual that ended almost a century ago.

Here is a detail that most people miss. The pillars in front of you are not the originals. Teak is strong, but sun, rain, and termites wear it down. The swing was repaired several times over its history. Then, in the mid-2000s, the city carried out a full replacement. Huge teak trunks were brought from Phrae province in northern Thailand, and the new pillars were raised in 2007 with royal ceremony.

The old pillars were not thrown away. They were preserved and taken to the National Museum in Bangkok. So the frame here carries the shape and position of the original, but the wood is new. It is a little like a temple roof that is rebuilt again and again. The form stays the same, the material is renewed, and the meaning passes from one generation to the next.

Look at the base of the pillars. They stand on a raised stone platform with steps, and a low railing keeps the crowd at a respectful distance. Then look up at the top. The crossbeam has carved ends and decorative details, all in the same red. The contrast between the heavy pillars and the light sky is the reason so many photographers come here. From a distance, the frame looks almost delicate.

Now turn around and look at the large temple behind the swing. This is Wat Suthat, one of the most important royal temples in Bangkok. Its main hall holds a large bronze Buddha image, and its wide courtyards are lined with rows of seated Buddha figures. The swing and the temple form one scene. A Brahmin monument stands in front of a Buddhist temple, and nobody here sees a conflict between them.

The streets around the square have their own character. Along Bamrung Mueang Road, you will find shops that sell supplies for monks and temples. Saffron robes hang in the windows. Brass alms bowls, large Buddha images, candles, and ceremonial umbrellas fill the shop fronts. Many of these businesses have served temples for generations. Walking past them is one of the best ways to understand how religion and daily commerce live side by side in old Bangkok.

The square is also a civic space. The large building on one side houses offices of the Bangkok city government. Officials, students, monks, and tourists all cross this square in the same few minutes. In the early morning and late afternoon, the light is softer, and the red of the pillars glows against the sky. At night, the swing is lit, and the square becomes quieter and cooler.

Before you move on, look up one more time. Imagine the creak of the wooden seat, the shouts of the crowd, and a young man reaching for a bag of coins high above the ground. Then come back to the present — the traffic, the incense, the red paint warm in the sun. The swing no longer moves. But it still marks the centre of this old part of the city, and it still asks every passer-by to stop and look up.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in early morning for softer light and lighter traffic.
Combine your visit with Wat Suthat directly behind the swing.
Dress modestly if you plan to enter the temple afterwards.
Walk Bamrung Mueang Road to see the monk supply shops.
Return after dark to photograph the illuminated frame.
