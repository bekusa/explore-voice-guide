The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Outside the walls, there is wind across open ground and the low traffic of Vagharshapat. Then you step inside, and the sound drops away. The air is cool. Light falls from high windows onto plain stone, and the dome above seems to float. This is one of the oldest standing churches in Armenia, and it has stood here for roughly fourteen centuries.

Before we look at the building, we need the story of the woman it honours. According to Armenian tradition, Hripsime was a young Christian woman from the Roman world. She belonged to a community of devout women led by an elder named Gayane. When persecution of Christians grew under the Roman emperors, the women fled east. They crossed into Armenia and found shelter near the royal city that stood here, the place we now call Vagharshapat.

The tradition continues with a darker turn. The Armenian king, Tiridates the Third, was still a pagan. Legend has it that he heard of Hripsime's beauty and wished to marry her. She refused him. In the story, she was martyred for that refusal, close to the spot where you stand now. Gayane and the other women were killed as well. Locals still point out that Gayane has her own church a short walk away.

What happened next is the founding story of Christian Armenia. According to the tradition, the king fell into madness after the killings. One popular tale says he took on the form of a wild boar. Only one man could heal him — Gregory, a Christian whom the king had thrown into a deep pit years before. Gregory was freed and the king recovered. Armenia then became one of the first kingdoms to adopt Christianity as its state religion, traditionally in the early fourth century.

So this ground is more than a church site. In the story Armenians tell about themselves, the death of these women set the conversion of a nation in motion. Gregory became known as Gregory the Illuminator. The first shrine here was a small chapel over the martyr's remains. The building around you is not that first shrine. It is a later and grander church, built in the early seventh century by the Catholicos Komitas. The Catholicos is the head of the Armenian Apostolic Church.

Now look at the shape of the space. From the centre, four broad semicircular apses open out in four directions, like the arms of a cross. Between them, in the corners, sit four smaller round niches. Each niche leads to a small corner room. Above all of this rises a single central dome. Architectural historians call this plan a tetraconch, which simply means a building with four curved lobes. This particular arrangement is so closely tied to this church that scholars often call it the Hripsime type.

Here is something many visitors miss. Go back outside later and walk around the walls. The outside looks almost like a solid block of stone. But the walls are cut by deep, narrow, triangular notches running from top to bottom. These notches are not decoration alone. They mark exactly where the curves of the interior meet. In other words, the plan of the inside is written on the outside. Once you see it, the whole building starts to make sense.

The builders worked in volcanic stone, cut into large, carefully fitted blocks. Armenia has a long tradition of building in this kind of stone, which is easy to carve when fresh and hardens over time. Run your eye along the joints. The stonework is tight and even. Surfaces inside are mostly bare. There are no great painted walls here. The beauty comes from proportion, from the way the curves gather the light and lead your eyes upward to the dome.

That dome deserves a long look. It sits on a tall drum, pierced by windows. During the day, light enters from above and moves slowly across the stone as the hours pass. In the morning the light is cool and soft. Later in the day it can turn warmer. Stand under the centre and turn slowly in a full circle. You will see how each apse and each niche plays its part in holding up the weight above.

The second surprise lies below your feet. The church keeps the tomb of Saint Hripsime in a small chamber beneath the building. Visitors are usually able to go down and see it. The space is low, quiet and often lit by candles. For many Armenian pilgrims, this is the heart of the visit. You may see people lighting thin candles, touching the stone, or simply standing in silence. If you go down, move slowly and keep your voice low.

The church has not stayed frozen since the seventh century. Over many generations it was repaired, strengthened and restored. A porch with a bell tower was added at the western end in a much later period, so the entrance you used is younger than the walls behind it. Today the church is part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, together with the cathedral of Etchmiadzin, nearby churches and the ruins of Zvartnots. It is still an active place of worship.

Vagharshapat itself is the spiritual centre of the Armenian Apostolic Church. The Mother See, with its cathedral, sits a short ride away. Saint Gayane Church and Shoghakat Church are also close by, and both connect to the same story of the martyred women. Seeing them together gives you a fuller picture. On a clear day, look south from the church grounds. You may see the great snowy shape of Mount Ararat rising above the plain.

Before you leave, stand outside once more and look at the whole building. It is compact, heavy and calm. It does not try to impress you with ornament. Notice the triangular notches you now understand. Notice the dome sitting on its drum, and the later bell tower at the front. And as you walk away, listen again to the wind across the open ground — the same wind that has moved around these stones for fourteen hundred years.
