The guide

The story

Bare feet on cool grey stone. That is how most visitors meet this temple — shoes left behind at the entrance, soles pressing on granite worn smooth by countless pilgrims. The air carries marigold, wet earth, and the faint smoke of ghee lamps. Bells ring in short, uneven bursts. Somewhere a priest chants in Sanskrit, the words rising and falling like breath.

First, a word on where you are standing. The Vishnupad Temple is not in Bodh Gaya itself. It stands in the old city of Gaya, a short drive north of the Buddhist pilgrimage town. The two places sit side by side on the map. Yet they carry very different religious lives. Bodh Gaya draws Buddhists to the tree of the Buddha's enlightenment. Gaya draws Hindus for one of the most solemn duties in their faith — honouring the dead.

The temple is dedicated to Vishnu, one of the central gods of Hinduism, often called the preserver of the universe. Its name explains its heart. Vishnupad means "the foot of Vishnu." Inside the sanctum, set into solid rock, is an impression that devotees believe is the god's own footprint. This is not a statue or a painting. It is a mark on stone, and that simple fact gives the place its quiet power.

The story behind the footprint is a legend, and locals tell it with obvious affection. According to the popular tale, a mighty being named Gayasura grew so holy through his devotion that anyone who touched him could reach heaven without effort. The gods grew uneasy with this shortcut. Vishnu placed his foot on Gayasura's chest and pressed him down into the earth. Before he sank, Gayasura asked for one blessing — that this ground would free the souls of anyone honoured here.

That request, legend says, is why the city carries his name. And it explains the main reason people come. Families travel from across India, and from Indian communities abroad, to perform pind daan. This is a ritual offering for ancestors who have died. Rice balls, sesame, and water are offered with prayers. The belief is that these offerings help the souls of the departed find peace. For many Hindus, a journey to Gaya is a duty owed to their parents and grandparents.

Now look up at the building itself. The temple you see today is not ancient in its current form, though the site is much older. Historical accounts credit its rebuilding to Ahilyabai Holkar, a queen of the Maratha state of Indore, in 1787. She was a remarkable ruler. She funded the repair of many temples across northern India, at a time when many had fallen into ruin. Here she left one of her most lasting works.

The stone is grey granite, cut into heavy blocks and fitted with care. The tower above the sanctum rises in a tapering spire, typical of temples in northern India. This style is called a shikhara, which simply means "mountain peak." The idea is that the temple becomes a small sacred mountain, a home for the god. Notice how the stone darkens in places, stained by rain, smoke, and the touch of many hands.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The footprint inside is held within a basin ringed with silver. Pilgrims press flowers, sandalwood paste, and coloured powder onto it during worship. By the end of a busy morning, the mark can almost disappear beneath petals. Then priests clear it, and the cycle begins again. It is less a fixed relic than a living point of contact, renewed every single day.

A practical note, and an important one. Access to the inner sanctum has traditionally been restricted, and rules for non-Hindu visitors are not always the same from day to day. Ask politely at the entrance before you go further. Even if you stay in the outer areas, you can still watch the rituals, hear the chanting, and feel the rhythm of the place. Photography inside is often not allowed, so check first and keep your camera down.

The people guiding the rituals are worth noticing too. Gaya has a long tradition of hereditary priests, known as Gayawal pandas. Families often return to the same priestly lineage across generations. Some priests keep old record books listing the pilgrims who came before — fathers, grandfathers, and further back. A visitor may find a great-grandparent's name written in an ancestor's hand. That thread of memory is one of the most moving things about this place.

Walk to the edge of the temple grounds and you reach the Phalgu River. Do not expect a broad flowing stream. For much of the year, the riverbed looks like a wide stretch of pale sand. Water often runs beneath the surface. One popular legend says the river was cursed by Sita, the heroine of the epic Ramayana, which is why it hides its water underground. Pilgrims still bathe and make offerings along its ghats, the stone steps that lead down to the river.

The busiest time here is Pitru Paksha, a fortnight in the Hindu calendar set aside for honouring ancestors. It usually falls in September or October. During those days, the lanes fill with pilgrims, and the ghats grow crowded from early morning. If you come at another time, the temple feels calmer. You can stand longer in one place and watch the small gestures — a hand touching a threshold, a whispered name.

There is a striking contrast in this corner of Bihar. A short distance south, monks meditate under the Bodhi tree, chasing release from the cycle of rebirth. Here, families perform rites so their ancestors can find peace. Two great traditions, side by side, both deeply concerned with what happens after life. Many travellers visit both in a single day, and the change in mood between them stays with you.

Before you leave, pause on the stone once more. Feel how cool it is under your feet, even in the heat. Listen for the bells, the chanting, the shuffle of pilgrims behind you. Somewhere nearby, someone is saying the names of people they have lost. As you walk back towards your shoes and the noise of the street, you carry a little of that quiet with you.
