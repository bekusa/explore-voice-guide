The guide

The story

Listen for the gravel first. Every step on the long paths here makes a soft, dry crunch. Somewhere ahead, water falls into a stone basin. The air smells of clipped hedges and damp earth. And in front of you, the garden opens into a wide, flat circle of lawn, flowerbeds and lime trees. You are standing in one of the great pleasure gardens of eighteenth-century Germany, a short journey west of Heidelberg, out on the flat plain toward the Rhine.

Schwetzingen Palace was the summer home of the Electors Palatine. These were the princes who ruled this region, and who held a vote in choosing the Holy Roman Emperor. Their main court sat in nearby Mannheim. But when the weather turned warm, the court came here. They brought musicians, actors, gardeners and guests. For a few months each year, this small town became the centre of a glittering, cultured world.

The man who shaped most of what you see was Elector Carl Theodor. He ruled for much of the eighteenth century, and he loved the arts. He collected paintings, supported scientists, and funded one of the finest orchestras in Europe. Schwetzingen was his playground and his laboratory. Here he could try new ideas in architecture and garden design, far from the formal duties of the city.

Now, here is the first surprise. The palace itself is fairly modest. Look at the main building, and you will see something closer to a large country house than a grand royal residence. Its roots go back to an older moated castle and hunting lodge. That older building suffered badly in the wars of the late seventeenth century, and it was rebuilt and extended afterward. The real ambition of this place was never the house. It was the garden.

And the garden begins with a shape you rarely see. Most formal French gardens are long rectangles, stretching away from the palace in straight lines. Here, the central parterre is a circle. Curved buildings frame it on two sides, and avenues of trees close the ring. The effect is calm and enclosed. You feel held inside the design, rather than pushed out toward a distant horizon. Take a moment to turn slowly and see the full round.

In the centre, fountains and statues mark the axes. Look for the Arion fountain, which shows the singer from Greek myth who was saved from drowning by a dolphin. Further along, a group of stags stands over cascading water. These sculptures belong to the classical world that educated Europeans of the time knew by heart. Every god, nymph and hero was a small story, waiting for a guest to recognise it on an evening stroll.

But walk beyond the circle, and the mood changes. The paths begin to curve. The trees grow less trimmed and more natural. This is the English landscape garden, a style that came into fashion later in the eighteenth century. Instead of geometry, it offered meadows, lakes and carefully placed views. The great garden designer Friedrich Ludwig von Sckell worked here. So Schwetzingen holds two opposing ideas of nature side by side — one ordered, one apparently wild.

Scattered through this landscape are small buildings, almost like stage sets. There is a Temple of Apollo, raised on an artificial rock above a cascade. There is a bathhouse, an intimate retreat built for the Elector himself. And there is a Temple of Mercury, designed from the start to look like a ruin. Its broken look is entirely deliberate. In this period, ruins suggested time, memory and melancholy, and wealthy owners happily built fake ones to create that feeling.

The second surprise is the most famous building in the garden. Through the trees, you will see domes and two slender minarets. This is the garden mosque, built in the late eighteenth century by the court architect Nicolas de Pigage. It was created as a garden building, an expression of European fascination with the Islamic world. It was not built for prayer. Yet it remains one of the earliest mosque-style buildings in Germany, and its cloisters and courtyard are worth a slow walk.

Look out, too, for a little scene called the End of the World. At the end of a green tunnel of trellis, a painted landscape seems to open into endless distance. It is a trick of perspective, painted to fool the eye. Close by is the bird bath, where metal birds spray water toward a central figure. Locals tell a playful story about the scene, linked to an owl. Children usually love it, and so do adults who pretend they do not.

Music is a deep part of this place. Carl Theodor commissioned a small rococo theatre beside the palace, also designed by Pigage. Rococo means the light, curving, decorative style of the mid eighteenth century. Leading composers and performers came to the summer court. A young Mozart visited with his family as a child prodigy, during their long tour of Europe. The writer Voltaire also stayed here as a guest of the Elector in the 1750s. Today, a spring music festival still fills the theatre.

The golden age did not last forever. Late in the eighteenth century, Carl Theodor inherited Bavaria, and he moved his court to Munich. The summer season here faded. But the garden survived, and so did its strange collection of temples, ruins and fountains. The town around it also kept its own fame. Schwetzingen is known across Germany for white asparagus. In spring, local restaurants serve it everywhere, with butter, ham and boiled potatoes.

So before you leave, find a quiet bench near the edge of the circle. Listen again to the gravel under passing feet, and the water in the basins. Watch the light move across the hedges and the pale stone of the statues. This garden was built for pleasure, conversation and slow walks. More than two centuries later, it still works exactly that way. As you walk out, notice how the straight lines gently give way to the curving paths behind you.
