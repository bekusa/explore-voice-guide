The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beyond the traffic, you may hear the small river Düssel moving over stones, and the wind in the beech trees on the valley slopes. This green, quiet valley lies just outside Solingen, near the town of Mettmann. It looks ordinary at first glance. Yet the name of this valley became the name of an entire human species. Every time someone in the world says the word Neanderthal, they are pointing, without knowing it, at this patch of earth.

The valley's name has a story of its own. In the seventeenth century, a young poet and hymn writer called Joachim Neander liked to walk here. His family name was originally Neumann, meaning new man. Like many educated people of his time, he translated it into Greek, and Neumann became Neander. Later, locals named the valley after him. So the word Neanderthal simply means Neander's valley. And by an odd coincidence, the hymn writer's Greek name also means new man.

In the nineteenth century, this valley looked very different. Steep limestone cliffs rose above the river, full of caves and narrow gorges. Then the quarrymen came. Limestone was valuable for building and industry, and workers cut the cliffs away piece by piece. Much of the dramatic landscape you might imagine is simply gone. The gentle slopes around you today are partly the result of that industrial work. Keep that in mind as you walk... the ground itself has been reshaped by human hands.

In 1856, workers were clearing a small cave called the Feldhofer Grotto. They threw the clay and debris down the slope, and among it they found bones. At first they thought the bones belonged to a cave bear. They kept a few of the larger pieces — a skullcap, thigh bones, parts of the arms. Most of the rest was lost. It was an accident, found by men who were looking for stone, not for history.

The bones reached a local schoolteacher and naturalist, Johann Carl Fuhlrott. He saw immediately that these were not animal bones. The skullcap was long and low, with a heavy ridge above the eyes. Fuhlrott believed they came from a very ancient human. He worked with the anatomist Hermann Schaaffhausen from Bonn to present the find. Many scholars at the time disagreed. Some argued it was simply a sick or unusual modern man.

The timing matters here. Charles Darwin published his book on evolution in 1859, only three years after the discovery. The idea that humans had ancestors or relatives who looked different from us was new and controversial. In 1864, the bones received a scientific name: Homo neanderthalensis, the human from the Neander valley. It was one of the first times anyone had officially named an extinct kind of human. This small valley stood at the start of a very large debate.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The original bones are not displayed here. They are kept at the regional museum in Bonn, where they have been for a long time. What you see in the museum are careful copies and reconstructions. But the story does not end there. In the late 1990s and around 2000, researchers searched the old quarry debris again. They found more bone fragments, and some of them fit perfectly onto the original skeleton — like missing pieces of a puzzle, returned after nearly a century and a half.

Now look at the museum building itself. It opened in the 1990s, and it does not look like a traditional museum. The structure is made of concrete and green-tinted glass, and its shape suggests a spiral. Inside, a long ramp carries you slowly upward through the exhibition. The idea is simple and clever. As you climb, you move through the story of human development, from the distant past toward the present day. Your own walking becomes part of the timeline.

As you follow the ramp, pay attention to the faces. The museum is known for its lifelike reconstructions of early humans, with skin, hair and eyes that look surprisingly real. One famous figure shows a Neanderthal man dressed in a modern suit. It is a quiet joke with a serious point. Put him on a city train, and you might not look twice. These were not the hunched brutes of old cartoons. They were people who made tools, used fire, and cared for their sick and injured.

Here is a second surprise. Neanderthals lived across Europe and western Asia for hundreds of thousands of years, and they disappeared around forty thousand years ago. But they did not vanish completely. Modern genetic research shows that they had children with early modern humans. Many people alive today, especially those with roots outside Africa, carry a small share of Neanderthal DNA. So when you look at those reconstructed faces, you may be looking at distant family.

After the museum, the valley invites you to walk. A path leads to the original find site, a short distance away. The cave itself no longer exists, because the quarry destroyed it. Instead, the site is marked with installations and markers that help you imagine where the grotto once stood. There is also an art trail with sculptures along the paths. It is a calm, reflective place, and it feels very different from the indoor exhibition.

Further along the valley, there is an enclosure with large animals that recall the Ice Age world. You may see European bison, sturdy wild-looking horses, and cattle that were bred to resemble the extinct wild ox. These animals give the landscape a sense of scale. Neanderthals hunted relatives of these creatures in colder, open landscapes. Standing at the fence, watching a bison move slowly through the grass, the distant past suddenly feels a little closer.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Feel the soft ground under your feet and look at the trees on the slopes. The river still runs where it ran when the quarrymen worked here, and long before that, when Neanderthals walked this region. The cliffs are gone, the cave is gone, and the original bones are elsewhere. But the name remains. As you walk back toward the road, remember that a few broken bones, found by chance in this valley, changed how we understand what it means to be human.
