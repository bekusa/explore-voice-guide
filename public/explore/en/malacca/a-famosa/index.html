The guide

The story

Put your hand on the stone of this gateway. It is rough, pitted, and a deep rusty red, almost like dried clay. Up close you can see small holes and dark specks inside each block. Around you, traffic hums along the road, trishaws ring their bells, and music drifts from their speakers. Above the arch, the green slope of a hill rises toward the ruins of an old church. You are standing at the last surviving piece of a fortress called A Famosa.

The name is Portuguese, and it means simply 'the famous one'. That tells you something about how its builders saw it. In 1511, a Portuguese fleet led by Afonso de Albuquerque captured Malacca from its sultan. At that time, Malacca was one of the great trading ports of Asia. Ships came here from China, India, Arabia, and the islands of Southeast Asia. Whoever held this harbour controlled a key point on the route of the spice trade.

Albuquerque knew that a conquest is easy to lose. So the Portuguese began building a fortress almost immediately. Historical accounts describe a strong ring of walls wrapped around the hill behind you, with a tall keep, or central tower, near the waterfront. Inside the walls stood homes, offices, and churches. The fort was the heart of Portuguese power in the region. For more than a century, it gave them a secure base between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. The labour and the stone did not all come from far away. Contemporary records say that stone from local buildings, including mosques and tombs of the former sultanate, was taken and reused in the walls. Much of the fort was also built from laterite. Laterite is a reddish rock formed in hot, wet climates, rich in iron. That iron gives this gate its colour, and it explains the small holes you can feel under your fingers.

Now look up at the top of the arch. You will see a carved coat of arms and a date. This is a surprise for many people, because the carving is not Portuguese at all. It belongs to the Dutch East India Company, the powerful trading company of the Netherlands. The date you can read there is 1670. So the gateway in front of you is not quite the original Portuguese structure. It carries the mark of the next empire that came to Malacca.

The Dutch arrived with force. In 1641, after a long siege, they took the city from the Portuguese. The siege did serious damage to the walls, and disease and hunger struck the people inside. Once the Dutch were in control, they repaired the fortress and used it for their own purposes. They added their emblem to the gates. Under Dutch rule, the fort continued to guard the harbour for well over another century.

This gate has its own name: Porta de Santiago, or the Gate of Saint James. It was one of several entrances through the walls. Imagine it as a narrow, guarded passage. Carts, soldiers, merchants, and officials would have passed through here. Beyond it lay the protected town on the hill. Today you can walk straight through the arch in a few seconds. But in its working years, this was a controlled point, where people were watched, questioned, and checked.

At the end of the eighteenth century, Europe was at war, and Malacca changed hands again. The British took control of the city during the conflicts with France. In the early nineteenth century, British officials decided to destroy the fortress. They worried that if Malacca were handed back to the Dutch, a strong fort could be used against British interests. Workers used gunpowder to bring the great walls down. Almost the whole structure disappeared.

So why does this one gate survive? The usual story credits Sir Stamford Raffles, the British official who later founded modern Singapore. According to this account, Raffles visited Malacca during the demolition and asked for the remaining gate to be spared. He valued it as a piece of the city's past. Whatever the exact details, the result stands in front of you. One gateway was left, while the rest of the fortress turned to rubble and was cleared away.

Take a moment to notice the cannons near the gate. They are a reminder that this was always a military place, not a decorative monument. Then look at how small the gate seems compared with the fortress it once belonged to. It is a little like finding a single door from a vanished castle. Try to picture the walls running away from it on both sides, around the hill, and down toward the sea.

The sea, by the way, is no longer where it used to be. Over the years, land has been reclaimed along the Malacca waterfront. When the Portuguese built here, the water came much closer to the hill. Ships would have anchored in clear view of the walls. Today, buildings and roads stand between the gate and the strait. This change makes the fort's original purpose a little harder to imagine, but it was built to face the water.

This gate is part of a larger historic centre. In 2008, Melaka and George Town in Penang were listed together as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The listing recognises centuries of exchange between Malay, Chinese, Indian, and European cultures. Within a short walk you can find the ruins of Saint Paul's Church on the hilltop, and the red Dutch buildings around the old square. Each layer of the city belongs to a different chapter of the same story.

If you climb the steps behind the gate, the path leads up the hill to the church ruins. It is a short walk, but the heat can be strong, so go slowly. From the top, you can look out across the rooftops toward the strait. That view helps explain everything. This hill was the high ground over a famous harbour, and empire after empire wanted to hold it.

Before you move on, stand back from the arch and look at it once more. Notice the red stone, darker in the shaded corners. Notice the Dutch carving, softened by weather. Listen to the traffic, the trishaw music, the voices of other visitors. Four centuries of soldiers and traders passed under this arch. Now it is your turn. As you walk away, look for other red stones around the old town, and wonder where they came from.
