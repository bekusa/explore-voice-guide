The guide

The story

Listen first. The traffic of the city fades behind a wall of dark red sandstone, and the air turns cooler. Underfoot, worn stone flags dip slightly in the middle, smoothed by six centuries of feet. There is a faint smell of old paper, wax and timber. You are standing in one of the oldest complete buildings in this part of England. It sits at the very edge of Manchester, a short walk from the River Irwell, the water that divides Manchester from Salford.

This is Chetham's Library. It is often described as the oldest free public reference library in the English-speaking world. That phrase matters. Free meant anyone could come in and read. Reference meant the books stayed here, and you came to them. In the middle of the seventeenth century, that was a radical idea. Books were expensive, private things, kept by churches, colleges and wealthy families. This place set out to open them to ordinary people.

The man behind it was Humphrey Chetham. He was a Manchester merchant who made his fortune in the textile trade and in lending money. He never married and had no children of his own. When he died in 1653, his will left money for two things. The first was a school for poor boys from the local area. The second was a library, open to scholars and to anyone who wished to study. Both still exist, side by side, on this same site.

But the building is far older than the library. It dates to the early fifteenth century, around 1421. It was built to house the priests of the collegiate church next door, the church that is now Manchester Cathedral. Those priests lived, ate and prayed here. When the religious upheavals of the Tudor period swept England, the college was dissolved. The building passed into private hands, and for a time it belonged to the powerful Stanley family, the Earls of Derby.

By the time Chetham's executors bought it, the old college was in poor repair. They turned it into the school and the library. That is why the layout feels like a small monastery rather than a public building. There is a cloister, a courtyard, low doorways and narrow stairs. Look at how thick the walls are. They were built for a community that wanted quiet, and that quiet is still here, just metres from one of the busiest stations in the city.

Now to the books. The library collection grew to many thousands of volumes. Early printed books, manuscripts, maps and pamphlets sit on the shelves today. In the early years, the books were chained to the shelves, so readers could study them but not carry them away. The chains were later removed. In their place came something unusual: wooden gates at the end of each bay of shelving. A reader could be let into a bay and the gate locked behind them.

Picture that for a moment. Instead of a book being brought to you, you were shut in with the books. The long gallery of dark oak shelves, called presses, runs in an L shape along the upper floor. Light falls through leaded windows onto the spines. Many of these volumes have been here for centuries, in the same building, in roughly the same order. Very few libraries anywhere can say that.

Here is a story that surprises many visitors. In the summer of 1845, two young men sat in the Reading Room together. One was Friedrich Engels, whose family owned a cotton business in Manchester. The other was Karl Marx, visiting from abroad. They read economics and history here, using the library's collection. A few years later they published The Communist Manifesto. Their desk sat in an alcove by the bay window, and that window still lets light into the room today.

Think about the contrast. Outside these walls, Manchester was becoming the first great industrial city, full of mills, smoke and crowded housing. Inside, two writers studied the economic ideas that shaped their response to it. A library founded by a cloth merchant gave space to two critics of the system that made merchants rich. Humphrey Chetham could not have imagined that outcome. But it fits his idea: books open to anyone, whatever they planned to do with them.

There is also a darker tale attached to this place, and it is folklore, not history. In the late sixteenth century, John Dee served as warden of the college. Dee was a real scholar, a mathematician and an adviser to Queen Elizabeth the First, who was also drawn to astrology and the occult. Legend has it that he summoned the devil here, and that a burn mark on a table is the print of a hoof. Nobody can prove it. Locals still enjoy telling it.

The Reading Room itself is worth slow attention. It has dark carved woodwork, a decorated ceiling and a large fireplace. Paintings and old portraits hang on the panelled walls. The furniture is heavy and plain, made for long hours of reading. The room feels lived in rather than displayed. That is because it is still a working library. Researchers still come here to study rare material, just as scholars did in the seventeenth century.

You may hear music drifting across the courtyard. That comes from the school that Humphrey Chetham founded. Over time it changed from a charity school for poor boys into a specialist school of music, now known for training young musicians. So the site still holds both halves of his will. One building cares for old books. The other trains young people. The sound of a violin or a piano practice is a small, living link to that seventeenth-century gift.

Visiting usually means joining a tour or booking in advance, because the building is small and fragile. The staircases are narrow and the floors are uneven, so take your time. Photography rules can vary by room, so check with staff before you lift a camera. And when you step back outside, look around. The cathedral stands close by. The modern city rises behind it. This sandstone building has watched the whole of Manchester grow up around it.

Before you leave, pause in the courtyard. Run your eyes along the red stone, darkened by centuries of rain and soot. Listen to how the noise of the street stays outside these walls. Somewhere above you, thousands of books sit quietly in their oak presses. They have been waiting for readers since 1653. As you walk away, toward the river or the station, you carry a little of that quiet with you.
