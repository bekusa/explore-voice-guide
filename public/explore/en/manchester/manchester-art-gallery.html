The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the steps on Mosley Street and look up. Six tall stone columns rise in front of you. Their pale sandstone is darkened in places by two centuries of city air. Behind you, trams hum and buses brake. Ahead, the portico waits, calm and symmetrical, like a temple dropped into a commercial street. This is Manchester Art Gallery, and the building itself is the first object on display.

That temple look is deliberate. The style is called Greek Revival, which means architecture that copies the columns and proportions of ancient Greek temples. It was fashionable in early nineteenth century Britain, when wealthy cities wanted to show they were cultured as well as rich. Notice the scroll-shaped tops of the columns. Those curls mark them as Ionic, one of the classical orders of ancient Greece.

Here is the first surprise. The architect was Charles Barry. A few years after this building, Barry won the competition to rebuild the Houses of Parliament in London. So before he shaped the most recognisable skyline in Britain, he was working here, on a Manchester street, testing his ideas in stone. This was one of his early major commissions, and it helped make his name.

The building was not first meant to be a public gallery. It was built for the Royal Manchester Institution, a society of local merchants, manufacturers and artists. They wanted a place for exhibitions, lectures and learning. Manchester was then growing fast on cotton and industry. Factory chimneys filled the sky. These men believed a city of machines also needed a place for art and ideas.

In 1882, the Institution handed the building and its collection to the city. From that moment, it belonged to the people of Manchester. That shift matters. A private club for the comfortable became a civic space for everyone. Many visitors walk past the portico without knowing this. The gallery is a gift, made at the height of the Victorian age, from one part of the city to the whole of it.

Now look along the street to the right. The neighbouring building, also designed by Barry, was once the Manchester Athenaeum, a club for reading, debate and self-improvement. Today it is part of the gallery too. In the early 2000s, a modern extension by the architects Michael Hopkins and Partners joined the old buildings together. Inside, you can move from Victorian stone into glass and light without stepping outside.

Once through the doors, you feel the change in sound. The traffic fades. Footsteps echo on stone. The grand staircase draws your eye upward, and the walls carry a rich, warm colour. Take a moment before you climb. Many people rush to the paintings. But the hall itself shows how the Victorians thought about art — something to approach slowly, step by step, almost like entering a church.

Upstairs, the collection is known above all for its Pre-Raphaelite paintings. The Pre-Raphaelites were a group of Victorian artists who rejected polished academic painting. They wanted bright colour, sharp detail and serious moral themes. Look for The Hireling Shepherd by William Holman Hunt. A young man flirts in a sunlit field while his sheep wander into trouble. The colours are almost electric, and every leaf seems painted one by one.

Then find Work by Ford Madox Brown. It is a crowded street scene of labourers digging a trench, while gentlemen, beggars and children surround them. Brown packed it with social comment about who works and who watches. Take your time with it. Every corner holds another small story. And here is a link most visitors miss: Brown also painted the large murals inside Manchester Town Hall, a short walk away.

One painting here has a modern story attached. Hylas and the Nymphs, by John William Waterhouse, shows a young man drawn toward a pool by pale water nymphs. In 2018, the gallery took it down for a short time, as part of an artwork by Sonia Boyce. The empty space and its notice invited visitors to discuss how women are shown in art. The debate went far beyond Manchester. The painting returned, but the conversation stayed.

The gallery has seen protest before. In 1913, suffragettes campaigning for women's right to vote entered these rooms and damaged several paintings. It is a reminder that galleries are never quite separate from the politics outside their doors. Look for the work of Adolphe Valette too. He was a French painter who taught art in Manchester. One of his students was L. S. Lowry, famous for his crowded industrial scenes. Valette's misty city views show the Manchester that Lowry learned to see.

The collection is wider than paintings. There are galleries of decorative arts, including ceramics, metalwork, furniture and glass, plus changing exhibitions of contemporary work. If you travel with children, look for the family spaces, where young visitors can draw, build and play. Spend time with a few objects rather than trying to see everything. A single painting, looked at closely for five minutes, often stays with you longer than a whole room seen quickly.

When you leave, pause again under the columns. The city carries on all around you, much as it did when Barry's builders laid these stones. Merchants gave way to students, office workers and travellers like you. Notice the people on the steps, the light on the sandstone, the sound of the street returning. Then walk on, toward the Town Hall perhaps, and carry one picture with you.
