The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through tall, dry grass with a sound like slow surf. Somewhere a bird calls, sharp and repeated, and then there is silence. The light here is wide and flat in the middle of the day, and soft and golden at its edges. The ground underfoot may be cracked clay, or sand, or springy grass, depending on the season and where you stand. You are in one of the largest protected landscapes in Central Africa, and very few people ever see it.

This is Manovo-Gounda Saint Floris National Park, in the north of the Central African Republic. The name is a map in itself. The Manovo and the Gounda are rivers that cross this country, and Saint Floris was the name of an older reserve that was folded into the larger park. Together they cover a vast territory of savanna, floodplain and plateau. The park reaches across more than one prefecture, and the Bamingui-Bangoran region is part of its southern frame.

The scale is hard to hold in your head. The park covers roughly 17,000 square kilometres, which is larger than many small countries. You could drive for a full day and still be inside it. There are no fences around this land. Animals have always moved across it freely, following water, grass and the turning of the seasons. That openness is the reason the park exists, and also the reason it is so difficult to protect.

In 1988, UNESCO added the park to its World Heritage List. The reason was not a building or a monument. It was the land itself, and the life it supports. UNESCO pointed to the wide range of habitats here, from wet grassland to wooded savanna to rocky upland. It also pointed to the animals that once filled these spaces in large numbers. Few places in this part of Africa held such a complete set of savanna wildlife.

Look at the landscape in layers. In the north, the land is low and flat. During the rainy season, the rivers spill over and turn wide areas into shallow wetland. When the water pulls back, it leaves rich grassland behind. Further south, the ground rises. Scattered trees become woodland, and woodland gives way to the sandstone heights of the Bongo Massif. Each layer is a different home, with different plants and different animals.

Those northern floodplains are the heart of the park. In the dry months, water becomes scarce across the region, and the remaining pools and river channels draw animals from far away. Historically, this is where herds of buffalo gathered, along with antelope of many kinds. Large wading birds stand in the shallows, and storks and pelicans work the water for fish. If you arrive in the dry season, water is the thing to watch. Where there is water, there is life.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The grass is not just scenery. It is fuel, food and shelter at once. In the dry season, fires often move across savannas like this one, some natural and some set by people. The burned ground looks bleak for a while. Then fresh green shoots push through the ash, and grazing animals come to feed on them. Fire, in a landscape like this, is part of how the land renews itself.

The list of animals once recorded here is long. Elephants, giraffes, buffalo, lions, leopards, cheetahs and African wild dogs all belong to this ecosystem. The park was also known for the black rhinoceros, and for smaller species such as the red-fronted gazelle. For a time, this was one of the richest wildlife areas in the whole region. That word, once, matters. The story of the park over recent decades is a hard one.

In 1997, UNESCO placed the park on its List of World Heritage in Danger. The main reason was poaching on a large scale. Armed groups crossed into the area, often from far away, hunting elephants for ivory and rhinos for their horns. Many other animals were killed for meat. Instability in the country made protection even harder. The black rhinoceros is now widely believed to be gone from the park, and other populations fell sharply.

That is the second surprise, and it is a sobering one. A place can be protected on paper and still be emptied in practice. Rangers here have worked in remote and dangerous conditions, often with limited support. Conservation groups and the national authorities have made repeated efforts to restore control over the land. The outcome is still uncertain. When you look across this savanna, you are looking at a landscape waiting to see what it will become.

And yet the land itself remains. The rivers still flood and retreat. The grass still grows tall in the rains and turns pale in the dry season. The sandstone of the southern hills still catches the late light in shades of rust and ochre. Birds still arrive with the water. An ecosystem this large keeps much of its structure even when it loses many of its animals. That structure is the foundation any recovery will need.

If you are here, you are among a very small number of visitors. There are no crowds, no souvenir stalls, no busy viewing platforms. What you get instead is space and quiet. Notice how far your eye can travel without meeting a road or a roof. Notice how the sound of wind changes as it passes from open grass into a stand of trees. Notice how small a single human figure looks against this horizon.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Look at the line where the grass meets the sky, and the shapes of the trees along the nearest river. Listen again for that bird call, and for the wind. Everything in front of you is part of a story that is not finished. As you walk away, carry one simple thought with you. This land is still here, still wide and still alive, and its future depends on people choosing to protect it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current government travel advisories before planning any visit.
Travel only with authorised operators and accompanying park rangers.
Come in the dry season when wildlife gathers near water.
Bring binoculars for distant birds along the river channels.
Carry ample water and sun protection for long open stretches.
