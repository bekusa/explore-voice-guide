The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your shoes. It is dry, pale, and loose, and the dust here holds tiny fragments of fired clay. Some of those fragments may be thousands of years old. Around you the land is flat and open, with the brown line of the Kirthar hills to the west and the Indus valley to the east. The wind moves across low mounds that look, at first, like ordinary rises in the earth. They are not ordinary. You are standing on one of the oldest excavated village sites in South Asia.

This is Amri. It sits on the western side of the Indus, between the river and the hills. It is a fair drive from Nawabshah, across the river. Today the village of Amri and its surrounding fields carry on with everyday life. But beneath these mounds lie layer after layer of human settlement. People lived here before the great cities of the Indus Valley were built. That one fact gives this quiet place its weight.

Archaeologists use the name Amri for a whole early culture. They call it the Amri culture, or the Amrian phase. It belongs to the period before the mature Indus Valley Civilisation — the civilisation of Mohenjo-daro and Harappa. Scholars generally place the earliest settlement here in the fourth millennium before Christ. In plain words, people were farming, building, and making pottery on this spot more than five thousand years ago.

The first serious excavation here came in 1929. It was led by an Indian archaeologist named N. G. Majumdar, who surveyed many ancient sites across Sindh. His work at Amri gave an early and important clue. Below the familiar Indus-style remains, he found an older layer with a different kind of pottery. This suggested that the story of the Indus Valley had a long beginning. The great cities did not appear from nothing. There was something here first.

Majumdar's story has a sad ending. He continued his survey work in Sindh in the following years. In 1938, during an expedition in the region, he was killed by bandits. Many visitors never hear this part of the story. When you look across these mounds, remember that the first person to read their layers carefully did not live to see how important his work would become.

A second major excavation came between 1959 and 1962. A French team led by the archaeologist Jean-Marie Casal returned to Amri. Casal dug more carefully through the layers and described a clear sequence of occupation. At the bottom were the early Amrian levels. Then came a period of transition. Above that sat remains from the mature Harappan age. Later still, other cultures left their own traces. One small patch of ground carried a long timeline of human life.

What did these early people leave behind? Their pottery is the clearest signature. Amrian pots are often thin and wheel-made, painted with geometric designs. Archaeologists describe bands, checks, lines, and repeated shapes, usually in dark paint, sometimes with a second colour. Some later pieces show simple animal figures. These painted patterns helped scholars recognise the same culture at other sites across Sindh and Balochistan. A pattern on a pot became a way to map an ancient world.

The builders of Amri worked with the materials around them. They used mud brick, and in some places stone from the nearby hills. Excavators found small rooms divided into compartments. Some scholars think these may have been used for storing grain, though their exact purpose is still discussed. Imagine the village in its early days — low walls, flat roofs, smoke from cooking fires, and the sound of grinding stones. Life here was practical and close to the land.

As you walk, notice the position of the site. The hills stand to one side. The river plain spreads out on the other. This was a sensible place for a village. People could reach water and fertile soil, and they had stone close at hand. Over the centuries, the Indus shifted its course many times across this plain. The landscape you see today is not exactly the one the first settlers knew. But the basic logic of the place is still clear.

Here is something many visitors miss. Amri is valuable not only for what it contains, but for its order. Because the layers were stacked one above another, archaeologists could see change over time. They could watch early village pottery slowly give way to Harappan styles. This kind of sequence is like a page of history written in soil. It showed that the Indus Valley Civilisation grew from local roots, step by step, rather than arriving suddenly from somewhere else.

Do not expect tall ruins or grand gateways. What you see here is mostly earth, scattered sherds, and the gentle shape of the mounds. The excavation trenches from earlier decades have weathered over time. That can surprise people who arrive expecting something like Mohenjo-daro. But there is a different kind of reward here. The quiet lets you think. You can look at the open land and picture a small community building the foundations of a much larger story.

Please treat the ground with care. The fragments of pottery you may notice belong to the site. Leave them where they lie. Each one is part of a record that archaeologists still study. Walk gently on the mounds, especially near the edges of old trenches, where the soil can crumble. In the heat of the day the sun is strong and there is little shade, so the early morning or late afternoon is the kindest time to explore.

As the light lowers, the colours of the land change. The dust turns warm and golden, and the hills to the west grow darker against the sky. Listen for the sounds of the present — a tractor in a nearby field, birds, voices from the village. People still live and work beside these mounds, as people have done here for thousands of years.

Before you leave, look down once more at the pale soil. Somewhere beneath it lie the floors where the first families of Amri cooked, stored grain, and painted their pots. Then look up at the open plain, the river valley, and the hills. The setting has changed, but it has not changed completely. As you walk back toward the road, you are crossing the same ground those early villagers crossed... one more set of footsteps on a very old path.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in early morning or late afternoon to avoid heat.
Bring water and sun protection; shade is very limited.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose soil and uneven mounds.
Leave any pottery fragments exactly where you find them.
Combine your trip with other sites along the Indus.
