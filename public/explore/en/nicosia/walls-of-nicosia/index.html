The guide

The story

Run your hand along the stone here. It is warm, a little gritty, and the colour of dry honey. This is local sandstone, cut in large blocks and stacked at a slant. Below you, where water might once have been expected, there is only grass, gravel, and perhaps a few parked cars. You are standing beside one of the most complete Renaissance fortifications in the Mediterranean, and most people walk past it on the way to coffee.

The walls you see were built by the Venetians in the late 1560s. Venice ruled Cyprus at the time, and its merchants and admirals were nervous. The Ottoman Empire was growing stronger across the eastern Mediterranean. Cyprus sat right in its path. Nicosia, the capital in the centre of the island, needed protection that could stand up to modern cannon. So Venice sent military engineers, and they designed something entirely new for the city.

Nicosia already had walls before this. The Lusignan kings, a French noble dynasty that ruled Cyprus in the Middle Ages, had built a much larger circuit. But those older walls were tall and thin. They were made for an age of arrows and siege towers, not gunpowder. Against cannon, tall walls simply crack and fall. The Venetians decided the old city was too big to defend. Their answer was radical — make the city smaller.

And they did exactly that. To shrink the circle, the engineers cleared away whatever stood in the path of the new line and the open ground outside it. Churches, houses, monasteries, and palaces were demolished. Historians often mention that a large Dominican monastery, where Lusignan royals were buried, was among the losses. Imagine living here then. Your neighbourhood could vanish in a season, simply because it stood too close to where the defences had to go.

The chief designer most often named is Giulio Savorgnan, a respected Venetian military engineer. His plan was elegant and mathematical. The walls form an almost perfect circle, roughly five kilometres around. Spaced evenly along that circle are eleven bastions, each shaped like an arrowhead or a heart. From the air, the whole shape looks like a star, or a snowflake. Here is a small surprise: the city of Nicosia still uses this outline in its civic symbols today.

Why those pointed bastions? It is a matter of angles. A round tower leaves blind spots at its base, where attackers can shelter. A pointed bastion leaves none. Soldiers on one bastion could fire along the face of the next, covering every metre of the wall. The walls themselves are low and thick, sloped and backed with packed earth. A cannonball does not shatter earth. It sinks in. This design came from Italy, and it is sometimes called the trace italienne, or the Italian style of fortification.

Many bastions carry Italian family names, such as Caraffa, Podocataro, D'Avila, Barbaro, and Quirini. These names are usually linked to the noble families who helped pay for the work, or who were connected to its defence. Walking the circuit today, you can think of it as a ring of names. Each bastion is a small monument to someone who expected to defend this city for generations. That expectation did not last long.

In 1570, before the work was fully finished, an Ottoman army landed on Cyprus and marched on Nicosia. The siege lasted around seven weeks through the heat of summer. The defenders were outnumbered and short of trained soldiers. In September, the Ottomans broke through, and the city fell. The cost in lives was very heavy. So the most modern walls in the eastern Mediterranean were tested only once in battle, and they were overcome almost as soon as they were built.

The Ottomans kept the walls and repaired them. The city stayed inside its circle for about three centuries. Entry was through three gates: Kyrenia Gate to the north, Paphos Gate to the west, and Famagusta Gate to the east. Each gate pointed toward the town it was named after. Famagusta Gate, with its long, dim vaulted passage, is now used as a cultural centre. Concerts and exhibitions fill the space where carts and soldiers once passed through.

When Britain took over the administration of Cyprus in 1878, the city began to spill outside the walls. Motor traffic arrived, and the narrow gates became a problem. At Kyrenia Gate, the British cut openings through the wall on either side, so roads could run around the old gateway. The gate now stands almost alone, like a small island in traffic. Look up at it, and you may notice the small room built on top during Ottoman times.

The moat that surrounds the walls was never really a water moat. It was a wide, dry ditch, designed to keep attackers exposed below the guns. Today, Nicosia uses this ditch in very practical ways. Parts of it hold gardens, playgrounds, sports grounds, and public car parks. There is something quietly funny about that. A space designed for killing is now where people walk their dogs and look for parking.

There is a more serious layer, too. Since 1974, Nicosia has been divided. A buffer zone, often called the Green Line, runs through the old city from west to east. Some bastions lie in the north, some in the south, and the line itself cuts across the walls. This is a detail many visitors miss. The circle that was built to unite and protect one city is now crossed by a border. You can walk part of the ring on each side, but not the whole loop in one continuous stroll.

On the southern side, the Podocataro Bastion carries the Liberty Monument, a large sculpture group that marks the island's independence struggle. Elsewhere, bastions hold a town hall, offices, and parks. Each section of wall has found a different second life. Some stretches are clean and restored, with crisp stone edges. Others are weathered, patched with newer masonry, and softened by weeds growing from the joints.

Before you move on, take one more look along the line of the wall. Notice how low it sits, how it leans slightly backward, how it turns sharply at each bastion point. It was built for one war, lost in one summer, and has outlived every empire that held it. Listen for the traffic, the birds in the moat, the voices from the café nearby. The wall is still here, holding the old city in its circle.

Practical tips

Walk sections on both sides of the Green Line for context.
Bring your passport or ID for crossing at Ledra Street.
Visit Famagusta Gate in the evening for cultural events.
Walk the moat paths early morning before the heat builds.
