The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. It is dry, pale and crumbly, with loose pebbles and patches of wild thyme. In summer the air smells of dust and warm resin. In spring the low hills turn green for a few short weeks. Around you, two gentle rises lift out of the plain. Somewhere below them, a river bed runs, often dry. This quiet landscape near the village of Dali once held one of the ancient kingdoms of Cyprus.

This is Idalion. In the first millennium before Christ, Cyprus was not one state. It was divided into a group of small city-kingdoms, each with its own king, its own gods and its own coins. Idalion was one of them. It sat inland, away from the coast, in the centre of the island. That position mattered. It gave the city access to fertile farmland and to the copper-bearing foothills that made Cyprus valuable to the whole Mediterranean.

Look at the two hills again. Archaeologists usually describe the ancient city as having two acropolis hills, a western one and an eastern one, with the lower town spread between them. An acropolis is simply a fortified high point of a city, a place for temples, rulers and defence. Here the hills are low, not dramatic. But in a flat plain, even a modest height gives you a long view. Guards on these slopes could watch the approaches from every direction.

The western hill held a sanctuary connected with a goddess the Greeks knew as Athena. Excavations there found walls of a fortified sacred area and many offerings left by worshippers. People came with figurines, small objects in clay and stone, and gifts made of metal. Cyprus was a meeting place of cultures, so these offerings mix influences. Some look Greek. Some look Phoenician. Some look Egyptian or Near Eastern. Many look simply Cypriot, with a style found nowhere else.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. The most famous object from Idalion is not here at all. It is a bronze tablet, inscribed on both sides, now kept in Paris at the National Library of France. It dates to the fifth century before Christ. It is written in the Cypriot syllabary, a local script where each sign stands for a syllable rather than a single letter. Through this script, Cypriots wrote their own dialect of Greek.

What does the tablet say? It records an agreement. During a siege of Idalion, the king and the city promised payment to a doctor named Onasilos and his brothers. Their task was to treat the wounded without charging them a fee. In return, the city promised the family land and silver. The text names the attackers as the Medes, meaning the Persians, together with the people of Kition. It is one of the earliest known public contracts for medical care anywhere in the Greek-speaking world.

Kition was a neighbouring kingdom on the southern coast, near today's Larnaca. It was ruled by a Phoenician dynasty. The Phoenicians were seafaring traders from the coast of what is now Lebanon. Historians believe that, not long after the siege described on the tablet, Kition took control of Idalion during the fifth century before Christ. Idalion lost its own kings. From then on, it served the rulers of Kition, and its copper and harvests flowed toward the coast.

That change left marks in the ground. On the eastern hill, Cypriot archaeologists uncovered a large fortified administrative building from the period of Kitian rule. Inside, they found many inscribed fragments written in Phoenician, often on broken pottery. These seem to be records of administration — goods, payments, accounts. It is a rare thing. We usually know ancient kingdoms through temples and tombs. Here we also have traces of their bookkeeping, the everyday paperwork of power.

Now for the second surprise. In the nineteenth century, Idalion was badly plundered. Foreign diplomats and collectors dug here with little care for recording where objects came from. The most notorious was Luigi Palma di Cesnola, an American consul who shipped thousands of Cypriot antiquities abroad. Many of them ended up in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. So when you walk this site, remember that pieces of it sit in museums across Europe and America, far from the soil you are standing on.

Scientific work came later. In the late 1920s, the Swedish Cyprus Expedition excavated on the western hill and recorded what they found with new care. In more recent decades, the Cyprus Department of Antiquities and foreign university teams have continued the work. Each season adds detail. The picture is still incomplete, which is part of what makes Idalion interesting. You are visiting a place where the story is still being written, one trench at a time.

Idalion also has a place in myth. Ancient poets linked it with Aphrodite, the goddess of love, whom many Greeks believed was born from the sea off Cyprus. According to one popular tale, her beloved Adonis was killed by a wild boar while hunting in this region. This is legend, not history. But worship of Aphrodite on Cyprus was real and ancient, and the story shows how strongly the Greek world connected this inland kingdom with her.

If you have time, visit the Local Museum of Ancient Idalion, close to the site. It gives the hills a human scale. You will see pottery, figurines, tools and objects from daily life, along with clear explanations of the excavations. It also helps you understand what the plain looked like when the city was alive. After the museum, the low walls and scattered stones outside make more sense. You start to see streets and buildings where before there was only rubble.

As you walk, pay attention to the stones themselves. Much of the construction uses local rock, rough and uneven, set without the polished finish of later Roman buildings. This was a practical kingdom, built by farmers, metalworkers and soldiers. The walls were made to hold, not to impress. Notice too how quiet it is. A dog barks in the village. A tractor moves somewhere in the fields. Birds call from the scrub on the slopes.

Before you leave, stand still on one of the hills and look around once more. The plain stretches out toward the mountains, and the modern village sits close by, its rooftops catching the light. Thousands of years ago, a king and his citizens watched an enemy army from somewhere near this spot. They made a promise to a doctor, and they wrote it in bronze. Take that thought with you as you walk back down the path.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Local Museum of Ancient Idalion before the hills.
Come early morning to avoid strong midday summer heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose, uneven ground on the slopes.
Bring water and a hat; shade on the site is limited.
Climb one hill for a view across the whole plain.
