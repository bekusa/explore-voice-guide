The guide

The story

Step inside, and let your eyes adjust. The light here is soft and golden, and it seems to come from everywhere at once. Gilded carvings climb the walls in tiers. Painted curtains, swags and tassels hang in place. And the air carries a faint, dry scent of old wood. Take a slow breath and look up. You are standing inside one of the finest surviving Baroque theatres in Europe, and almost everything around you is made to look richer than it is.

This is the Margravial Opera House in Bayreuth. It was built in the 1740s for a small German court. That court had big ambitions and a remarkable woman at its centre. Her name was Wilhelmine, the Margravine of Brandenburg-Bayreuth. A margravine is the wife of a margrave, a ruling noble of a frontier territory. Wilhelmine was also the elder sister of Frederick the Great of Prussia, and she never let anyone forget her royal upbringing.

Wilhelmine did not arrive in Bayreuth happily. By her own account, she found the provincial court dull compared with Berlin. So she set about changing it. She wrote, she painted, and she composed music. She even wrote an opera of her own. Over the years she and her husband, Margrave Frederick, turned this quiet town into a place of gardens, palaces and performance. This theatre was the jewel of that project.

The opera house opened in 1748. The occasion was a court wedding. The couple's only daughter, Elisabeth Fredericka Sophie, was marrying Duke Karl Eugen of Württemberg. A wedding of that rank needed a setting that would impress visiting royalty. It needed to say that Bayreuth was small, perhaps, but not unsophisticated. The building around you was the answer to that challenge.

Now look closely at the decoration. The interior was designed by Giuseppe Galli Bibiena, working with his son Carlo. The Bibiena family came from Bologna in Italy. Across several generations, they were the most sought-after theatre designers in Europe. They specialised in illusion — painted architecture that seems to stretch far beyond the real walls. Here, Giuseppe created one of his masterpieces. And it is one of very few Bibiena interiors that still survives.

Here is the first surprise. Much of what looks like marble, stone and heavy fabric is actually wood and canvas. The columns, the drapery and the sculpted figures are largely carved timber and painted surfaces. The curtains that seem to hang in folds are painted. This was cheaper than real stone, and much lighter. It also helped the sound. Wood resonates, so a voice or a violin carries warmly through the hall.

Let your gaze move to the tiers of boxes. They rise in layers around the horseshoe-shaped auditorium. In the eighteenth century, where you sat said everything about who you were. The most important seat was not facing the stage directly from the front rows. It was in the grand court box at the back, opposite the stage. From there, the margrave and his guests could see the performance. Just as importantly, everyone in the house could see them.

That is worth remembering as you look around. A Baroque court theatre was not only a place to watch opera. It was also a stage for the court itself. Nobles watched the singers, but they also watched each other. Who arrived with whom, who sat closer to the ruling family, who received a nod. The architecture was designed to make the audience part of the show.

When you step back outside later, compare the façade with the interior. The front of the building was designed by the French architect Joseph Saint-Pierre. It is built of sandstone, calm and orderly, with columns and a balcony. It gives little hint of the theatrical gold waiting inside. That contrast was common in the period. The outside speaks to the town. The inside speaks to the court.

Now for the second surprise. More than a century after it opened, this theatre played a part in music history. In the 1870s, the composer Richard Wagner came to Bayreuth. He was looking for a place to stage his huge operas. He knew about this house and its unusually deep stage. But for his vast productions, it was still not large enough. So Wagner built his own festival theatre on a hill outside town instead. In a sense, this small Baroque theatre helped bring Wagner to Bayreuth.

That festival hill still draws music lovers every summer. Yet this older house has its own story of survival. In 2012, UNESCO added the Margravial Opera House to its World Heritage List. Soon after, a long and careful restoration began. Conservators worked on the painted surfaces, the gilding and the wooden structure. The theatre reopened in 2018. The colours you see today are the result of that patient work.

Take a moment to notice the scale of the stage, too. The stage area is deep, built for elaborate scenery that could slide and change. Baroque opera loved spectacle. Clouds descended, gods appeared, and palaces turned into gardens in a moment. Imagine candlelight instead of electric light, flickering across all this gold. The whole room would have shimmered, and the painted illusions would have looked even more convincing.

Before you leave, stand still for a few seconds. Listen to the quiet of the hall, and to the creak of old wood as people move around you. Look once more at the court box, and at the painted drapery that never moved at all. Then, as you step back into the streets of Bayreuth, glance at the plain sandstone front behind you. It is hard to believe that so much gold, so much theatre, and so much ambition waits behind that calm face.
