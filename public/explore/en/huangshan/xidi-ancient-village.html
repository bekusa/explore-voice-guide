The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, water is running over stone. It slips along narrow channels beside the lanes, past doorsteps and under small slab bridges. The paving under your feet is uneven, worn smooth by centuries of cloth shoes and wooden carts. Above you, white walls rise and close in, and the sky becomes a thin strip of blue. This is Xidi, a village in Yi County, in the hills of southern Anhui, about an hour's drive from the peaks of Huangshan.

The name gives a clue to what you are hearing. Xidi roughly means the western stream, and locals explain that the streams here run west. In most of this region, water flows east toward the sea. So the village took its name from something unusual in its own landscape. Its older name, Xichuan, carried the same idea. As you walk, follow one of these channels for a while. They were planned. They brought water for washing, for cooking, and for fighting fires.

For most of its life, this was the home of a single family, the Hu clan. They settled here around the Northern Song dynasty, roughly a thousand years ago. There is a story that comes with them, and it is important to call it a story. Legend has it that the clan's founder was a son of a Tang dynasty emperor. He was smuggled out as a baby during the chaos at the end of that dynasty, and a family named Hu raised him as their own. Historians treat this as family tradition, not proven fact. But the Hu clan held it with pride.

Now look at the shapes around you. This is Huizhou architecture, the building style of this old region. The walls are lime-white. The roofs are covered with small dark grey tiles. And the tops of the walls step upward in tiers, like a staircase in the air. People here call them horse-head walls. Their main job was practical. They are firewalls, built to stop flames jumping from one roof to the next in a village packed this tightly.

Why is a mountain village this fine? The answer is trade. From the Ming dynasty onward, men from Huizhou left home to work as merchants across China. Many dealt in salt, timber, and tea, and some became very rich. They spent years away, sometimes decades. Their wealth came back here as houses, ancestral halls, and gardens. Xidi grew during this period, and many of the buildings you see date from the Ming and Qing dynasties, roughly the fifteenth to the nineteenth centuries.

Here is something most visitors miss. From the lane, many of these grand houses look almost blank. The outer walls are tall, and the windows are few, small, and set high up. That was deliberate. With the men away trading, families wanted security and privacy. The beauty was turned inward. To see it, you have to step through the doorway and into the courtyard.

Inside, light falls through an opening in the roof called a skywell. It is a small rectangle of sky in the middle of the house. Rain falls through it too, and runs into the courtyard from all four sloping roofs. There is a local saying about this. Water is linked with wealth, so rain flowing inward meant fortune stayed inside the family. Stand under a skywell for a moment. Notice how the light moves down the dark wooden beams as the day goes on.

Next, look closely at the decoration. Huizhou is known for three kinds of carving, in wood, brick, and stone. Over gateways you will find brick panels carved with scenes of figures, flowers, and buildings, so detailed they look like miniature stages. Indoors, the wooden window lattices and beam brackets are carved with deer, bats, vases, and lotus. Each image usually carries a hopeful meaning, such as long life, success in exams, or many children.

In the main halls, look at the long table against the back wall. A common Huizhou arrangement places a clock in the centre, a vase on one side, and a mirror on the other. Locals explain it as a pun in Chinese. The words for vase and mirror sound like the words for peace and calm. Together with the ticking clock, the setting was read as a wish for a lifetime of peace. Not every house still keeps it, but watch for it.

On the door frames and pillars, you will see pairs of written lines called couplets. Many are about study, honesty, and modest living. This matters, because Huizhou merchants were famous for respecting learning. Wealthy families paid for sons to study for the imperial examinations, which could lead to official posts. Money from trade was meant to become respect through scholarship. The couplets carry that ambition in a few carved characters.

The Hu clan also left a mark at the entrance to the village. A tall stone memorial archway stands there, built in the Ming dynasty to honour a clan member who served as an official. These archways, called paifang in Chinese, were granted to recognise achievement or virtue. Older visitors remember the village had several more, but this is the main survivor. Walk around it slowly. The stone is grey and weathered, and the carving is still sharp in places.

In 2000, Xidi and its neighbour Hongcun were listed together by UNESCO as World Heritage. The listing recognised them as rare surviving examples of traditional villages in this region. Much of the street plan and building stock has lasted. People still live here. You will pass open doors, drying laundry, small shops, and kitchens. You may also see rows of art students with easels, sketching the walls and rooftops. The village has long been a favourite place for drawing practice.

Before you leave, find a quiet corner and stand still. Look up at the stepped white walls against the hills. Listen again for the water in its stone channels. It is the same sound you heard when you arrived, and the same sound families here heard for centuries while they waited for travellers to come home. As you walk back toward the archway, notice which doors are open, and which ones stay closed.
