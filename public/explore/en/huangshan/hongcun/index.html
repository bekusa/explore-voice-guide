The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, water is moving. It runs through a narrow stone channel at your feet, barely wider than a hand span, and it makes a soft, steady sound against the granite. Look down and you may see it slipping past a doorstep. Look up and you see white walls, black tiles, and stepped rooflines cut sharply against the hills. Welcome to Hongcun, an old village in the Huizhou region of southern Anhui, in the shadow of the Huangshan mountains.

Hongcun is not a museum built to look old. People still live here. Laundry hangs from bamboo poles. Someone is washing vegetables in a channel. A cat sleeps on a worn stone step. In 2000, UNESCO listed the village as a World Heritage Site, together with its neighbour Xidi. The listing honours them as rare survivors of a traditional rural landscape — villages whose layout, buildings and water systems stayed largely intact across many centuries.

The story begins with one family. Hongcun was settled by the Wang clan during the Southern Song dynasty, in the twelfth century. Over generations, the clan shaped the village around a single, practical idea. Water had to reach every household. It was needed for drinking, for washing, and above all for fighting fire, which was a constant danger in a village of tightly packed timber interiors. Much of what you see today grew from that need.

Here is the first surprise. According to a long tradition, the whole village was planned in the shape of an ox. The hill behind the village is the ox's head. Two old trees near the entrance are its horns. The houses form its body, and the small bridges are its legs. The water channels running through the lanes are its intestines. Locals say the design was meant to bring the clan strength and good fortune. Whether it was planned that way from the start, or described that way later, the image is easy to hold in your mind as you walk.

Now follow the water toward the heart of the village. You will come to the Moon Pond, a half-moon of still, dark water, surrounded by houses and an old ancestral hall. On a calm day, the white walls and black roofs appear upside down on its surface, almost perfectly. In the ox story, this pond is the animal's stomach. Notice how the houses stand right at the water's edge. Their front steps lead straight down to the pond.

The pond is shaped like a half-moon, not a full one, and there is a traditional explanation for this. A saying often linked to the pond holds that a full moon begins to wane, while a half-moon still has room to grow. For a family that hoped to rise in fortune, the half-moon was the better sign. It is a small detail, but it tells you how closely the builders tied everyday design to hope and meaning.

The second large body of water is South Lake, at the southern edge of the village. It was added later, during the Ming dynasty, as the village grew. In the ox story, this is the animal's belly. A narrow arched bridge crosses the lake, and willows lean over the water. Film lovers may recognise this scene. Parts of Ang Lee's film Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon were shot here, and the lake and its bridge appear on screen.

Along the lake's edge you will almost certainly see students sitting on small folding stools with sketchbooks or easels. Hongcun is a favourite place for art schools across China to send students to practise drawing. The reason is simple. The strong contrast of white wall and black tile, the clean geometry of the rooflines, and the mirror of the water make an ideal subject for line drawing and ink painting. Pause and watch for a moment — just give them a little space.

That contrast of white and black is the signature of Huizhou architecture. The walls are plastered white. The roofs are covered in grey-black tiles. And at the ends of many houses, you will see stepped walls that rise above the roof in tiers. These are often called horse-head walls, because their tips can look like a horse lifting its head. They are not just decoration. They work as firewalls, stopping flames from jumping from one roof to the next.

Why is a small mountain village so finely built? The answer is trade. From the Ming dynasty onward, Huizhou merchants became some of the most successful traders in China, dealing in salt, tea, timber and more. Many left home young and worked far away. When they grew rich, they sent money back to build fine houses, ancestral halls and schools. The quiet streets around you were paid for by fortunes made in distant cities.

The clearest example is Chengzhi Hall, built by a salt merchant in the late Qing dynasty, the last imperial era of China. From the outside it looks modest. Inside, the wooden beams and panels are covered with very fine carvings — scenes of daily life, opera stories, fishing, and celebrations. Some surfaces still carry traces of gold leaf. Many visitors walk in expecting a simple house and stop at the first carved beam. Take your time here and look closely.

As you wander, notice how the houses face inward. Windows on the outer walls are small and set high. Inside, many homes open onto a small courtyard, sometimes called a sky well, where light and rain enter from above. Rainwater collected there was seen as wealth flowing into the house, never away from it. It is a simple, practical design. It kept the homes cool, private and safe while their owners were away trading.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot by a channel and stand still for a moment. Feel the cool air near the water. Watch the light move across the white walls. Listen to footsteps on stone, a door closing, water running. Generations of the Wang family shaped this place so that water would pass every home. It still does. As you walk back toward the village entrance, follow the channel beside you — it has been running this way for centuries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early morning for mist and calm reflections on the water.
Stay overnight to see the lanes after day groups leave.
Wear shoes with grip for smooth, damp stone lanes.
Give sketching art students space along South Lake.
Respect residents; many houses are private family homes.
