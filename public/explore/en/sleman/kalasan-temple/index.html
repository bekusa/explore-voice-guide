The guide

The story

Listen first. Behind you, motorbikes and buses hum along the main road between Yogyakarta and Solo. In front of you, there is a small green lawn, and a block of dark grey volcanic stone that seems to absorb the heat of the day. Run your eyes slowly over the surface. In places, you may notice something unusual — a thin, pale skin on the stone, like old plaster. That skin is one of the quiet surprises of this place, and we will come back to it.

This is Kalasan Temple, in the Sleman area of Yogyakarta. It sits only a few kilometres west of the great Prambanan complex, yet many travellers drive straight past it. That is a pity. Kalasan is among the oldest Buddhist temples in this part of Java. It is older than the famous towers of Prambanan, and it belongs to the same era of royal building that also produced Borobudur, the vast Buddhist monument northwest of Yogyakarta.

The temple can be dated with unusual confidence, because of a carved stone inscription found nearby. Scholars call it the Kalasan inscription. It is written in Sanskrit, the classical language of Indian religion and learning, and in a north Indian style of script. It records a date in the Saka calendar that corresponds to the year 778. For a monument this old, a written foundation date is rare. Most temples on Java leave historians guessing.

What does the inscription say? It describes a ruler with the title Panangkaran, linked to the powerful Sailendra dynasty, a royal family that supported Buddhism in Central Java during the eighth and ninth centuries. According to the text, Buddhist teachers asked for a sanctuary dedicated to the goddess Tara. The king agreed, and he granted the village of Kalasa to the Buddhist community. That village name likely survives today in the name Kalasan. The original stone is now kept in Jakarta.

So who is Tara? In Mahayana Buddhism, the branch that spread widely across East and Southeast Asia, Tara is a female figure of compassion. Devotees call on her for protection and for rescue from danger. Kalasan was built as her home. Look toward the main chamber. The great statue of Tara that once stood inside is gone. Historians believe it may have been made of bronze. Today, only the empty space and the stone pedestal remain.

Now walk slowly around the outside. Notice the shape of the building. It is not a simple square. The plan forms a cross, with projecting sections on each of the four sides. Each projection once held its own smaller chamber. The main entrance faces east, toward the rising sun. As you circle, you can see how the walls step in and out, creating deep shadows that move across the stone as the day goes on.

Here is that first surprise again. Look closely at the carvings and the wall surfaces. In some protected corners, a fine white layer still clings to the stone. Javanese builders used a special coating, often called vajralepa, a word that roughly means diamond plaster. It sealed the soft stone and gave it a bright, smooth finish. Picture the temple fully covered in it. It would have shone almost white against the green rice fields.

The carvings deserve your time. Above the doorways, look for the face of Kala, a guardian figure with wide eyes, bared teeth and no lower jaw. Kala heads appear on many Javanese temples, but the one at Kalasan is admired by scholars for its depth and fine detail. Around the guardian, carved vines, flowers and small heavenly figures twist across the stone. Even after more than twelve centuries, the edges of the leaves still feel crisp.

Now raise your eyes to the roof. The upper part of the temple rises in tiers, and the outline changes as it climbs. Small bell-shaped forms, called stupas, ring the upper levels. A stupa is a Buddhist shrine shape that represents the presence of the Buddha and the path to enlightenment. Many of the upper stones are damaged or missing. Still, you can follow the rhythm of the design and imagine the full crown that once rose here.

The second surprise is about what you are not seeing. When the Dutch colonial archaeological service began studying Kalasan in the early twentieth century, they found evidence of earlier building layers inside. The temple was rebuilt and enlarged more than once. Its present form wraps around older structures. In other words, the walls in front of you are a later shell. Somewhere inside the stone, earlier versions of the sanctuary are still hidden from view.

Kalasan also has a neighbour. Just a short distance to the north stands Sari Temple, a two-storey building that many scholars link to Kalasan. One common idea is that Sari served as a residence or study hall for the monks who cared for the shrine of Tara. Visiting both gives a fuller picture. One building was for the goddess. The other may have been for the people who served her.

This area has seen hard times. Java sits on an active volcanic zone, and earthquakes have shaken these temples many times over the centuries. Mount Merapi, the volcano to the north, has also buried and reshaped the landscape around Yogyakarta. Some stones here have been reset by restorers, and gaps show where pieces could not be found. Each missing block is a small reminder of how fragile even solid stone can be.

Before you leave, stand at the east side again, where pilgrims once entered. Let the noise of the road fade into the background for a moment. Look at the pale traces of plaster, the guardian face above the door, the empty chamber where Tara once sat. Twelve centuries ago, a king gave a whole village to keep this shrine alive. As you walk away, glance back once — and notice how the grey stone holds the afternoon light.
