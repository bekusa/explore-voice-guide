The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see the gold, you hear the shrine. There is the shuffle of thousands of bare feet on cool marble, a low murmur of prayer, and the soft clink of a door ring touched for luck. The air smells of rosewater and dust. Overhead, two gilded domes catch the Baghdad sun so strongly that people often lower their eyes. You are standing in Kadhimiya, on the northwestern edge of the city, at one of the holiest places in Shia Islam.

This mosque is really a tomb. In fact, it holds two. Beneath the domes rest two men whom Twelver Shia Muslims honour as Imams. In this tradition, an Imam is a divinely guided leader descended from the Prophet Muhammad. The first is Musa al-Kadhim, the seventh Imam. The second is his grandson, Muhammad al-Jawad, the ninth Imam. The whole neighbourhood takes its name from the first of them, and the mosque does too.

The names carry meaning. Al-Kadhim means, roughly, the one who restrains his anger. Pilgrims remember him as a patient man who endured great hardship without rage. Al-Jawad means the generous one. These are not formal titles carved on a document. They are names given with affection, and you will hear them spoken here constantly. Listen for the phrase Bab al-Hawaij, the Gate of Needs. It is the nickname many believers give Musa al-Kadhim, because people come to him with their hopes.

His story is a hard one. Musa al-Kadhim lived in the eighth century, when the Abbasid caliphs ruled from Baghdad. The Abbasids saw the Imams as dangerous rivals, because many Muslims looked to them for leadership. Musa al-Kadhim was held in prison for years. He died in custody in 799. His body was buried in a cemetery on this side of the Tigris River, a burial ground that the early Abbasids had set aside for their own Quraysh tribe.

That is the first surprise of this place. The ground was meant for the ruling family. Over the centuries, the graves of the caliphs faded from memory, while the grave of their prisoner became a centre of pilgrimage. Then, in the ninth century, his grandson Muhammad al-Jawad was buried beside him. He died young, in 835, still in his mid-twenties. From that moment, this cemetery had two Imams, and a town slowly grew around them.

What you see today is not the first building on this spot. Shrines here were built, damaged, and rebuilt many times. Floods from the Tigris, fires, and wars all left their mark. The basic shape of the present complex comes from the early sixteenth century, when the Safavid ruler Shah Ismail of Persia ordered a major rebuilding. Later work came under Ottoman rule, and in the nineteenth century the Qajar rulers of Iran paid to cover the domes in gold.

So the building carries traces of several empires at once. The tilework is Persian in style, with deep blues, turquoise, and yellow, and flowing calligraphy. The courtyard plan feels Iraqi and open. The gold came from Iranian patrons. Look up at the minarets. There are four of them, tall and slender, their tops also covered in gold. Together with the twin domes, they make a skyline you can recognise from far across the city.

Now look at the doorways and archways as you move inside. Many are framed with tile panels, and the inner halls glitter with mirror work. Thousands of small pieces of cut mirror are set into the ceilings and walls. Each one catches a lamp, so the room seems to shimmer as you walk. This craft is strongly associated with Persian shrines. It turns a simple light into a field of stars, and it is designed to make you slow down.

At the heart of the shrine is the zarih, a large metal enclosure built over the tombs. It looks like a cage of silver and gold lattice. This is the most emotional point in the building. Pilgrims press their hands and faces against it. Some weep. Some whisper long prayers. Some push folded notes or coins through the openings. Parents lift small children so they can touch the metal. If you stand back quietly, you will feel how personal this place is for the people around you.

Here is a second detail that many visitors miss. The shrine is not a museum, and it is not quiet in the way a cathedral can be quiet. Families eat together in the courtyard. Old men sleep in the shade of the arches. Students recite the Quran. Cleaners sweep constantly, because so many feet pass every hour. The holiness here lives alongside ordinary life, and that mixture is part of what pilgrims love.

On major religious anniversaries, the scale changes completely. The most important is the anniversary of Musa al-Kadhim's death in the Islamic month of Rajab. Huge numbers of pilgrims walk here from across Baghdad and beyond, many of them on foot. Volunteers set up tents along the roads and hand out water, tea, and food for free. These crowds have also known tragedy. In 2005, rumours of a bomb caused a deadly stampede on the nearby Al-Aimmah Bridge, and many hundreds of pilgrims died.

That bridge carries a quieter meaning too. It crosses the Tigris and connects Kadhimiya with Adhamiyah on the other bank. Adhamiyah grew around the tomb of Abu Hanifa, a great scholar honoured by Sunni Muslims. So two neighbourhoods, built around two revered graves from two traditions, face each other across the same river. In calmer years, residents from both sides have crossed to help each other. The bridge is a reminder that Baghdad's faiths have always lived close together.

Beyond the walls, the old streets of Kadhimiya press right up to the shrine. Narrow lanes are lined with shops selling prayer beads, small clay prayer tablets, scarves, perfume oils, and pictures of the Imams. The district is also well known for its gold sellers, and their windows glow almost as brightly as the domes. Street vendors fry snacks and pour sweet tea. This commercial life has fed and housed pilgrims here for centuries.

Before you leave, find a spot in the courtyard and stand still for a minute. Watch the light move across the gold as clouds pass. Notice how people touch the door frames as they leave, and how many walk out backwards, facing the tombs. Listen again to the feet on the marble. Then, as you step into the lanes of Kadhimiya, look back once. The two domes will still be there above the rooftops, the same way they have guided travellers for generations.

Before you go

Practical tips

Women should wear a full abaya; loaners are often offered nearby.
Expect thorough security checks and leave large bags behind.
Remove shoes at the entrance and use the shoe-keeping counters.
Ask before photographing pilgrims, especially near the tombs.
Avoid major anniversaries in Rajab unless you want huge crowds.
