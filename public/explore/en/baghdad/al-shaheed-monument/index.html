The guide

The story

Listen first. Water moves gently around the base of the platform. Wind crosses open paving. Somewhere behind you, Baghdad traffic hums without pause. Then you look up and see two enormous curved shells of turquoise tile, each one shining in the sun. They lean toward each other, and they do not touch. The sky shows through the gap between them, and that gap is the heart of this place.

This is the Al-Shaheed Monument, often called the Martyr's Memorial in English. The Arabic word shaheed means martyr, a person who has died for a cause or for their country. The monument stands on the eastern bank of the Tigris, in the half of Baghdad known as Rusafa. It opened in the early 1980s. It was built to honour Iraqi soldiers who died in the long war between Iraq and Iran. Over time, many Iraqis have come to see it as a memorial to all of the country's dead.

Look carefully at the shape. It began as a single dome. The designers cut it in half and then moved the two halves apart. They also turned them so that they are offset, not mirrored, and face each other at an angle. Walk around the platform and the relationship between the halves keeps changing. From one side they look like two hands cupped around something precious. From another side they look like a wound that will not close.

The dome is a familiar form across the Islamic world. Mosques and shrines in Iraq often carry glazed domes in blue, green, or gold. So an Iraqi visitor recognises this shape at once, and feels its sacred weight. But no traditional builder would split a dome in two. That single act turns a symbol of wholeness and heaven into a symbol of loss. It is a simple idea, and it is very hard to forget.

The monument is usually credited to the Iraqi sculptor Ismail Fattah al-Turk, working together with an architectural team. Al-Turk was one of the leading figures of modern Iraqi art in the twentieth century. He was known for sculpture that was bold and abstract, yet rooted in local tradition. Here, he worked on a scale that most sculptors never reach. The result is part building, part landscape, and part artwork.

Now look at the tiles. The surface of each half is covered in glazed ceramic in a deep turquoise. In strong midday light the colour almost turns white at the edges. Late in the afternoon it darkens toward green and blue. Up close, you can see that the colour is not one flat sheet. It is made of many small units, and each one catches the light slightly differently.

Between the two halves, at the centre, is the focus of the whole design. Here an eternal flame was placed, a fire meant to burn for the dead without ending. The two shells seem to protect it, or to bow toward it. Stand in line with the gap and look straight through. You will see how the whole composition guides your eye to this one point.

Here is something many visitors miss. A large part of the monument lies beneath your feet. Under the platform there are spaces for a museum, a library, galleries, and a lecture hall. The upper part is the image, and the lower part was designed for memory and study. It is a reminder that this was planned as a working institution, not only a structure to photograph from a distance.

The monument also does not stand on dry ground. It rises from a wide circular platform set in the middle of an artificial lake. Walkways and bridges connect it to the surrounding park. The water does two things. It reflects the turquoise shells, so on a calm day you see the dome twice. And it creates a quiet edge, a short distance that you must cross to reach the centre.

Think about the scale for a moment. Each half of the dome is several storeys tall. People walking at the base look very small. That contrast is deliberate. A war memorial must hold the grief of many families at once, and so it must be larger than any single person. Yet the open gap keeps it from feeling like a closed fortress.

The years since the monument opened have been hard for Baghdad. The city has lived through more wars, sanctions, and long periods of instability. The memorial has stayed in the skyline through all of it. Its image has appeared on posters, in photographs, and in the memory of people who grew up in the city. For many Baghdadis it is simply part of home, a turquoise landmark seen from the road.

As you move around, notice the people here with you. Some come for quiet reflection. Some families come to walk, and children run along the paved paths. Photographers wait for the right light on the tiles. All these uses exist side by side. A memorial that people visit only out of duty slowly dies. A memorial that people also enjoy stays alive.

Before you leave, find a spot where you can see both halves and the gap between them. Watch how the light moves across the turquoise as clouds pass. Listen again to the water at the base of the platform. Two curved shells, set apart forever, with an open sky between them. Take that image with you as you walk back into the city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in late afternoon for softer light on the tiles.
Bring water and a hat for the exposed paving.
Walk the full circle to see the halves shift.
Dress modestly, as this is a place of mourning.
Ask about the underground galleries before you visit.
