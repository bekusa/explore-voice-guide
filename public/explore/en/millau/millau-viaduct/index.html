The guide

The story

Listen first. Down in the valley, the Tarn river runs over stones, and somewhere a bird calls across the slopes. Then, very high above you, comes a faint rushing sound — cars crossing a road in the sky. Look up. A thin white line runs from one plateau to the other, and seven slender towers rise to meet it. On some mornings, the valley fills with fog and the road seems to float on the cloud.

This is the Millau Viaduct, in the Aveyron region of southern France. It is a cable-stayed bridge. That means the road deck hangs from steel cables, and the cables fan out from tall masts above each pier. From a distance, the cables look like the sails of a ship. Up close, they look like the strings of an enormous harp. The bridge opened to traffic in December 2004, after about three years of construction.

Why build a bridge here at all? For decades, travellers between Paris and the Mediterranean coast had a problem at Millau. The A75 motorway came down from the north, but the deep Tarn valley cut it in two. Every summer, holiday traffic had to drop into the valley, crawl through the small town, and climb out again. The jams became famous across France. Drivers could lose hours here. The viaduct was the answer — a way to cross the valley without ever touching the town.

The numbers are hard to picture, so here are just a few. The bridge runs for about 2,460 metres, close to two and a half kilometres. At its highest point, the top of one mast stands around 343 metres above the ground. That makes it taller than the Eiffel Tower. By structural height, it is often described as the tallest bridge in the world. The tallest single pier alone rises roughly 245 metres from the valley floor.

Two minds shaped this structure. The French structural engineer Michel Virlogeux worked out how the bridge would stand and carry its load. The British architect Norman Foster and his firm gave it its final form. Their goal was a bridge that looks as light as possible. Notice how little of it there is. The deck is thin. The piers taper as they rise. The whole thing seems to be drawn in pencil against the sky.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look at the upper part of each pier. Near the top, the single concrete column splits into two thinner columns. This is not just for beauty. A steel deck this long grows and shrinks with heat and cold. The split columns can flex slightly, so the deck can move without stressing the piers. The design solves an engineering problem and makes a graceful shape at the same time.

The second surprise is the curve. The road is not perfectly straight, and it is not perfectly flat. It bends in a long, gentle arc, and it slopes slightly from north to south. The curve has a purpose. On a straight bridge, drivers might feel they are heading into empty sky. The arc lets them see the road ahead. It also gives anyone below, like you, this sweeping view of the whole line at once.

The way the deck was put in place is a story of its own. The builders did not lift the steel road up from the valley. Instead, they assembled sections on solid ground at each end. Then hydraulic machines pushed the deck out horizontally, a little at a time, across the tops of the piers. Temporary support towers helped carry it between them. The two halves crept toward each other over the valley, until they met in the middle, high above the river.

The builder was the French group Eiffage. There is a quiet link to history here. Eiffage counts among its roots the company founded by Gustave Eiffel, the engineer behind the Paris tower. More than a century after Eiffel built his iron viaducts across French valleys, his corporate descendants crossed this one in steel and concrete. Eiffel also left his mark on this region, so the connection feels close to home.

Now turn your eyes to the landscape itself. To the north and south sit the causses — high, dry limestone plateaus, cut by deep gorges. To the south lies the Causse du Larzac, open land of grass, stone, and sheep. Those sheep matter. Their milk becomes Roquefort cheese, aged in natural caves in the village of Roquefort-sur-Soulzon, not far from here. Millau itself was long known for leather and fine gloves, made from local lambskin.

The light changes the bridge through the day. In the early morning, the white piers catch a pale gold glow, and the valley may still be full of mist. At midday, the masts look almost silver against the blue. In the evening, the long shadows fall across the fields, and the cables turn warm. After dark, the structure is lit, and the line of the road becomes a band of light hanging over the black valley.

There are several ways to experience it. You can drive across, though from the deck the wind screens and the speed make it feel like an ordinary motorway. The real view is from outside. There is a rest area and visitor space near the northern end, with paths and viewpoints. Small roads in the valley run close to the base of the piers. Boat trips on the Tarn give another angle, looking straight up from the water.

Before you go, stand still for one more moment. Look at the nearest pier and follow it with your eyes, from the ground all the way up to the tip of its mast. Notice how the cables catch the light, and how quiet it is down here, compared to the traffic far above. As you walk away, glance back once or twice. From every new spot, the line of the bridge changes shape against the hills.

Practical tips

Return after dark to see the deck lit up.
