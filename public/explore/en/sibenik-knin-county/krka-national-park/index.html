The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, there is a low, steady roar ahead, softened by leaves. The air turns cool and damp on your skin. The wooden boards under your feet are slightly wet, and they flex a little with each step. Somewhere close, water is falling. It falls over stone, over moss, over roots, again and again. This is the River Krka, and the sound around you is the sound of a river that has spent thousands of years building its own staircase.

The Krka rises in the hills near the town of Knin, in the Dalmatian interior. From there it runs roughly seventy kilometres toward the Adriatic Sea, reaching the coast near Šibenik. Along the way it cuts a deep canyon through grey limestone. Much of the river's middle and lower course lies inside the national park, which Croatia declared in 1985. Today it is one of the country's most visited protected areas. In summer, the trails can be busy. But the river was here long before the crowds, and it still sets the pace.

Now look at the falls themselves. They are not made of hard bedrock in the way most waterfalls are. They are made of travertine — a soft, porous stone that the river builds as it flows. The water carries dissolved calcium carbonate, the same mineral found in chalk and limestone. Mosses and algae grow on the edges of the falls. As water splashes over them, the mineral settles onto the plants and slowly hardens. Layer by layer, the barriers grow. In other words, these waterfalls are partly alive.

Here is the first surprise. Because the barriers are built this way, they are fragile. A footprint, a broken branch, or a sunscreen film on the water can disturb the delicate growth. That is the main reason the walkways exist. They lift you above the travertine so that the river can keep working. It is also why swimming below the great falls is no longer allowed. For many years people swam here, and the old photographs are full of bathers. The park ended that practice to protect the stone.

The largest and best-known set of falls is Skradinski buk. In the local language, the word buk means a roaring cascade, and the name fits. This is not one waterfall but a long chain of them. Water spills over many steps and terraces, splits into channels, and gathers again in clear green pools. Small islands of travertine stand between the streams, covered with trees and grass. The scale is easy to miss from one viewpoint. Walk the loop and you see how wide the whole system really is.

As you walk, notice the colour of the water. In the deeper pools it turns a deep emerald green. In the shallows it looks almost like glass, and you can count the stones on the bottom. The clarity comes from the minerals and the low amount of floating sediment. Look also at the plants. Fig trees, ash, and willow grow right beside the falls. In the spray zones, the moss is thick and bright. On a hot Dalmatian afternoon, this is often the coolest place for many kilometres.

Now the second surprise, and it is about electricity. Near the bottom of Skradinski buk stand the remains of the Jaruga hydroelectric plant. It opened in 1895. It is often described as one of the first alternating-current power systems in the world, starting work only days after the famous plant at Niagara Falls. Its power was sent to nearby Šibenik, which soon had electric street lighting. That was very early for any city in Europe. A quiet Dalmatian river helped light the way into the modern age.

The river was useful long before electricity. For centuries, local families built watermills along the falls to grind grain. They also used the force of the water to beat and wash woollen cloth. Some of the old stone mill buildings still stand beside the paths. Several have been restored and now show how the mills worked, with millstones, wooden channels, and simple tools. Stop inside one if you can. You will hear the river even through the thick walls, and you will understand why people settled here.

The human story here is very old. The Romans knew this river by the name Titius. Upstream, near the village of Puljane, are the ruins of Burnum, a Roman military camp. Its stone arches and the remains of a small amphitheatre still stand on the plateau above the canyon. Soldiers once guarded the river crossing from that high ground. If you have time to travel further upstream, those arches give a very different picture of the Krka — dry, open, and wide.

Further upstream, the river widens into a calm lake, and in the middle sits a small island called Visovac. On it stands a Franciscan monastery and church, surrounded by cypress trees. Franciscan monks have lived on the island for centuries. The image of the pale stone church on the green water is one of the quietest scenes in the park. Boat trips run there in the main season. Even from the shore, the island shows how still the river becomes between its noisy falls.

There is also an Orthodox monastery in the park, known as the Krka Monastery. It stands in a remote part of the canyon, with a bell tower that rises above the trees. It belongs to the Serbian Orthodox Church and has been a religious centre for a long time. Together, the Catholic island monastery and the Orthodox monastery show how many communities have lived along this river. Further upstream again is Roški slap, another group of falls, with a long line of small cascades that locals sometimes compare to a necklace.

The park is also a home for wildlife. The canyon and the wetlands support a long list of bird species, from herons in the reeds to birds of prey above the cliffs. The river holds fish found only in this part of the Adriatic basin. In the warm months, look for dragonflies over the pools and lizards on the warm stones by the path. Bats roost in the caves and old buildings. If you stand still for a minute, the life of the river becomes easier to see.

Many visitors arrive by boat from the small town of Skradin, at the river's lower end. The ride runs up a long, calm stretch of water between steep green slopes. Here the river meets the sea, and fresh water mixes with salt. That is one more change in a short distance. In less than an hour, you can travel from salt water and sailing boats to forest, mills, and falling water. The river keeps changing its character, and that is part of its appeal.

Before you leave, stand on the walkway for one more moment. Close your eyes and listen to the falls again. That roar is the same sound the millers heard, and the Roman soldiers, and the engineers in 1895. And right now, under the spray, the river is still adding a very thin new layer of stone. It is too slow to see. But as you walk away, remember that the waterfall you are looking at is still being built.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early in summer to walk the trails before crowds.
Stay on the wooden walkways to protect the travertine.
Take the boat from Skradin for a scenic approach.
Wear shoes with good grip for damp boardwalks.
Bring water and sun protection for the open sections.
