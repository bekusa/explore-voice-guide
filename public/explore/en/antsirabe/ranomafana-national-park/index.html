The guide

The story

Listen first. Water drips from broad leaves. Somewhere below, a river rushes over rock. The air is warm, wet, and heavy with the smell of moss and soil. A bird calls once, then stops. The path under your feet is soft, often muddy, and threaded with roots. Mist sits in the valleys and slowly lifts as the morning warms. This is Ranomafana National Park, a steep green fold of rainforest on the eastern side of Madagascar's central highlands. You are standing in one of the most closely studied forests on the island.

If you travelled here from Antsirabe, you came a long way south. The journey runs several hours along the national highway, through the woodcarving town of Ambositra and past terraced rice fields. Then the road leaves the open highlands and drops toward the east. The red, cleared hills give way to dense forest. The temperature changes. The air grows damp. That descent is part of the story. Ranomafana sits on the edge where Madagascar's high plateau falls toward the Indian Ocean coast, and rain from the sea feeds everything you see.

The name itself is a clue. In Malagasy, the language of Madagascar, Ranomafana means hot water. The name comes from the thermal springs in the small village near the park entrance. People have bathed in these warm waters for generations. In the colonial era, the springs drew visitors from the highland towns. Today the village is the gateway for travellers, guides, and scientists. Its small hotels, food stalls, and guide offices line the road beside the Namorona River, which runs down through the gorge.

Now for the first surprise. This park exists largely because of a single small lemur. In 1986, researchers working in this forest found a lemur that science had never described. It was later named the golden bamboo lemur. The American primatologist Patricia Wright was part of that work. She and Malagasy colleagues then pushed to protect the forest from logging. Their effort helped create the national park, which opened in 1991. A small animal that eats bamboo became the reason a whole landscape was saved.

And that diet is the second surprise. Young bamboo shoots contain cyanide, a strong poison. The golden bamboo lemur eats large amounts of these shoots every day. Scientists estimate that its daily intake would be enough to harm an animal of much larger size. Exactly how the lemur survives this is still not fully understood. So if your guide stops near a stand of giant bamboo and points upward, look carefully. A small, reddish-brown face may be chewing on something that would make you very ill.

The golden bamboo lemur is only one of many. Around a dozen lemur species live in this forest, and your guide will know where each family group has been feeding lately. You may see the greater bamboo lemur, a rare species that researchers once feared was lost. You may see red-bellied lemurs moving quietly in pairs. And you may see Milne-Edwards' sifaka, a large dark lemur that leaps from trunk to trunk with its body upright. Lemurs live naturally only in Madagascar. Nowhere else on Earth has them in the wild.

Take a moment to look at the forest itself. This is montane rainforest, meaning rainforest on mountain slopes. The trees here are not as tall as lowland jungle giants, but the layers are dense. Tree ferns spread their crowns like green umbrellas. Moss and lichens cover branches. Orchids grow on bark. Strangler figs wrap around older trunks. Because the park climbs and falls across steep ridges, the plants change as you move uphill. A short walk can take you through several different kinds of forest.

Ranomafana is also part of a larger protected group. In 2007, UNESCO listed the Rainforests of the Atsinanana as a World Heritage Site. That listing joins several eastern Malagasy parks, and Ranomafana is one of them. UNESCO is the United Nations body that recognises places of global importance. Sadly, the same rainforests were later placed on its list of sites in danger, because of illegal logging and other pressures. The forest around you is protected, but it is not safe by default. It needs constant care.

Much of that care comes from research. Near the park stands Centre ValBio, a research station founded with Patricia Wright's involvement. Scientists from Madagascar and many other countries study lemurs, frogs, insects, and plants here. The station also works with surrounding villages on health, education, and farming projects. This matters, because people live all around the park and depend on the land. Protecting the forest only works if the families beside it also have a future. Many of the local guides grew up in these villages.

Now look smaller. Some of the best things in Ranomafana are not lemurs at all. Chameleons sit motionless on twigs, changing tone slowly. Leaf-tailed geckos press flat against bark, and their skin matches it so well that you can stare straight at one and miss it. Frogs call from streams after rain. Many of these frog species exist only in this region. A good guide will find a gecko that you walked past twice. That moment of sudden recognition is one of the quiet pleasures of this place.

After dark, the forest changes completely. Many visitors join a night walk, often along the edge of the park near the road. With a torch, you may see the bright reflected eyes of mouse lemurs. These are among the smallest primates in the world, small enough to sit in your palm. You may also find sleeping chameleons, pale against the dark leaves. The sounds shift too. Insects buzz, frogs call, and something moves in the branches overhead. Keep your light low and your voice quiet.

The trails here are steep. Some paths climb hard ridges, and steps cut into the slope can be slippery after rain. Rain comes often, and in the wetter months leeches wait on the ground and on low leaves. None of this is dangerous, but it shapes the day. Good shoes, long socks, and patience help a great deal. Lemur sightings are never guaranteed. Animals move. Sometimes your group will wait in silence for a long time, and then suddenly the canopy shakes above you.

So pause for a moment before you go. Look up at the light coming through the leaves, green and broken. Listen for the river below and the drip of water from the branches. Somewhere near you, a small animal may be watching from the canopy, perfectly still. As you walk back toward the village and its warm springs, notice the edge where forest meets field. That line is the whole story of Ranomafana — a forest kept alive because people decided it was worth keeping.

Before you go

Practical tips

Hire a licensed local guide at the park entrance.
Wear waterproof shoes with good grip for steep trails.
Tuck trousers into long socks to keep leeches off.
Start early, when lemurs are most active and feeding.
Book a night walk to look for mouse lemurs.
