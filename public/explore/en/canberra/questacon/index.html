The story

Reach out and touch the exhibit in front of you. Press the button. Turn the wheel. Feel the floor tremble under your feet. At Questacon, no one asks you to stand back behind a rope. Instead, you are asked to take part. This is a place where science is not just shown to you. It is something you do with your own hands.

Questacon is the National Science and Technology Centre. It stands in Parkes, on the shore of Lake Burley Griffin, close to the centre of Canberra. The building is easy to recognise by its shape. Round drums of different sizes sit together beneath the open sky. Inside, bright galleries circle a tall central space that draws the eye upward.

The current building opened in 1988. It was a gift to mark two hundred years since the first European settlement of Australia. Japan helped pay for its construction, as a sign of friendship between the two countries. The idea itself, though, had begun earlier and on a much smaller scale, back in 1980, with only a handful of simple exhibits.

Step inside and look up. Daylight falls from above into the central drum. All around you, galleries rise floor by floor. Each one is given over to a different field of science. There is no single route marked out for you. You are free to wander as you please and follow whatever happens to catch your eye.

Almost every exhibit invites you to act. You can build a small bridge and test whether it holds. You can make a wave travel, bend a beam of light, or watch a ball roll along a track that defies what you expect. Children race ahead from one station to the next. The adults with them, more often than not, are close behind.

The aim of all this is simple but deep. Science can feel distant and cold when it lives only inside books. Here it is placed within easy reach. When you make something happen with your own hands, you tend to remember it. A fact that you only read can quickly fade. A thing that you actually did is far more likely to stay.

One exhibit draws a steady line of waiting people. It is a tall slide that drops almost straight down. You climb to the very top, sit at the edge, and let yourself go. For a brief moment you are in free fall. Then the curve of the slide catches you and carries you out. It is a lesson in gravity that your whole body learns at once.

Other areas explore the great forces of nature. You can feel how an earthquake shakes the ground beneath you. You can watch how lightning leaps and cracks through the air. These shows take forces that are large and frightening in the real world and turn them into something you can study, in safety, and slowly begin to understand.

Staff in bright coats move through the halls to help. They run live demonstrations and answer the questions that visitors call out. Many of them are young and clearly full of energy for their subject. They do not stand and lecture. Instead they spark a question in your mind, then step back and let you chase the answer down yourself.

Questacon was built upon a clear belief. The future of any country depends on people who understand science and are not afraid of it. Curiosity, in this view, is not a luxury for the few. It is a skill worth growing in the young. A place like this plants that seed early, through play and delight rather than through pressure.

Find a quiet spot on an upper level and look down for a moment. Watch the crowd moving below you. See the faces light up with surprise each time something works as it should, or fails in a way no one expected. That look, repeated all day long, is the true exhibit here. It is exactly what the whole building was made to produce.

It is worth noticing how the ages mix in this place. A grandparent and a small child can stand at the same machine, equally puzzled and equally pleased. Science here belongs to no single group. The youngest visitor and the oldest one meet as fellow beginners, and that shared sense of wonder is part of the point.

Before you leave, find one machine that you do not understand at all. Press its button. Watch closely what it does. Let yourself simply wonder how and why it works. That small, honest spark of wonder is the thing you carry back out through the doors, and it lasts long after the day itself is done.
