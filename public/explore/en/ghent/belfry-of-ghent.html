The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Somewhere above you, bells are working through a melody, note by note, and the sound drops down onto the paving stones like rain. Tilt your head back. A grey stone tower rises over the old city, and at the very top a golden dragon catches the light. You are standing at the civic heart of medieval Ghent. This tower was not built for prayer. It was built to watch, to warn, and to hold the city's pride.

The Belfry of Ghent stands between two church towers. To one side is Saint Bavo's Cathedral. To the other is Saint Nicholas' Church. Locals often speak of the three towers together, a row of spires that has shaped the skyline for centuries. But look at the difference. The two churches belong to faith. The belfry belongs to the citizens. In the Middle Ages, that difference mattered very much. It was a statement of independence in stone.

Work on the tower began in the early fourteenth century. Ghent was then one of the largest and richest cities in northern Europe. Its wealth came from cloth. Flemish weavers turned English wool into fine fabric, and merchants sold it across the continent. With that money came confidence. The city's guilds and its leaders wanted a symbol of their own power. A belfry gave them one. It said clearly that this city governed itself and answered to its own rules.

Here is something many visitors miss. The tower was also a safe. Inside, the city kept its most precious documents, the charters and privileges that rulers had granted to Ghent over the years. These papers set out the rights of the citizens. They were locked away in a strong chest, high above the street and well guarded. Losing them could mean losing freedom. So the belfry protected both the bodies and the legal rights of the people below.

The tower also had a living guard. Watchmen climbed to the upper galleries and scanned the rooftops day and night. Their main enemy was fire. In a city of wooden beams and crowded streets, a single spark could destroy whole districts. If the watchmen saw smoke, or an approaching army, they rang the bells. The sound carried across the city and into the fields beyond. Everyone knew what it meant. The tower was the city's early warning system.

The most famous of those bells was called Roland. It was the great alarm bell of Ghent, and it became a symbol of the city's defiance. An old inscription linked to the bell says, in short, that it tolled for fire and rang for victory. Rulers who wanted to punish Ghent understood its meaning. After the city rebelled against the Emperor Charles the Fifth in the sixteenth century, the bell was taken down as a punishment. Silencing the bell meant silencing the city.

A later bell carried on the tradition. It was cast in the seventeenth century and became known as the Triumphant. For centuries it hung in the tower. Then, in the early twentieth century, it cracked. Today it rests at ground level beside the belfry, where you can walk up close to it. Look at its size and the thickness of its bronze. Then imagine it ringing above you. It is one of the few chances to stand next to a great bell instead of hearing it from far away.

Now look up again at the dragon. It serves as a weathervane, turning with the wind above the city. The figure has watched over Ghent for centuries, and it is a guardian in local imagination. Legend has it that the dragon came from far away. One popular tale says that crusaders brought it back from the East. Another story says that Ghent took it from its rival, Bruges. Historians treat these stories with caution, but locals enjoy telling them.

The dragon you see on top is not the oldest one. Over time, older versions were taken down and replaced. One earlier dragon is kept inside the tower, so visitors who go up can meet it face to face. Seeing it closely changes your sense of scale. From the street it looks small and delicate. Up close, it is a large, heavy piece of craftsmanship. It is a reminder that everything on this tower was built to be seen from far below.

The top of the tower has changed too. The belfry took a long time to reach its full height, and its crown was rebuilt more than once. The stone spire you see today dates from the early twentieth century. It was completed around the time Ghent hosted a world's fair, when the city wanted to present its medieval past proudly to visitors. So the silhouette is old in spirit, but newer in parts. Like many great European monuments, the tower is a mix of eras.

At the foot of the tower is the Cloth Hall. This long Gothic building was where cloth was inspected and traded. It connects the story of the tower directly to the money that paid for it. Look also for a small building attached to the side. It once served as a jail, and its entrance carries a carved scene of a daughter feeding her imprisoned father. The story comes from ancient Rome, and it was a symbol of charity. Locals know this building by the name Mammelokker.

If you go inside, the climb takes you past the different levels of the tower's life. You will see the rooms where watchmen worked and the space where the bells hang. The carillon is a set of tuned bells played from a keyboard, and it can play full tunes. If you are lucky, you may hear a carillonneur at work. At the top, the galleries open onto views across the red roofs, the canals, and the church towers. In 1999, the tower was listed by UNESCO, along with other belfries in Belgium and France.

Before you walk on, take one last look. The stone is grey and worn, and the dragon still turns in the wind. Around you, people cross the square on bicycles, sit at café tables, and stop to take photographs. Few of them think about fires, rebellions, or locked charters. Yet the tower still keeps time for the city. Listen for the bells as you leave. When they ring again, you will know what this tower once meant to the people below.
