The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Water moves slowly against old stone, and somewhere a gull calls over the rooftops. In front of you rises a wall of grey stone, dark and patched, with small turrets hanging from its upper edge. This is the Gravensteen, the Castle of the Counts. It does not sit on a hill or a cliff. It sits right in the middle of a working city, beside a narrow river, with trams and bicycles passing almost at its feet.

That contrast is the first thing worth noticing. Most visitors expect a castle to stand apart from the town it controls. Here, Ghent grew right up to the walls. The castle was meant to protect the Count of Flanders, but it was also a message to the people around it. In the Middle Ages, Ghent was one of the largest and richest cities north of the Alps. Its wealth came from cloth, and its citizens were famously hard to govern.

The stone castle you see today dates from 1180. It was built by Philip of Alsace, Count of Flanders. Look up above the main gate and you may find an inscription that records his name and that date. Philip had travelled to the Holy Land on crusade. Many historians believe that the castles he saw there shaped this design. The tall central keep and the ring of walls follow a pattern that crusader builders used in the eastern Mediterranean.

But Philip did not start from nothing. An earlier fortification stood on this spot long before him, built mostly of wood and earth. Over the centuries the site was rebuilt again and again. So when you look at these walls, you are looking at layers. Some parts are original twelfth-century work. Other parts are later additions. And a good share is the result of a much more recent rescue, which is one of the surprises of this place.

Before we come to that rescue, think about the water. The small river that runs beside the castle is the Lieve. Its water once fed the moat around the walls. A moat was not only a barrier for attackers. It also kept people at a distance and controlled who could enter. Today the water looks calm and almost decorative. In the Middle Ages, it was part of a serious defensive system, and the only way in was across a guarded gate.

Now look at the top of the walls. You can see small round turrets that stick out from the corners and the edges. These allowed defenders to look straight down at the base of the wall. You can also see openings where archers could shoot while staying protected. Notice how few large windows there are. Comfort was not the priority here. Control was. Even so, the counts did not live here forever. Over time, they moved to more comfortable residences elsewhere in the city.

When the counts left, the castle did not stand empty. It took on new jobs. For a long period it served as a seat of justice. Courts met here, and prisoners were held inside the walls. Coins were also struck here for a time, so the building worked as a mint. The square in front of the castle, Saint Veerle Square, was a place of public punishment. Ghent's citizens watched sentences being carried out in the open, in the shadow of the counts' old fortress.

That history of justice is still part of the visit. Inside, one section displays instruments that were used for punishment and interrogation in earlier centuries. Some visitors find it fascinating. Others find it hard to look at. It is worth knowing in advance if you are visiting with young children. The displays are a reminder that this building was not only about knights and banners. For many people, it was a place of fear.

Here is the surprise most visitors miss. By the nineteenth century, the castle was almost unrecognisable. Industrial Ghent had become a centre of textile production, especially cotton. The old fortress was turned into a factory, and small houses were built against and inside the walls. The keep was hidden behind newer buildings. For a time there was serious talk of tearing the whole thing down. The Gravensteen came very close to disappearing from the city map.

Instead, the city of Ghent bought the site in the late nineteenth century. Workers cleared away the factory and the houses. Then came a long restoration. It ran into the early twentieth century, around the time Ghent hosted a world exhibition in 1913. So the castle you see is partly a medieval building and partly a careful reconstruction of what restorers believed it looked like. Some historians question certain details. That debate is part of the story too.

The castle has one more episode that makes locals smile. In 1949, a group of students from Ghent University occupied the Gravensteen. Their protest was about a rise in the price of beer. They held the castle for a while and threw vegetables and other harmless objects down at the police and firefighters below. Eventually the occupation ended and the students were removed. People in Ghent still remember it with affection. It shows the same independent spirit that made the medieval counts build such thick walls.

If you go inside, take your time climbing to the top. The stairs are narrow and uneven, and the steps are worn by many feet. From the roof, the view opens across the old town. You can see towers and church spires rising above the tiled roofs. You can follow the line of the rivers and canals that once carried cloth, grain, and wool. From up there, it becomes clear how the castle sat at the meeting point of the city's routes.

When you come back down, walk to the edge of the water and look at the walls again. Notice the different shades of stone. Some blocks are darker and rougher. Others are cleaner and more even. You are seeing both the twelfth century and the restoration work side by side. Then turn around and look at the cafés, the bicycles, and the people crossing the bridges. The castle was built to stand apart from them. Today it belongs to them... and to you, for as long as you stay.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb to the roof for views over Ghent's old town.
Wear sturdy shoes for the narrow, worn stone stairs.
Warn children before the punishment instruments display.
Walk around the outside along the water before entering.
Visit early in the day to climb the stairs with fewer crowds.
