The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry teak leaf, as wide as a dinner plate, cracks under a tyre. Somewhere ahead, a langur barks a sharp, hoarse cough from the canopy. Then a spotted deer stamps its hoof and the whole forest goes quiet. In Pench, sound tells you what is happening before your eyes catch up. The air smells of dust, warm bark and crushed grass. Welcome to the teak forests of the Satpura hills, in the heart of central India.

Pench National Park lies in the state of Madhya Pradesh, stretching across Seoni and Chhindwara districts. It takes its name from the Pench River, which runs from north to south through the middle of the park. The river divides the forest into two roughly equal halves, eastern and western. Across the state border, in Maharashtra, a separate reserve also carries the Pench name. Together they protect one continuous block of wild country, joined by the same water and the same animals.

Here is the first surprise. If you grew up with The Jungle Book, you already know this landscape. Rudyard Kipling set Mowgli's adventures in the Seonee hills, the old spelling of Seoni. The wolf pack, Bagheera the black panther, Baloo the bear and the tiger Shere Khan all belong to this country. Yet Kipling, according to most accounts, never travelled here himself. He drew on the writings of British naturalists and officials who had walked these hills in the nineteenth century.

One of those writers was Robert Armitage Sterndale, whose book on camp life in the Satpura range described Seoni in close detail. Through such accounts, Kipling built a jungle he knew only on paper. So when you look at the low ridges, the open meadows and the river pools, you see the real place behind the stories. The names changed, and the tone became a fable. But the forest in those pages is recognisably this one.

The area became a wildlife sanctuary in the 1970s and a national park in the early 1980s. Its full official name honours Indira Gandhi, a former Prime Minister of India. In 1992, Pench was declared a tiger reserve under Project Tiger, India's national programme to save the tiger from extinction. That programme gave the forest a protected core, where people do not live, and a surrounding buffer zone, where villages and the forest share the same ground.

Now look at the trees. Teak dominates much of the park, tall and straight, with huge leaves that turn brown and fall in the hot months. Mixed among them you will find mahua, a tree whose sweet flowers villagers collect for food and drink. There are also pale, ghostly trunks that seem almost white in the morning light. In the dry season, the forest opens up. Leaves drop, grass thins, and animals become easier to see.

The open meadows are the second key to Pench. Many of them grew where old villages once stood, before families were moved out of the core. Today they are grazing grounds for herds of chital, the spotted deer. Chital often feed close to langur monkeys. The langurs watch from the treetops, and the deer listen below. When either one spots a predator, it sends out an alarm call. Guides read these calls the way you might read a road sign.

And yes, the predators are here. Pench supports tigers and leopards, as well as dholes, the Indian wild dog. Dholes hunt in packs and whistle to each other as they move. You may also see sloth bears, jackals and the gaur, the Indian bison, which is a massive wild ox with white lower legs that look like socks. Sambar, India's largest deer, stand in the shallow water near the river. Nilgai, the blue bull, graze in the open.

One tigress made this park known far beyond India. Local guides called her Collarwali, because she wore a radio collar for research. Over many years she raised an unusually large number of cubs, and she became a symbol of the reserve. She died in 2022, and people across the region marked her passing. Many tigers living in and around Pench today descend from her. Ask your guide about her. Almost every one of them has a story.

The second surprise is that Pench is a famous film location. A well-known BBC wildlife series about tigers was filmed here, using hidden cameras disguised as logs, rocks and even elephants. Those cameras recorded tiger families at very close range. Much of what television audiences around the world learned about wild tiger behaviour came from these same forest roads. The film crews chose Pench because the animals here are used to vehicles and often stay calm.

Birds give the park its daily soundtrack. More than two hundred species have been recorded. Peacocks call loudly at dawn and dusk, and you may see a male open his tail in a clearing. Look for the Indian roller, a dull bird at rest that flashes bright blue when it flies. Along the river and the reservoir, watch for kingfishers, storks and fish eagles. In winter, migrating waterbirds arrive and settle on the open water.

That reservoir comes from a dam built across the Pench River near the Maharashtra border. The water spreads into wide, still channels inside the park. In the dry months, the level drops and leaves muddy flats and green grass along the edges. These banks attract deer, wild boar and the animals that hunt them. Visitors usually enter through one of several gates, such as Turia, Karmajhiri or Jamtara, each with its own landscape and its own regular wildlife.

A safari here is a slow business. You sit in an open vehicle with a trained guide and a driver, and you follow sandy tracks between the trees. Many hours pass with deer, birds and monkeys, but no tiger. That is normal. The best moments often come from small things: a pugmark pressed into the dust, a fresh claw scratch on a tree, a sudden silence. Patience is part of the experience, and so is respect for the animals' space.

So take a moment now. Notice the light coming through the teak leaves, the red dust on your shoes, the smell of dry grass. Listen for the langur's cough or the deer's sharp call. This is the forest that Kipling imagined from far away. You are standing inside it. And as you leave, keep listening. Somewhere behind you, the jungle is still talking, whether or not anyone is there to hear it.
