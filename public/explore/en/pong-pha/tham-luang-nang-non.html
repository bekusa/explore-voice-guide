The guide

The story

Cool air reaches you before the cave does. It drifts down the slope, damp and heavy, and it smells of wet limestone and earth. Around you the forest is loud with insects. Above you, a long green ridge runs along the border country of northern Thailand. This is Tham Luang Nang Non, the Great Cave of the Sleeping Lady, in the hills of Pong Pha in Chiang Rai Province.

Before the history, look up at the mountain itself. Many people say the ridgeline has the shape of a woman lying on her back, with her head, her chest, and her long hair stretched across the sky. Locals call the range Doi Nang Non, the Mountain of the Sleeping Lady. From the right angle on the road in, the profile is easy to see. The cave sits deep inside her body.

The name comes from a legend, and it is a legend, not recorded history. According to the popular tale, a princess fell in love with a common man, and she became pregnant. Her father did not approve, and his soldiers killed her lover. In her grief, the princess took her own life. Locals say her blood became the water that flows through these hills. Her body became the mountain you are looking at now.

That story matters here more than you might expect. Water is the real character in this place. The cave is a long limestone system, and it runs for roughly ten kilometres under the ridge. Limestone dissolves slowly in rainwater. Over a very long time, that water carved passages, chambers, and narrow squeezes. In the dry months, you can walk into parts of it. In the rainy season, the passages can fill with water fast.

That is exactly what happened on 23 June 2018. Twelve boys from a local youth football team, the Wild Boars, came here with their assistant coach, Ekkapol Chanthawong. The boys were between eleven and sixteen years old. They rode bicycles to the park, left them near the entrance, and went inside. While they were underground, heavy rain began. The water rose behind them and cut off the way out.

The bicycles were one of the first clues. Families came looking that evening, and they found the bikes and shoes near the mouth of the cave. Within days, this quiet forest park filled with soldiers, police, volunteers, journalists, and engineers. Thai Navy divers arrived. Cave divers came from Britain, Australia, the United States, China, and many other countries. Thousands of people worked here in the mud and the rain.

For nine days, no one knew if the boys were alive. Then, on 2 July, two British cave divers, Rick Stanton and John Volanthen, surfaced in a flooded passage several kilometres inside. Their torches found thirteen thin faces on a muddy ledge above the water. All of them were alive. The coach had kept them calm and still, and the group had survived by drinking the water that dripped from the rock.

Finding them was only the first problem. The way out was long, dark, and partly underwater, with currents and very narrow gaps. Most of the boys could not swim well, and none of them were divers. Oxygen in the chamber was falling. The monsoon was coming. The teams had to choose between waiting months for the water to drop, or attempting a dive that no one had done before.

The cost of the operation became clear on 6 July. Saman Kunan, a former Thai Navy diver who had volunteered to help, died underwater while he was placing air tanks along the route. He is the reason many visitors come here today. Near the entrance you will find a statue and memorial in his honour. Thai visitors often stop there, bow, and leave flowers or small offerings.

The rescue took place over three days, from 8 to 10 July. An Australian anaesthetist and cave diver, Richard Harris, helped to sedate each boy so that he would not panic in the dark water. Divers then guided and carried them out, one at a time, through the flooded sections. In the drier parts, teams passed them hand to hand along ropes. All twelve boys and their coach came out alive.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. To lower the water inside, pumps ran day and night, and they pushed huge volumes out of the cave. That water had to go somewhere. It flowed onto the rice fields in the valley below. Local farmers accepted the flooding of their crops so that the rescue could continue. Many of them asked for nothing in return.

Another surprise is how little of the cave you will actually see. The ledge where the boys waited is far beyond any public route, and it is only reachable by expert divers. Visitors usually walk only into the first chambers, if the cave is open at all. Access depends on the season and on park rules, which change. The real drama happened kilometres away, inside the rock beneath your feet.

Since the rescue, this place has changed. The cave reopened to visitors, and the forest park now has walkways, information boards, and small shops near the entrance. You may hear Thai families talking quietly, or see school groups. Some people come because of the films and news stories. Many local people come for a simpler reason. They come to remember, and to give thanks.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the entrance. Feel that cool air on your skin again. Listen to the water and the forest. Then look back up at the long ridge of the Sleeping Lady against the sky. Inside her, the water is still moving through the stone, as it has for thousands of years, and as it will long after we walk away.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in the dry season for the best chance of cave access.
Wear shoes with good grip for wet, muddy paths.
Check current park rules locally before planning a cave visit.
Pause quietly at the memorial, as many Thai visitors do.
View the mountain profile from the approach road for the clearest outline.
