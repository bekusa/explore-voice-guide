The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Out here in Prince George's County, the air carries pine and cut grass, and on a warm day the asphalt gives off a faint, tarry heat. For decades this ground was filled with other sounds: the rattle of a chain lift, the clack of wooden track, and the rising, collective scream of strangers who had briefly become friends. Today those sounds have gone quiet, and that silence is part of the story.

Six Flags America closed for good at the end of the 2025 season. The company announced the decision that spring, after Six Flags merged with Cedar Fair, another large American park operator. The new company reviewed its portfolio and decided this Maryland park no longer fit its plans. So if you are standing near the gates now, you are looking at the end of a long chapter. It is a chapter that began with something far stranger than roller coasters.

The story here did not start with steel and speed. It started with animals. In the 1970s, this land opened as a drive-through wildlife preserve. Families rolled slowly along the roads with their windows up, while exotic animals wandered past the cars. It was a popular idea across America at the time, a kind of safari for the suburbs. Picture station wagons, children pressed against the glass, and a giraffe's long neck appearing over the trees of suburban Maryland.

In the early 1980s, the park changed its character and its name. It became Wild World, and rides and a water park began to take the place of the animals. Then came another name, Adventure World, as the park grew through the late 1980s and the 1990s. Each new name marked a new owner or a new ambition. The land stayed the same, but its personality kept shifting, a little like a teenager trying on new identities.

The most remarkable thing on this site may be its oldest attraction. Wild One is a wooden roller coaster that was first built in 1917. It did not begin life in Maryland. It stood at Paragon Park, a seaside amusement park in Massachusetts, near Boston. When that park closed, the coaster was taken apart and carried south, then rebuilt here in the 1980s. So a ride from the First World War era kept carrying passengers into the twenty-first century. Very few visitors realised they were riding a piece of early American amusement history.

Think about what that means for a moment. The timber frame of a wooden coaster needs constant care. Boards are replaced, bolts are tightened, and the track is checked again and again. Over a century, much of the original wood was certainly renewed. Yet the layout, the drops, and the rhythm of the ride came down from 1917. Riding Wild One was a little like hearing an old song played by a new band. The notes were the same, even if the instruments had changed.

In 1999, the park took the Six Flags name, and the company brought a new kind of scale. The following year, a giant steel coaster called Superman: Ride of Steel opened. It was a hypercoaster, the industry term for a coaster with a very tall first drop, usually above two hundred feet. Its first hill rose high over the treetops. On a clear day, riders at the top could see far across the flat, green countryside of southern Maryland before the long plunge.

Alongside the dry rides, the park ran a large water park, known in its later years as Hurricane Harbor. In summer, this was where much of the crowd ended up. Families from Washington, Baltimore, and the surrounding suburbs came to escape the heat. Summers in this region are humid and heavy, the kind of weather that makes a wave pool feel like a gift. For many local children, a day here was the high point of the school holidays.

That local connection is worth remembering. This was never a destination park in the way that the big Florida resorts are. It was a regional park, a place you reached by car within an hour or two. People came for birthday parties, for school trips, and for first dates. Teenagers took summer jobs here, running rides and selling funnel cakes. For a whole generation in the Washington area, this was simply where you went for a thrill.

The park also changed with the seasons. In autumn, it hosted Halloween events, with fog machines, costumed performers, and themed haunted areas after dark. The same paths that felt cheerful in July felt eerie in October. Many regional parks in America came to depend on these autumn nights, because they brought in older crowds after the summer rush had ended. Here, too, the falling leaves and early darkness became part of the experience.

So why did it close? The full business reasoning belongs to the company, but the general picture is clear enough. After the merger, the combined company owned many parks and chose to focus its money on the ones it saw as strongest. A regional park on valuable land near a growing metropolitan area can be worth more for other uses. What will happen to this site in the future is not settled in anything we can confirm, so it is best to watch the local news.

If you are here now, take a careful look at what remains visible from public areas. Ride structures can stand for a long time after the last train runs. A tall steel hill or a wooden lattice against the sky can remain a landmark even when no one rides it. Please respect any fences and signs. A closed park is private property, and old ride areas can be dangerous to enter. The best view is from the edge, not from inside.

It is worth noticing how the landscape still shows the park's long history. The roads were laid out first for cars and animals, then adapted for crowds on foot. Trees planted or spared decades ago now frame the old structures. In a quiet moment, you can almost read the layers: the safari, Wild World, Adventure World, and finally the Six Flags years. Each owner built on top of the one before.

Before you leave, pause and listen once more. Perhaps you hear traffic on a nearby road, birds in the trees, or the wind moving across open ground. Somewhere in that quiet are half a century of summer afternoons. People shared laughter, sunburn, and nervous jokes in the queue here. As you walk back to your car, look up at the skyline one last time. These silent shapes once moved fast and carried a lot of joy.
