The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the birdsong there is the soft rush of water moving fast and cold. Somewhere close, a peacock calls. It is a sharp, almost human cry that carries across the stone courtyard. The air near the springs feels cooler than the air in town. It carries the faint mineral smell of water that has travelled a long way underground. Before you is a small church with a red-tiled roof, and behind it, the wide blue surface of Lake Ohrid.

If you came from Pogradec, you crossed a border to stand here. The Monastery of Saint Naum sits on the southern shore of Lake Ohrid, just inside North Macedonia. The Albanian frontier is only a few kilometres away. For many visitors based in Pogradec, this is the easiest trip abroad they will ever take. It is a short drive along the lakeshore, a passport check, and then the monastery gates. The two countries share this lake, and they share its stories too.

The monastery carries the name of its founder. Naum was a monk and scholar of the late ninth and early tenth centuries. He was a disciple of the brothers Cyril and Methodius. They were the missionaries who created an alphabet for the Slavic peoples, so that scripture could be read in their own language. After their mission in Central Europe collapsed, their followers scattered. Naum and his companion Clement eventually came to this region and helped build a centre of learning around Ohrid.

That centre is often called the Ohrid Literary School. Its scholars copied texts, trained priests, and spread literacy in the Slavic tongue. This work shaped the written culture of much of Eastern Europe. When you think of Cyrillic letters on a sign in Sofia, Belgrade, or Moscow, part of that story runs back to this lakeshore. Naum founded a monastery here around the year 900. Tradition says he spent his last years here, and he was buried within its walls.

The church you see today is not the original building. The first church was dedicated to the Holy Archangels. Over the centuries it was damaged, rebuilt, and changed. The present structure largely dates from the Ottoman period, roughly the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Look at the walls. Bands of stone are laid between rows of thin red brick. This is a typical Byzantine way of building. It gives the facade a striped, almost woven texture that catches the light differently through the day.

Step inside, and your eyes need a moment to adjust. The interior is dim and close, and nearly every surface is painted. The frescoes you see mostly come from later restoration periods, including work from the nineteenth century. Saints look down from the walls with large, steady eyes. Some panels show scenes from the life of Saint Naum himself. Look for the miracles and healings that made him one of the most loved saints in this region.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. Look for a small side chapel that holds the tomb of Saint Naum. Locals say that if you press your ear against the stone, you can still hear the saint's heartbeat. It is a legend, of course. But you will often see people kneeling there in silence, leaning close and listening. Whatever you believe, it is a moving thing to watch. Naum was long remembered as a healer, and pilgrims have come here for centuries seeking help.

Notice also the wooden iconostasis. This is the carved screen that separates the altar from the main space of the church. In Orthodox churches it holds rows of icons. Here, the woodwork is dense with vines, leaves, and small figures, cut by skilled local carvers. Stand close and let your eye follow one tendril from the bottom to the top. The craftsmen of this region were known across the Balkans for exactly this kind of patient, deep carving.

Now for one of the region's best-known tales. According to a popular legend, Naum once harnessed a bear to his plough. The story goes that a bear killed the ox Naum used to work his fields. So the saint tamed the bear and made it pull the plough instead. You may find this scene painted inside the church. It is a gentle, almost humorous image. It shows how local people imagined their saint. He was holy, but he was also practical.

Walk back outside toward the water. The springs beside the monastery are the second surprise. Much of the water feeding Lake Ohrid rises right here. Many scientists believe this water comes from Lake Prespa, which sits higher up on the other side of the mountains. It is thought to flow through hidden channels in the limestone and appear again in these clear pools. The water is so clear that you can see weed swaying and the pale riverbed beneath it.

Small wooden boats wait near the springs. Boatmen row visitors slowly through the reeds and over the clear pools. On a calm day, the only sounds are the oars, the water, and the birds. Some boats stop at a small wooden chapel on the water's edge. From the lake, you also get the best view of the monastery itself. You can see how it sits on its rocky rise above the shore. It looks both sheltered and watchful.

The border history of this place is unusual too. After the First World War, both Albania and the new Yugoslav state claimed this corner of the lake. After a long diplomatic dispute in the 1920s, the monastery and its land were given to Yugoslavia. That is why you stand in North Macedonia today, so close to Albanian soil. Yet people from both shores still come here to light candles. Faith and family ties have never followed the borderline exactly.

Before you leave, pause in the courtyard once more. Watch the peacocks drag their long tails across the stones, or watch one open its fan near the church wall. Feel the cool breath of the springs on your skin. Look out across the lake toward the far shore, where Ohrid town lies hidden in the haze. More than a thousand years ago, a scholar chose this quiet place to teach and pray. The water still rises here, steady and clear, just as it did then.
