The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you look at anything, listen. There is the soft knock of a wooden oar against stone, the slap of water under a hull, and a boatwoman's voice rising and falling somewhere around the next bend. Under your feet, the paving stones are worn smooth and slightly uneven. The air smells of canal water, wet stone, and something rich and sweet cooking nearby. Welcome to Zhouzhuang, one of the water towns of the lower Yangtze, where the streets were built of water long before they were built of stone.

Zhouzhuang lies southeast of central Suzhou, within the city of Kunshan. This flat, wet country sits between lakes and rivers. For centuries, people here moved goods, crops, and news by boat rather than by road. Towns grew along the canals the way towns elsewhere grow along highways. Houses face the water. Steps lead down from front doors straight into the canal. Water was the front door, the market road, and the laundry, all at once. Keep that in mind as you walk, because it explains almost everything you see.

The town is old. Local tradition says it takes its name from a man called Zhou Digong. During the Song dynasty, roughly a thousand years ago, he is said to have donated his land to a Buddhist temple. The village that grew around that gift became known as the Zhou estate — Zhouzhuang. The story is told everywhere here, on signs and by guides. It is best heard as the town's own account of its beginnings, rather than a precise record.

What we can see more clearly is the shape of the place today. Its canals form a rough cross-shaped pattern, and many old bridges still span them. Most are built of grey stone, some with steep steps, some flat and low. Look at how the arches are shaped. A high, round arch let loaded boats pass underneath. A flat stone slab was cheaper, but it only worked where boat traffic was light. Each bridge tells you something about the traffic it once carried.

Now for the town's most famous pair. Find the Twin Bridges, where two canals meet at a corner. One bridge is a round arch. The other is flat and square. Together they make a shape that some say looks like an old-fashioned Chinese key. Both date to the Ming dynasty — the era that ruled China from the fourteenth to the seventeenth century. Stand back a little, on the bank, and you will see why painters have loved this corner for so long.

Here is a surprise most visitors miss. In the 1980s, the Shanghai painter Chen Yifei made an oil painting of these bridges. It was shown in the United States and bought by the American businessman Armand Hammer. Hammer later gave it as a gift to the Chinese leader Deng Xiaoping. The story spread, and so did the image. Before that painting, Zhouzhuang was a quiet farming and trading town. After it, the world started to come.

Walk on toward the Shen House. This large merchant residence belonged to the Shen family, and it stretches far back from the canal. Room follows room, courtyard follows courtyard. Notice how each section rises slightly as you move deeper inside. The front parts near the water were for business and boats. The middle held formal halls for guests. The private family rooms sat furthest back, quiet and protected. A house like this was a map of social order, from public to private.

The family's most famous name is Shen Wansan, a very wealthy merchant of the early Ming period. Legend has it that he grew so rich that he offered to help pay for the walls of the capital at Nanjing. According to the popular tale, the first Ming emperor saw this as a threat and sent him into exile. Historians debate how much of this is true. But the story is still told in Zhouzhuang, and his name appears everywhere — even on the menu.

That sweet, rich smell? It is probably Wansan pork hoof, the town's signature dish. A whole pork knuckle is slow-cooked in soy sauce and sugar until it is dark, glossy, and soft enough to pull apart with chopsticks. You will see it stacked in shop windows, wrapped and ready to carry home. Locals connect the dish to Shen Wansan himself. Whether or not he ate it, it is the taste most people take away from this town.

Not far away is the Zhang House, another old family residence. Its most unusual detail is water. A small stream runs into the property, and in the old days boats could enter the house itself. Imagine a family receiving rice, firewood, or guests without stepping outside. Inside, look at the dark wooden beams, the carved window lattices, and the small courtyards that pull light down into the rooms. These houses were built to stay cool in the humid summers.

As you walk, look up at the roofs. Grey tiles overlap in neat rows, and the whitewashed walls are often stained by damp and time. Some walls rise in stepped shapes above the rooftops. These are firewalls, built to stop flames from jumping between tightly packed wooden houses. This is the classic look of the region: white walls, grey tiles, dark wood, and green water. It is simple, but it repeats in endless small variations, and the eye never quite tires of it.

If you take a boat, you will see the town as it was meant to be seen. The boats are long and narrow, steered by a single oar at the back. The rower often sings local songs as you glide under the bridges. From the water, the houses look taller, and the bridges frame each view like a doorway. You also notice small things — washing steps, old mooring stones, and moss along the waterline.

Kunshan, the city around Zhouzhuang, is also the birthplace of Kunqu, one of China's oldest forms of opera. It is slow, graceful, and sung in a high, delicate style. You may hear it drifting from a stage or a teahouse as you walk. Quieter streets, a little away from the main canals, show daily life too: residents sitting in doorways, chopping vegetables, or chatting across the water from one window to another.

Before you leave, find a bridge and simply stand for a moment. Watch the light move on the canal. Listen to the oars, the voices, the footsteps on the stone. Look at the white walls reflected upside down in the water. This town has changed a great deal, and it is far busier than it once was. But the basic shape — water, stone, and houses facing each other across a canal — is still here, right in front of you.
