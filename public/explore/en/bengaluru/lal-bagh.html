The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The paths here are soft with fallen leaves and fine red dust. Early in the morning the air is cool and damp, and it smells of wet earth and cut grass. Somewhere nearby, a walker claps their hands as they stride past. Crows call from the high branches. Then the city traffic fades into a low hum beyond the trees. You are standing in Lal Bagh, one of the oldest and best-loved gardens in Bengaluru.

The name means the Red Garden. Some say it refers to the red roses that once grew here in great numbers. Others link it to the reddish soil you can see under your shoes. Either way, the name has stayed for more than two centuries. The garden spreads across roughly 240 acres in the south of the city. That is large enough to lose yourself for a whole morning, and many people do exactly that.

The story begins in the eighteenth century, with Hyder Ali, the ruler of Mysore. Around 1760, he ordered a private garden in the style of the Mughal gardens of northern India. Those gardens were formal spaces, built around straight paths, water channels and careful symmetry. His son, Tipu Sultan, continued the work. Tipu was curious about plants from distant lands. He had trees and seeds brought here from places such as Persia, Afghanistan and Mauritius.

After Tipu's death in 1799, the British took control of Mysore, and the garden changed character. It slowly turned from a royal pleasure garden into a centre for botany. In 1856, it became a government botanical garden. Superintendents arrived to manage the collection, test new crops, and send seeds across the region. One of them, a German gardener named Gustav Krumbiegel, later shaped much of the landscaping. The scientific work still continues here today, quietly, behind the flower beds.

Now look for the glass house. It is the building most visitors come to see. Its frame is iron, and its walls and roof are glass, so the light inside is soft and even. It was built in the late nineteenth century, and many people compare it to the Crystal Palace, the famous glass exhibition hall in London. A British prince laid its foundation stone. On a sunny day, the panes shine white, and the frame throws thin shadows across the floor.

Twice a year, the glass house fills with flowers. The great flower shows take place around Republic Day in January and Independence Day in August. These are two national holidays in India. For each show, gardeners build a huge model entirely from flowers. It might be a temple, a palace, or a portrait of a famous figure. Hundreds of thousands of blooms can go into a single display. The crowds are enormous, so expect queues and slow walking.

Here is a surprise that many visitors miss. Walk towards the large rock outcrop in the garden. It looks like a simple grey hill, worn smooth by rain and feet. But geologists count it among the oldest rocks on Earth. It is a type of stone called Peninsular Gneiss, a banded rock formed deep underground. Scientists estimate its age at around three billion years. India has named it a National Geological Monument. You can sit on rock older than almost any living thing.

At the top of the rock stands a small stone tower with a pillared roof. Local tradition links it to Kempegowda, the chieftain who founded Bengaluru in the sixteenth century. According to the popular account, he built four such towers to mark the limits of his city. The exact story is debated, so treat it as local legend. Either way, climb up slowly in the cool hours. From the top, the old garden meets the modern skyline, with towers of glass and concrete in the distance.

Now turn your attention to the trees. Lal Bagh holds a very large collection of plants, with well over a thousand species. Some of the trees are enormous, with trunks that several people could not wrap their arms around. Look for silk cotton trees, rain trees and tall palms. Many trees carry small labels with their names. Watch the ground too. Seed pods, red petals and huge leaves gather beneath the canopy, and they change with every season.

There is water here as well. On one edge of the garden, a lake stretches out under the open sky. Herons and egrets stand in the shallows, very still, waiting for fish. In the evening, the light turns gold on the surface. Benches face the water, and people sit in silence or talk quietly. If you have walked for a while, this is a good place to rest and simply listen.

Lal Bagh is not only a place for visitors. It belongs to the people of the city. In the early morning, you will see hundreds of local walkers. Some move briskly in sports shoes. Older couples walk side by side in slow, steady steps. Groups practise yoga on the lawns, or stretch in a circle and laugh together. Small vendors near the gates may sell fresh juice or cut fruit. This daily ritual has carried on for generations.

Look out for a few smaller details as you go. There is a floral clock, with its face planted in living flowers. There is an old bandstand, where music once played on quiet evenings. Here and there you will find statues and stone benches from the colonial era. None of these are grand on their own. Together, though, they show how many hands have shaped this ground, from Mysore's rulers to British botanists to today's gardeners.

Before you leave, pause for a moment under the nearest tree. Notice the light through the leaves, and the mix of sounds around you — birds, footsteps, a distant horn. The garden began as a ruler's private retreat. Now it is open to everyone, and it holds stone older than the continents and trees planted by kings. As you walk back towards the gate, take one last look at the red earth beneath your feet. Then step out into the city again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early morning for cool air and fewer crowds.
Expect long queues during the January and August shows.
Climb Lalbagh Rock at sunrise for skyline views.
Wear comfortable shoes for long walks on dusty paths.
Carry water and sun protection for the open lawns.
