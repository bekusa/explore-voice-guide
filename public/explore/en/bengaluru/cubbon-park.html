The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the traffic of central Bengaluru, there is another sound here: leaves moving overhead, a koel calling from somewhere high, and the soft crunch of red earth under walking shoes. The air feels a few degrees cooler under these trees. Light comes down in patches, green and gold, onto paths worn smooth by generations of morning walkers. You are standing in Cubbon Park, the large green space at the centre of this city.

The park dates from 1870, the period of British administration in the Mysore region. Its layout is credited to Richard Sankey, a British military engineer who served as chief engineer of Mysore State. The name honours Sir Mark Cubbon, a long-serving commissioner who ran the region's administration for many years in the middle of the nineteenth century. Cubbon himself had left India before the park took shape. The green space carries the name of a man who never walked its paths.

Here is a detail many visitors never learn. In 1927 the park received an official new name: Sri Chamarajendra Park. It honours Chamarajendra Wadiyar the Tenth, a ruler of the Mysore kingdom. The Wadiyars were the royal family who ruled Mysore for centuries. Yet almost nobody uses the official name. Signboards, maps and taxi drivers still say Cubbon Park. In this city, the old name simply stayed.

The park was much smaller at the start. Over the decades it grew, and today its area is often given as roughly three hundred acres, though encroachment and new roads have changed the figure over time. Either way, it is large enough to lose yourself in. Some paths run straight and formal, in the colonial style. Others curve gently between trees, so the far side of a lawn disappears from view.

Now look at the trees themselves, because they are the real residents here. The park holds thousands of trees from many species, both native and introduced. You will see huge rain trees with wide, umbrella-shaped crowns. There are tall silver oaks, clumps of bamboo that creak in the wind, and flowering trees that drop colour onto the paths in season. Botanists and nature groups lead regular walks here to identify them. Even without a guide, try touching the bark of a few. Each tree feels different under the hand.

The bamboo groves deserve a special pause. Stand inside one when there is a breeze. The tall stems knock against each other with a hollow, wooden sound, almost like distant drums. The light inside a grove is dimmer and greener than on the open lawns. Bengaluru was once called the Garden City of India, and corners like this one help explain the nickname. For many residents, this park is the clearest memory of what the city used to feel like.

At the northern edge of the park, a long building in deep red catches the eye. This is the Attara Kacheri, which now houses the High Court of Karnataka. It was built in the colonial period to hold government offices. The name comes from a local phrase meaning eighteen offices, after the eighteen departments it once contained. Its colour matches a theme you will see across the park. Many of the official buildings here wear the same strong, earthy red, which stands out against the green.

Inside the park itself stands another red building with white details: the Sheshadri Iyer Memorial Hall. It honours Sir K. Sheshadri Iyer, a celebrated chief minister, or dewan, of Mysore State. Today the hall serves as the State Central Library. Its classical columns and arched windows face a formal rose garden. On a quiet morning you may see students on the steps with books open. It is one of the few libraries in the world where you walk through a forest to reach the reading room.

Along the park's edges, there is more culture to find. On Kasturba Road you will find the Government Museum, one of the older museums in India, with sculpture and archaeological finds from across the region. Next to it is the Visvesvaraya Industrial and Technological Museum, named after the engineer Sir M. Visvesvaraya. The Venkatappa Art Gallery sits nearby as well. If you have a spare afternoon, these buildings turn a park walk into a full day.

The park also holds older statues from the colonial era. Near the entrance on the Mahatma Gandhi Road side, a marble statue of Queen Victoria stands under a canopy. Elsewhere you can find a statue of King Edward the Seventh. These figures belong to a different time, and they now share the park with joggers, yoga groups and school children. That contrast is part of the story. The park was built for one city and is now used by quite another.

If you come early, you will see the park at its most local. Before the heat rises, walkers move in steady loops, some in sports shoes, some in saris. Groups practise laughter yoga, which mixes breathing exercises with deliberate, shared laughter. Dogs pull their owners toward the lawns. In recent years, informal gatherings have also become popular, such as groups who sit together on the grass and read in silence. The park changes its personality several times through the day.

Families have their own corner too. Bal Bhavan, a children's area inside the park, has rides, play spaces and a small toy train that has delighted local children for decades. On weekends you will hear their voices carry across the trees. For many people who grew up in Bengaluru, a first train ride happened right here. Ask an older local, and you may hear a warm story about it.

This green space has also become a place where the city argues about its future. Bengaluru has grown very fast, and traffic, construction and new projects press against the park's borders. Residents and walkers' groups have often spoken up to protect the trees and limit vehicles inside. Rules on traffic through the park have changed over the years. So when you see a quiet road here with no cars, remember that the quiet has been defended.

Before you leave, stop under one of the big rain trees and look up. The branches spread wide, and small leaves fold and open with the light. Notice the red earth at your feet, the red walls of the old buildings beyond, and the city noise softening at the edges. As you walk out toward the streets, listen for the moment the birdsong fades and the horns return. That moment shows you exactly what this park gives the city.

Practical tips

Wear comfortable shoes for long loops on earth paths.
Bring a mat and sit on the lawns with a book.
