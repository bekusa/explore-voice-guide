The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the tall stone columns, and somewhere nearby a tractor rumbles along a village road. Underfoot, the ground is a mix of packed earth, loose gravel and pale marble fragments. Around you, fields of wheat and poplar trees stretch toward low hills. This is Aizanoi, an ancient Roman city that sits quietly at the edge of the modern village of Çavdarhisar, in the province of Kütahya. Few visitors reach this place. That quiet is a large part of what makes it special.

Aizanoi lies in the old region of Phrygia, the inland heart of western Anatolia. Long before Rome, Phrygian communities farmed this upland plain. Local tradition gave the city a heroic founder named Azan. According to Greek myth, he was a son of Arcas, the legendary ancestor of the Arcadians in southern Greece. Treat that story as legend, not history. It tells us how the people of Aizanoi wanted to see themselves: as Greeks, with a noble family tree, living far from the sea.

The city reached its greatest wealth under Roman rule. That wealth came from the land around you. Grain, wool and wine flowed from these fields, and the profits paid for marble buildings on a grand scale. As you walk, notice how flat and fertile the plain is. The river that runs through the site, known in antiquity as the Penkalas, watered those fields. Today the locals call it the Kocaçay. The river is still the spine of the ancient city.

Now turn toward the great temple. It rises on a raised terrace, and it is the reason most people make the journey here. The temple was dedicated to Zeus, the king of the Greek gods. It dates to the second century, during the reign of the emperor Hadrian. Many of its slender Ionic columns still stand, with their spiral-shaped capitals at the top. Ionic is one of the classical styles of Greek architecture, known for those graceful scrolls. Few Roman temples in Anatolia survive in such good condition.

Look closely at the walls of the temple. They carry long carved inscriptions in Greek. These are not decorations. They are official letters, including correspondence connected to Hadrian, about a long-running dispute over sacred land owned by the temple. In other words, the walls worked as a public archive. Anyone who could read could check the rules. Here is a surprise that most visitors miss — you are looking at legal paperwork, written in stone, nearly two thousand years old.

The second surprise lies beneath your feet. Under the temple is a vaulted underground chamber. Scholars connect it with the worship of a mother goddess, an ancient Anatolian figure the Greeks often linked with Cybele. Cybele was a powerful goddess of nature and fertility, honoured across this region long before Zeus arrived. So the building holds two layers of belief: a Greek sky god above, and an older Anatolian goddess below. Local legend even claims that Zeus himself was born in a cave near Aizanoi.

Step back and take in the setting of the temple. It once stood inside a large colonnaded courtyard. Walls and porches framed it on all sides, and the open space would have filled with pilgrims, priests and traders. Today the courtyard is mostly grass and scattered stone. Fallen column drums lie in rows, like slices of a giant loaf. Archaeologists from the German Archaeological Institute have studied the site for many decades, and much of what you see has been carefully measured and partly restored.

From the temple, look north toward the combined theatre and stadium. This is one of the most unusual buildings at Aizanoi. The theatre and the stadium were built as a single linked complex, sharing one long axis. Spectators could watch plays in one part and athletic races in the other. Few ancient cities joined the two in this way. The seating rises against the slope, and on a quiet day you can imagine the noise of thousands of voices drifting across the plain.

Next, find the round market building, called the macellum. A macellum was a Roman food market, where people bought meat, fish and other goods. The walls here carry a remarkable text: a copy of the Edict on Maximum Prices issued by the emperor Diocletian in 301. The edict tried to fix the highest legal price for hundreds of goods and services, from grain to shoes to a barber's work. It was an attempt to stop runaway inflation across the empire. It failed — but the inscription survives.

Walk down to the river and look at the Roman bridges. Several ancient stone bridges cross the Kocaçay, and some still carry traffic today. Villagers, farm carts and the occasional car pass over arches built by Roman engineers. Look for carved reliefs on the bridge stones. This is one of the quiet pleasures of Aizanoi. The ancient city is not sealed behind glass. It is part of daily life in Çavdarhisar, and the old and the new share the same road.

The Romans also built bath complexes here, because public bathing was a central part of city life. One bath building held a floor mosaic showing figures from the world of Dionysus, the god of wine and festivity. Mosaics like this were made from thousands of small coloured stones, set into mortar. Remember that the people of Aizanoi bathed, gossiped and did business in these halls. The baths were the social clubs of the Roman world, as much as places to get clean.

As the centuries passed, Aizanoi changed. In the Byzantine period it remained a settlement, and later the temple terrace was fortified. The name of the modern village, Çavdarhisar, contains the Turkish word hisar, which means castle or fortress. That name points to this later role. Afterwards the city faded into a quiet farming community. European travellers rediscovered the temple in the nineteenth century, and their drawings helped make the site known to scholars far away.

Before you leave, stand once more facing the temple. Notice the colour of the marble. In the morning it looks almost grey. Late in the afternoon it warms to honey and gold. Listen for the sounds around you: birds in the poplars, a dog barking in the village, the river moving under the old arches. The columns have stood through earthquakes, wars and long centuries of silence. As you walk back toward the road, look down at the stones beneath your feet. Some of them were shaped by Roman hands.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit late afternoon for warm light on the marble.
Wear sturdy shoes for gravel and uneven ancient paving.
Bring water and sun protection; the plain offers little shade.
Walk the Roman bridges across the river in the village.
Allow several hours to cover temple, theatre and market.
