The guide

The story

Listen first. A low wind moves through the grass on the old earthen rampart. Somewhere beyond it, water laps against a reedy shore. Sheep may be grazing on the slopes. It is a quiet, almost pastoral scene. Yet more than a thousand years ago this same ground carried the noise of hammers, foreign languages, barking dogs, and the creak of ships being unloaded. You are standing at Hedeby, once one of the most important trading towns of the Viking world.

Hedeby sits on the shore of a small lake called the Haddebyer Noor. The lake connects to the Schlei, a long, narrow arm of the Baltic Sea. That geography is the whole secret of this place. Merchants could sail in from the Baltic, then cover a short overland route to rivers that flowed west toward the North Sea. Instead of sailing the long, dangerous way around the tip of Jutland, traders could cross here. The town grew at this narrow point between two seas.

The settlement took shape in the Viking Age, roughly from the eighth century onward. Its name is usually explained as meaning something like settlement on the heath. In German you will also hear it called Haithabu, and you will see both names on signs around you. Over the next two centuries, Hedeby became a place where Scandinavians, Saxons, Frisians, Slavs, and travellers from much farther away met to buy and sell.

Look now at the great curved earthwork that surrounds the site. This semicircular rampart once enclosed the town on its landward side, with the open side facing the water. In its final form it was a substantial defensive wall, and today it still rises clearly above the fields. Walking along it gives you the best sense of the town's scale. Everything inside that curve was once streets, workshops, wells, and houses packed closely together.

And here is the first surprise. Hedeby was not an isolated fort. It was linked to the Danevirke, a long system of earthen walls that ran across the neck of the Jutland peninsula. For centuries, these walls marked a frontier between the Danish realm to the north and the lands to the south. In 2018, Hedeby and the Danevirke were inscribed together as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognised as one connected border landscape.

What did people trade here? Archaeologists have found evidence of an astonishing range of goods. There was glass, fine pottery, and wine from the Rhineland. There was silver from the Islamic world, often cut into pieces and weighed on small scales. There were amber, furs, walrus ivory, and soapstone from the north. Craftspeople worked bone and antler into combs, forged iron, and made jewellery. Coins were also minted here, an early sign of a real urban economy.

One of the most vivid accounts of Hedeby comes from a traveller from Muslim Spain, usually named as Ibrahim ibn Yaqub, who visited in the tenth century. His report survives only through later writers, so treat the details with some caution. He described a large town by the sea. He noted that women could choose to divorce, and that both men and women wore eye make-up. He was not impressed by the local singing. He compared it, rather unkindly, to the barking of dogs.

Hedeby also appears in the story of early Christianity in the north. In the ninth century, the missionary Ansgar worked in this region and is linked with the founding of a church in the town. That makes Hedeby part of a slow, uneven change of faith across Scandinavia. Older beliefs and newer ones lived side by side here for generations, just as foreign goods and local crafts did.

The end of the town was violent. In the middle of the eleventh century, Hedeby was attacked and burned. Written sources connect one attack with the Norwegian king Harald Hardrada, and a later one with raiders from the Slavic lands to the east. After these blows, the town was never rebuilt on this spot. Trade and settlement moved across the water to Schleswig, the town you can see on the far side of the Schlei.

This abandonment is the second surprise, and it is the reason Hedeby matters so much today. Because no medieval or modern town was built on top of it, the remains stayed in the ground. The wet soil near the shore preserved wood, leather, and textiles that normally rot away. Excavations have continued since the early twentieth century, yet archaeologists estimate that only a small part of the site has ever been dug.

Inside the rampart you will find a group of reconstructed houses. They are based on the plans of buildings found in excavation. Notice the walls of woven wattle covered with clay, the thatched roofs, and the low doorways. Step inside one if it is open. The air smells of wood and old smoke. The rooms are dim, lit mostly by the doorway and the hearth. This is the scale at which people actually lived here.

Nearby stands the Viking Museum, built just outside the old town. Its displays include finds from the excavations, from tools and jewellery to everyday objects. One of its most striking exhibits is the preserved remains of a Viking ship recovered from the harbour, displayed in a large hall. The museum also holds runestones from the area. These carved stones record names and deeds in the old runic alphabet, a writing system of straight lines made for cutting into stone and wood.

As you walk between the museum, the houses, and the rampart, notice how much of the experience is open landscape. Paths cross meadow and pass old trees. On a clear day, light moves fast across the water of the Noor. In wet weather, the ground can be soft underfoot, a reminder that this was always a shoreline town. Give yourself time here. The site rewards a slow walk more than a quick visit.

Before you leave, stand once more on the rampart and look toward the water. Imagine the shore lined with jetties, the masts of ships, and smoke rising from hundreds of hearths. Then let the picture fade, and see what is really there today. Grass, reeds, a few rebuilt houses, and the quiet lake. All of that busy town now lies under your feet, waiting in the soil.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes; meadow paths can turn muddy after rain.
Visit the museum first to understand the rampart walk.
Walk the full rampart curve for the best overview.
Allow at least half a day for museum and houses.
Bring a windproof layer; the lakeshore is exposed.
