The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Underneath the birdsong there is a low, steady hum. That is the traffic on the parkway far below, softened by trees and stone. Up here the air feels cooler than on the city streets. The paths are made of worn stone and old asphalt, and they curve along a high ridge of dark rock. To the west, through the leaves, you can see the Hudson River shining. This is Fort Tryon Park, at the northern tip of Manhattan.

Many visitors are surprised by how high you are. Manhattan is often pictured as flat and gridded, but this end of the island is very different. A spine of hard bedrock runs along the western edge here, and the park sits right on top of it. Look down at the exposed rock beside the paths. It is old, grey, and glittering in places with mica. The city did not shape this ridge. The ridge decided where the city could go.

That height is the reason for the name. During the American Revolution, this rocky ground was a defensive position. It guarded the northern approach to Fort Washington, an American stronghold to the south. In November 1776, British and Hessian troops attacked. Hessians were German soldiers hired by the British crown. The Americans were defeated, and Fort Washington fell. Afterwards the British renamed this outpost Fort Tryon, after William Tryon, the last British colonial governor of New York.

One person from that battle is remembered all over this park. Her name was Margaret Corbin. Her husband was serving with an artillery crew, and when he was killed, she took his place at the cannon. She kept firing until she was badly wounded. Later, she became the first woman to receive a military pension from the United States government. Keep an eye out for her name. A circle and a drive nearby carry it, and a plaque honours her.

Now jump forward more than a century. By the early twentieth century, this ridge was covered with private estates. Wealthy families built country houses here, with views over the river that money could not improve. One of the largest belonged to a businessman named C.K.G. Billings. Most of his mansion is gone now. But part of his estate survives in stone, as you will see if you know where to look.

The man who turned these estates into a public park was John D. Rockefeller Jr. In the 1910s and 1920s he quietly bought up the land on this ridge. Then he gave it to the City of New York. He also hired the Olmsted Brothers firm to design the park. The lead designer was Frederick Law Olmsted Jr., son of the man who designed Central Park. The new park opened to the public in 1935.

Walk to one of the western overlooks and face the river. Across the water, the cliffs of New Jersey rise straight up. They are called the Palisades. Notice what you do not see on top of them... no towers, no apartment blocks, just trees. That is not an accident. Rockefeller bought large stretches of land along those cliffs and helped protect them. He wanted the view from this park to stay wild. In a sense, part of this park is in another state.

Near the northern end of the park stands its most famous feature. It looks like a medieval monastery, with a square tower rising above the trees. This is The Met Cloisters, a branch of the Metropolitan Museum of Art. It opened in 1938. Here is the surprise. Parts of it really are medieval. Carved stone pieces from old monasteries and churches in France and elsewhere in Europe were brought across the ocean and built into the new walls.

Inside, the collection focuses on art and architecture from medieval Europe. Its best-known treasure is a set of woven tapestries showing the hunt for a unicorn. They are rich with plants, animals, and small human details. The museum also has gardens planted with species that people grew in the Middle Ages, for food, medicine, and symbolism. Even if you do not go inside today, walk up to the building. Feel the change from leafy park to cool, solid stone.

Back in the park, follow the paths to the Heather Garden. It runs along a terrace on the eastern side of the ridge. In the cold months, when most of the city looks bare, heathers and heaths here can still show colour, in shades of pink, purple, and white. The planting changes through the seasons. Spring brings bulbs and flowering shrubs. Summer is dense and green. Autumn turns the trees on the slopes to gold and copper.

The park was not always this well cared for. Like many New York parks, it struggled in the later twentieth century. Gardens became overgrown, and some paths felt neglected. Starting in the 1980s, gardeners and volunteers began bringing it back. The Heather Garden was carefully replanted. Later, a nonprofit called the New York Restoration Project, founded by the singer Bette Midler, helped care for the park. Much of what you see today is the result of that patient work.

Now look for the survivor from the Billings estate. On the western side, near the parkway, there is a long stone arcade. It is a series of arches built into the hillside. It once formed part of the grand approach to the Billings mansion. Today cars pass beneath a view that was designed for horse carriages and private guests. It is one of those details that many visitors walk past without a second look.

Take a final moment where you are standing. Notice the mix of layers around you. Ancient bedrock under your feet. The memory of a Revolutionary War battle. A rich man's garden, turned into a public one. Medieval stone, rebuilt in the twentieth century. As you walk away, listen again for that hum of traffic below. It reminds you that the city is still right there... just out of sight beyond the trees.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the western overlooks for open views of the Palisades.
Wear comfortable shoes; paths climb and dip along the ridge.
Book Met Cloisters entry separately if you plan to go inside.
Come in autumn for strong colour on the wooded slopes.
Pair your walk with nearby Inwood Hill Park to the north.
