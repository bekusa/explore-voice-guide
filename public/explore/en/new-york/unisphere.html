The guide

The story

Listen first. On a warm day, the fountains around the base hiss and patter, and wheels clatter as skateboarders cross the paved plaza. Then look up. A huge steel globe hangs above the water, open like a cage. The sky shows through every gap, so the whole world looks light enough to float. This is the Unisphere, in Flushing Meadows Corona Park, in the borough of Queens.

The globe is about twelve stories tall and about 120 feet across. It is built from stainless steel, so it does not rust or need paint. Instead, it changes with the weather. Under a grey sky it looks almost silver and cold. At sunset it can turn soft gold or pink. Walk slowly around the pool and watch how the light moves across the metal continents as your angle changes.

The Unisphere was built for the 1964 to 1965 New York World's Fair. A world's fair was a huge international exhibition, part trade show and part theme park, where countries and companies showed off their newest ideas. This fair had a theme: Peace Through Understanding. The globe was its central symbol, the image on the posters and souvenirs. Millions of visitors came through these grounds during the two seasons of the fair.

The steel was made and donated by United States Steel, one of the largest American companies of the time. For the company, it was also an advertisement. A giant stainless steel world in the middle of a global fair was a clear message about what American industry could build. The design is credited to the landscape architect Gilmore Clarke. He had worked on parks and parkways across the region for decades.

Here is something most visitors miss. The Unisphere is not the first giant globe on this exact spot. For the earlier World's Fair of 1939 and 1940, this site held the Perisphere — a huge white sphere next to a tall, thin spire called the Trylon. They were the symbols of that fair, just as this globe was for the next one. The Perisphere was taken down, and the Unisphere later rose on the same ground, where the earlier sphere had stood.

Now look at the three rings that curve around the globe. They are not decoration only. They represent the paths of early spacecraft orbiting the Earth. The space race was in the news every week in the early 1960s. The rings are commonly said to honour the flights of Yuri Gagarin, the first human in space, the American astronaut John Glenn, and Telstar, an early communications satellite.

Look closely at the surface of the globe. It is made from a grid of lines, like the lines of latitude and longitude on a school map. The continents are attached to this frame as flat steel shapes. The oceans are simply empty space. From some angles you can see straight through Africa to the trees behind it. Try to find your own country, or the place you started your journey.

The globe is also tilted, like the real Earth leans on its axis. That tilt makes the sculpture feel alive, as if it is turning slowly in space. And it does not sit on a heavy block. It rests on a slim base in the middle of the pool. When the fountains run, the water hides the support, and the world seems to hover over the spray.

The area around you was not always a park. Before the 1939 fair, much of this land was a huge ash dump. Coal ash from the city was piled here for years. The writer F. Scott Fitzgerald described a grey valley of ashes on the way to Manhattan in The Great Gatsby, and many readers connect that image with this ground. Two world's fairs turned the dump into the open green space you see today.

After the fair closed, many of the pavilions were removed. The Unisphere stayed. Over the years it became the symbol of Queens itself. You will see it on local logos, signs, and souvenirs. In 1995, New York City made it an official landmark, which means it is protected. The globe has also appeared in films, including Men in Black, so some visitors recognise it before they know its name.

Turn around and look at what remains of the fair nearby. The building with columns is the Queens Museum, a former fair pavilion. Inside it, there is a well-known scale model of the whole city, if you want to see New York from above. To one side, you may see the round towers and open ring of the New York State Pavilion, another survivor from 1964. Beyond the trees are the tennis stadiums of the US Open and the Mets baseball stadium.

The Unisphere is not a museum piece behind glass. It is part of everyday life in Queens. On weekends, families have picnics on the lawns. Couples pose here for wedding and celebration photos. Skateboarders practise on the smooth plaza, and children run along the edge of the pool. Queens is one of the most diverse places on Earth, with residents from many different countries. A steel globe is a fitting symbol for this neighbourhood.

If you want a good photograph, walk away from the pool along the long central path. From a distance, the whole globe fits in the frame with the sky behind it. Then come back close and stand below one of the rings. Looking up from here, the steel lines cross over your head, and the scale finally feels real.

Before you go, take one more slow walk around the water. Find the continent you came from, and the one where you are standing now. Notice the sound of the fountains, the voices in different languages, and the open sky through the empty oceans. The fair is long gone. But its idea of a shared world is still here, made of steel, in the middle of a park in Queens.
