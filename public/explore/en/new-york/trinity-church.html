The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment at the corner of Broadway and Wall Street. Listen. Taxis hiss past, and phones chime. A thousand footsteps cross the pavement every minute. Then, just above the noise, you may catch something older: a bell, or the soft creak of iron gates. In front of you rises a dark stone spire. Around it, grass and crooked gravestones sit in the shadow of glass towers. This is Trinity Church. It is one of the few places in Lower Manhattan where the city seems to slow down and take a breath.

Look straight down Wall Street from the church steps. The street runs east toward the river, and the church sits right at its head, like a full stop at the end of a sentence. That position is no accident of modern planning. The parish was here long before the street became shorthand for global finance. Bankers, clerks and traders have walked past these gravestones for generations. Many of them have stopped for a quiet minute inside before going back to the trading floor.

The story of the parish begins in the late seventeenth century. In 1697, King William the Third of England granted Trinity its charter. New York was then a small colonial port of the English crown, only a few decades after the Dutch had handed it over. The congregation was Anglican, part of the Church of England. After American independence, it became part of the Episcopal Church, the American branch of that same tradition. The church you see today is not the first building on this spot. It is the third.

The first church burned in the Great Fire of 1776. That fire tore through Lower Manhattan during the early, chaotic months of British occupation in the Revolutionary War. A second church was built after the war ended. It did not last either. By the late 1830s, its structure had been badly weakened, and the parish decided to tear it down and start again. That decision gave New York the building in front of you. It was completed in 1846.

The architect was Richard Upjohn, an English-born designer working in America. He chose the Gothic Revival style. That is a nineteenth-century return to the pointed arches, tall windows and soaring spires of medieval European cathedrals. Upjohn's design helped make this style popular for churches across the United States. Notice the vertical lines. The buttresses, the window frames and above all the spire pull your eye upward. The building was meant to lift the mind away from the busy street and toward something larger.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. When this spire was finished, it was the tallest structure in New York City. For several decades, sailors coming into the harbour used it as a landmark. It kept that title until around 1890, when early skyscrapers began to rise nearby. Today, the spire looks almost small. The towers around it make it seem like a model of itself. But stand back, look up, and imagine a low city of brick and timber with this single dark point rising high above it.

Now look closely at the stone. It is a sandstone, often called brownstone, and over many decades city soot turned it very dark, almost black. Cleaning and restoration work in more recent years has removed much of that grime. In some places the stone now shows warmer, lighter tones of pink and brown. Run your eyes over the surface and you will see variation from block to block. Some edges are crisp. Others are softened by weather and repair. The stone carries the marks of almost two centuries in a hard urban climate.

Before you go inside, spend time with the great bronze doors. They were added in the late nineteenth century as a memorial to John Jacob Astor, one of the wealthiest men of his era. The architect Richard Morris Hunt is credited with their overall design. Several sculptors worked on the panels. The panels show scenes and figures from the Bible and from church history. Come close. Bronze picks up the light differently through the day, and the raised figures throw small shadows that change as the sun moves.

Now step into the churchyard. This is one of the oldest burial grounds in Manhattan. Some of its gravestones were carved before the American Revolution. Look for the simple, thin slate and sandstone markers, many of them tilted and worn smooth. Winged skulls and cherubs appear on some of the older stones. These were common symbols of death and the soul in colonial America. The grass, the trees and the low railings make a calm pocket of green in a district made almost entirely of stone and glass.

The most visited grave here belongs to Alexander Hamilton. He was one of the founders of the United States and the first Secretary of the Treasury. He built much of the young country's financial system, so it is fitting that he lies at the head of Wall Street. Hamilton died in 1804, after a duel with Aaron Burr. His wife, Eliza Hamilton, is buried nearby. She outlived him by half a century and worked hard to protect his memory. Since the musical about his life became a hit, visitors often leave coins, flowers and notes at the grave.

Other well-known names rest here too, or are remembered here. Albert Gallatin, another early Treasury Secretary, is buried in the churchyard. There is also a memorial to Robert Fulton, the engineer linked with early steamboats. And then there is a stone for Charlotte Temple. She was the heroine of a popular early American novel about a young woman who was seduced and abandoned. Legend has it that the grave holds the real woman behind the story. Historians are not sure she ever existed. Still, for many years readers came here to mourn her.

Trinity is more than one building. For centuries, the parish has been a major landowner in Lower Manhattan, thanks to a large land grant from Queen Anne in the early eighteenth century. That land helped fund the church's work in the city. The parish also includes Saint Paul's Chapel, a short walk north along Broadway. George Washington worshipped there after his inauguration in 1789. In September 2001, Saint Paul's stood almost in the shadow of the World Trade Center. It survived, and it became a place of rest for recovery workers.

If the doors are open, step inside the church itself. Your eyes will need a moment to adjust. The light comes in through tall stained-glass windows, and the colour falls across the stone floor and the wooden pews. The nave is long and narrow, and it draws you toward the altar. Trinity is also known for its music. Choirs and organ concerts fill this space regularly, and the acoustics hold each note for a long, slow moment. Even on a quiet weekday, people come here to sit, to pray, or simply to rest.

As you leave, pause once more at the gate. Behind you is a churchyard where people were buried when New York was a colonial town. In front of you, Wall Street runs down toward the river, full of screens, suits and movement. Both belong to the same city. Listen again for the bell, or for the gate. Then look back up at the dark spire, now small among the towers. It was here first. It is still here, keeping its own slower time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the churchyard early morning before the lunchtime crowds.
Walk north on Broadway to see Saint Paul's Chapel.
Check the parish calendar for choir and organ concerts.
Keep voices low and photos discreet during services inside.
Stand at the head of Wall Street for the classic view.
