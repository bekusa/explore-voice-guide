The guide

The story

Stand on the corner for a moment and listen. Taxis lean on their horns along Fifth Avenue. Delivery bikes hiss past on the wet asphalt. People hurry toward Madison Square Park a few blocks south. Then you notice a doorway that most of them walk straight past without a second look. This is the Museum of Sex. It is one of the few museums in New York where the front desk checks your age before you buy a ticket.

The museum opened in 2002 on Fifth Avenue at 27th Street. That puts it in the stretch of Manhattan now called NoMad, short for North of Madison Square Park. Its founder, Daniel Gluck, had an idea that sounded provocative at the time. He wanted a serious museum about human sexuality, with curators, collections and research. He did not want a novelty shop with a gallery attached. More than two decades later, it is still here, and it still divides opinion. That tension is part of the experience.

Look at the building itself before you go in. From the outside it is restrained, almost polite. There is no flashing neon and no cartoon signage, only glass, stone and a clean modern frontage. The calm facade is deliberate. A visitor can walk in without feeling that they are entering somewhere seedy. Inside, the mood changes. Lighting drops, walls darken, and sound becomes softer and closer. The design keeps your attention on the objects and the stories.

Here is the first thing many visitors do not expect. The heart of the museum is history, not shock. The permanent collection holds many thousands of objects. These include artworks, photographs, films, advertisements, medical devices, costumes and everyday items from many decades and many cultures. Some objects are funny. Some are sad. Some are quietly beautiful. Together they show how ideas about the body, desire and privacy have changed, and how often those ideas were shaped by law, religion and commerce.

The exhibitions rotate, so no two visits are quite the same. In past years, galleries have looked at subjects like sex in early cinema, the history of erotic photography, animal behaviour in the natural world, and the cultural history of contraception. A good exhibition here works like a good magazine feature. It takes something people giggle about and asks a serious question. Who made the rules? Who broke them? Who was punished, and who profited?

Think about the neighbourhood around you. In the late nineteenth century, parts of this side of Manhattan sat close to the district New Yorkers called the Tenderloin. It was known for theatres, dance halls, saloons and brothels. Reformers campaigned against it, and police were accused of taking money to look the other way. So a museum about sexuality is not entirely out of place on this map. The city's own past shows the same mix of desire, morality and money.

New York has always had a complicated relationship with censorship. For much of the twentieth century, books, films and photographs that are now ordinary could lead to arrests or seizures. Some of the material in collections like this one survived only because private collectors kept it hidden. When you see a faded pamphlet or a grainy film still, remember that someone once took a risk to keep it. Preservation is a theme that runs quietly through the galleries.

Now for the second surprise. This museum is also known for its playful, hands-on installations. The best known is a room filled with large inflatable shapes that visitors can climb and bounce on. It is a giant bouncy castle for adults, built in a very particular form. It sounds silly, and it is. But the laughter is part of the point. The museum suggests that play and curiosity belong in the conversation alongside history and science.

Take a moment to watch the other visitors. You will see couples nudging each other, friends laughing too loudly, and solo travellers reading every label carefully. Some people move quickly, a little embarrassed. Others stand in front of one case for a long time. The museum works almost like a mirror. How you react to the rooms tells you something about where you grew up, what you were taught, and what you were never told.

The tone of the labels matters here. They are written in a calm, factual voice. They avoid winking jokes and moral lectures. That restraint lets difficult subjects sit next to light ones. A gallery about romance and courtship might sit near one about public health campaigns. A display about fashion might sit near one about protest movements. The museum asks you to connect these threads yourself instead of handing you a single conclusion.

Before you leave, many visitors spend time in the shop near the entrance. It is clearly designed with the same care as the galleries. You will find books on art, history and health beside more playful items. Even here, the presentation is clean and bright rather than secretive. It is another quiet statement about the museum's goal, which is to bring the subject out of the back room and into ordinary daylight.

When you step back outside, look north along Fifth Avenue. On a clear day, the top of the Empire State Building rises over the rooftops. Turn south, and the trees of Madison Square Park mark the edge of the Flatiron District, with its famous wedge-shaped tower nearby. This short walk holds hotels, cafes, office lobbies and old cast-iron facades. The museum sits among them almost unnoticed, which is perhaps exactly how it prefers to sit.

So take one last look at that modest doorway. People are still walking past it, phones in hand, heading somewhere else. You now know what is behind the glass. It is a collection about one of the oldest parts of human life, kept in one of the busiest corners of one of the busiest cities. As you walk away, notice the faces around you on the pavement... every one of them carries a private story, and none of them shows it.
