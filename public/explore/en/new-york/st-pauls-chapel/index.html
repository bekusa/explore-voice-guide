St. Paul's Chapel

The story

Step off the noise of Broadway and into sudden quiet. The traffic fades behind stone walls. Old trees lean over weathered gravestones, and the air feels cooler here, shaded and still. You are standing in the yard of St. Paul's Chapel, the oldest church building still standing in Manhattan.

Builders finished this chapel in 1766. At that time, the British king still ruled these colonies, and the city was small, clustered near the harbor to the south. The chapel stood at the edge of town, looking out over open fields. The stone is rough and gray, cut from local rock, and the style is plain and balanced, in the Georgian manner of its day. Notice, too, that the chapel's grandest face does not look toward Broadway. Its tall columns and main porch face the other way, toward the west, where the water once lay closer to the town.

Ten years after it was built, much of New York burned. A great fire swept through the city in 1776, during the war for independence, and destroyed about a quarter of its buildings. St. Paul's survived. It would not be the last time this small chapel outlasted disaster around it.

In 1789, a new nation chose New York as its first capital. On the thirtieth of April that year, George Washington took the oath as the first president a short walk from here. After the ceremony, he came to this chapel to pray. For the next two years, while the city served as the capital, Washington worshiped here regularly.

His pew still stands inside, set apart and marked. Above it hangs an early painting of the seal of the United States, made when the design was new. Step indoors and the light changes. Pale columns rise to a curved ceiling painted in soft colors. Cut-glass chandeliers hang from above. The room is bright, calm, and simple. Look up to find the carved wooden pulpit, raised on its slender stand. For a building so old, the space feels surprisingly light, made for gathering rather than for shadow.

For more than two centuries, the chapel kept its quiet routine of services and prayers. The city grew up and around it, rising higher each decade. Tall towers replaced the open fields. By the end of the twentieth century, two enormous towers of the World Trade Center stood just across the street, less than a hundred yards from the chapel door.

On the morning of September 11, 2001, those towers fell. The destruction reached across the whole neighborhood. Windows broke, and a thick gray dust covered everything for blocks in every direction. Yet St. Paul's Chapel, so close to the towers, came through without major harm. Not even a single window was lost.

Many believe a tree saved it. A large sycamore had grown for years at the edge of the churchyard, on the side facing the towers. When the buildings fell, the tree took the force of the flying debris and was destroyed. The chapel behind it stood. People began to call it the little chapel that stood.

In the weeks and months that followed, the chapel became a place of rest. Recovery workers labored day and night at the ruined site nearby, searching the rubble. They came here to sleep on the pews, to eat warm food, and to be cared for. Thousands passed through. Letters, photographs, and badges from around the world were left along the fence and the walls. The relief effort here went on for months, day after day, until the work at the ruined site was finally done.

Walk slowly through the rooms today and you can still see signs of that time, kept as a memorial. Cots, banners, and gifts from those difficult months are placed here with care. The chapel holds two stories at once now. One reaches back to the founding of the country. The other is close, and recent, and still tender.

Outside again, walk among the old graves in the yard. Some of the stones are so worn that the names have nearly faded away. Roots push up the earth between them. Birds move in the branches overhead. Beyond the iron fence, modern towers climb into the sky, and the rebuilt World Trade Center site stands close by.

St. Paul's Chapel is a survivor. It outlived a colonial fire, watched a nation begin, and stood through the worst day its neighborhood ever knew. It asks nothing of you but a few quiet minutes. Sit on a bench in the shade, between the old stones and the new towers, and let the city wait outside the wall a little longer.

Key facts

St. Paul's Chapel was completed in 1766.
It is the oldest church building in Manhattan.
George Washington worshiped here after his 1789 inauguration.
The chapel survived the Great New York Fire of 1776.
It stood undamaged through the September 11, 2001 attacks.
