The guide

The story

Listen first. Underneath the wind off the water, there is a low, steady hum. It comes from the traffic far above you, rolling across two decks of steel. Gulls cut across the grey-green water of the Narrows. Salt hangs in the air. And over all of it, two pale towers rise into the sky, joined by a long, gentle curve of cable. This is the Verrazzano–Narrows Bridge. It is the doorway to New York Harbor.

The Narrows is the tight strait between Brooklyn and Staten Island. Every ship that enters the inner harbour from the Atlantic must pass through this gap. For centuries, ferries were the only link between these two boroughs. Staten Island stayed quiet and semi-rural, cut off from the rest of the city by water. A bridge here was discussed for decades. Engineers knew the problem well. The span had to be very long, and very high, so that ocean liners could still pass beneath it.

The bridge finally opened to traffic in November 1964. At that moment it held the record for the longest suspension span in the world. Its main span runs nearly 1,300 metres between the towers. It kept that world title until 1981, when the Humber Bridge opened in England. Even today, it remains one of the longest suspension spans in the Americas. The lower deck came a few years later, in 1969, as traffic grew faster than anyone had planned.

The man behind the design was Othmar Ammann, a Swiss-born engineer. By then he was already a legend in this city. He had shaped the George Washington Bridge and several other crossings around New York. The Verrazzano was his last great work, completed when he was in his eighties. Look at the clean lines of the towers. There is almost no decoration. Ammann believed that a bridge should be beautiful through its proportions alone, not through ornament.

Here is a detail most visitors never hear. The two towers are more than two hundred metres tall, and they stand very far apart. Because of that distance, the curve of the Earth matters. The tops of the towers are slightly farther apart than their bases — by a few centimetres. Each tower stands straight up toward the sky. But the surface of our planet bends between them, so they lean apart just a little.

The bridge also moves with the seasons. Steel expands in heat and contracts in cold. On a hot summer day, the four main cables grow longer, and the roadway sags lower over the water. In winter, the cables shrink and lift the deck back up. The difference is several metres. So the bridge you see today is not quite the same shape it will be in six months.

Each of those four cables is not one solid rope. It is a bundle of thousands of thin steel wires, spun into place strand by strand and then squeezed tight. The wire inside, laid end to end, would stretch an enormous distance across the globe. From where you stand, the cables look smooth and simple. Up close, they are a careful weave of countless small pieces working together.

The men who built it worked at dizzying heights, often in wind and cold. Many were ironworkers, including Mohawk workers from Canada and northern New York, who had a long tradition on high steel. The writer Gay Talese followed these crews and told their story in his book called The Bridge. Construction had a human cost too. Several workers died during the project. And in Bay Ridge, thousands of residents lost their homes to make room for the approach roads.

Now, the name. Giovanni da Verrazzano was an Italian explorer sailing for the King of France. In 1524, he became the first European known to enter New York Harbor, passing through these same waters. For decades, the bridge carried his name with a spelling mistake — only one letter z. Local Italian-American groups campaigned for years to fix it. In 2018, the official name was finally corrected to its proper double z.

The opening of the bridge changed Staten Island completely. Before 1964, much of the island was farmland, woods and small villages. Once a direct road connected it to Brooklyn, new houses spread quickly across the hills. The population grew sharply in the years that followed. If you ask older Staten Islanders, many will say their borough has two histories — before the bridge, and after it.

Once a year, the bridge belongs to people on foot. Every November, the New York City Marathon begins at the Staten Island end. Tens of thousands of runners pour across both decks in the first miles of the race. Helicopters circle, fireboats spray arcs of water, and the whole structure trembles under the rhythm of running feet. On every other day of the year, there is no walkway at all. The bridge carries cars, trucks and buses only.

That is why the best views come from the shore. On the Brooklyn side, a long waterfront path runs beside the water, with benches facing the towers. On the Staten Island side, the old military ground at Fort Wadsworth looks straight across the Narrows. At sunset, the light turns the towers soft and warm. After dark, a string of lights traces the curve of the cables, like a necklace laid across the harbour.

Watch the water for a moment. A container ship may be sliding out toward the Atlantic, impossibly large, yet small beneath the deck. A ferry may cross in the distance. Ammann designed this bridge for exactly this traffic below as much as for the traffic above. Ships still pass here day and night, just as Verrazzano's small wooden vessel once did.

Before you leave, listen again for that hum from above. It is the sound of a city moving between two of its boroughs, every minute of every day. Look at the towers one last time. They stand straight, yet they lean apart with the curve of the Earth itself. As you walk away, notice how the bridge stays in view for a long time — a quiet line drawn across the sky, marking the edge of the harbour and the start of the open sea.
