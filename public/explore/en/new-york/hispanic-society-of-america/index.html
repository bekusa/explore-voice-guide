The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic on Broadway fades a little as you step onto the stone terrace. Your shoes tap on smooth paving, and pale limestone walls rise on both sides, cool and grey even on a warm afternoon. In the middle of the courtyard, a bronze knight sits high on a rearing horse. He lifts a banner toward the sky. This is Audubon Terrace in upper Manhattan, and behind these quiet walls sits one of the largest collections of Spanish and Latin American art outside Spain.

The Hispanic Society of America began with one man and one obsession. His name was Archer Milton Huntington. He was the son of Collis Huntington, a nineteenth-century railroad tycoon, so money was never his problem. As a young man, Archer fell in love with Spain. He learned Spanish, travelled through the country, and studied its history and literature. He did not want only to admire that culture... he wanted to explain it to Americans. In 1904, he founded this institution to do exactly that.

The name tells you the scope. Huntington did not limit himself to Spain. He collected from Portugal, from Latin America, and from the Philippines — the wide world where Spanish and Portuguese culture travelled. The collection runs from ancient times to the twentieth century. You will find paintings and sculpture, but also textiles, ceramics, silver, furniture, maps, and manuscripts. It is less a single museum and more a portrait of a civilisation, gathered by one determined collector.

Now look around the terrace itself. The ground you stand on was once part of the estate of John James Audubon, the naturalist famous for painting the birds of North America. That is why this cluster of buildings carries his name. Huntington bought land here in the early twentieth century and planned a small cultural campus. Other institutions joined him over the years. Today, the American Academy of Arts and Letters still shares the terrace, so writers and artists have gathered on this same stone for generations.

The buildings follow the Beaux-Arts style. That is a grand, classical way of building, taught at the famous art school in Paris and popular in American cities around 1900. Notice the columns, the carved names, the balanced, symmetrical facades. The architect was Charles Pratt Huntington, a cousin of Archer. Here is a detail many visitors miss: this whole ensemble was built far from the museum district of Fifth Avenue. Huntington chose a quiet hilltop neighbourhood and bet that people would come to him.

Now return to that bronze knight. He is El Cid, the medieval Castilian warrior who became Spain's great national hero. The sculptor was Anna Hyatt Huntington, Archer's wife. She was one of the leading American sculptors of animals, and you can see her skill in the horse. Look at the tense muscles, the flared nostrils, the weight balanced on two legs. Other casts of this statue stand in Spain and elsewhere, but this one has guarded the courtyard for about a century.

Around the courtyard, look for more of her work. Animals in stone and bronze appear along the terrace, and reliefs show figures from Spanish history and legend. Together, husband and wife shaped this place. He brought the books and paintings. She brought the sculpture that greets you outside. It is easy to walk straight to the doors, but it is worth slowing down here. The terrace is part of the collection, not just the approach to it.

Inside, the main gallery is a surprise. It feels like a Spanish courtyard brought indoors, with dark red walls, carved wood, and light falling from above. On these walls hang names that fill the great museums of Europe. There are works by El Greco, the Greek-born painter who worked in Toledo. There are portraits by Diego Velázquez, court painter to the Spanish king. There are religious scenes by Zurbarán and Murillo. Many visitors are amazed to find such painters on a quiet street in Washington Heights.

One painting draws a crowd of its own. It is a portrait by Francisco de Goya of the Duchess of Alba, one of the most powerful and talked-about women in late eighteenth-century Spain. She stands in black, in a landscape, and points down at the ground. Look closely at the sand by her feet. Two words are written there — Solo Goya, meaning only Goya. Art historians still debate what the painter and the duchess meant to each other. The painting itself does not answer.

Then comes the room that many people remember most. Huntington asked the Spanish painter Joaquín Sorolla to paint the regions of Spain. Sorolla worked on the project for years in the 1910s, travelling from province to province. The result is a series of enormous canvases, often called Vision of Spain, that wrap around an entire room. You will see fishermen, dancers, farmers, and festival crowds. Sorolla loved sunlight, and the colours here are bright and warm. The paintings show Spain's regional life a little over a century ago.

The second half of the institution is quieter, but just as important. The Hispanic Society has a major research library. It holds rare books, manuscripts, maps, and documents from the Spanish and Portuguese world. Scholars come here to study texts that are hard to find anywhere else. Huntington himself was a scholar who translated Spanish poetry. For him, books and art belonged together. That is why the full name today is the Hispanic Society Museum and Library.

For years, much of the building was closed to the public for a long renovation. The museum reopened after that work, with refreshed galleries and better spaces for visitors. The neighbourhood around it has also changed. Washington Heights is now home to a large Latin American community, especially families with roots in the Dominican Republic. So a museum founded to explain Hispanic culture to New Yorkers now sits in one of the city's most Hispanic neighbourhoods. History has a way of completing a circle.

As you leave, walk to the edge of the terrace and look back. The limestone glows softly in the afternoon light. El Cid still lifts his banner over the courtyard. Behind you, Broadway carries buses, music from passing cars, and the voices of the street, many of them speaking Spanish. Just to the south, Trinity Church Cemetery climbs the hill, where Audubon himself is buried. Take a final moment here, between the old stone and the living city, before you step back into the rhythm of upper Manhattan.

Key facts

Founded in 1904 by collector Archer Milton Huntington.

Joaquín Sorolla painted the Vision of Spain mural series.
