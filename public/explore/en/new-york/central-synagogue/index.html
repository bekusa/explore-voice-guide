The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the corner of Lexington Avenue and 55th Street. Taxis brake and honk. Office workers hurry past with coffee in their hands. Then look up. Two slender towers rise above the traffic, each one capped with a rounded, bulb-shaped dome. The stone below them is warm and banded, brown and cream, like layers in a cake. In a district of glass and steel, this building looks as if it arrived from another century... and in a sense, it did.

This is Central Synagogue, home to one of the oldest Reform Jewish congregations in New York. Reform Judaism is a modern branch of the faith. It adapted ancient tradition to life in a changing world. The building opened in the early 1870s, after construction began around 1870. It is widely described as the oldest synagogue building in continuous use in New York State. Think about that for a moment. Generations of families have walked through these same doors for more than a century and a half.

The architect was Henry Fernbach, a German-born Jewish immigrant. He is often called the first prominent Jewish architect in the United States. Fernbach worked in a city that was growing at enormous speed. Much of his other work went into the cast-iron commercial buildings of lower Manhattan. Here, though, he had a very different task. He had to design a house of worship for a congregation that wanted to show its confidence, its roots, and its place in American life.

The style he chose is called Moorish Revival. The word Moorish refers to the Islamic architecture of medieval Spain, with its horseshoe arches, geometric patterns, and rich colour. In the nineteenth century, many Jewish communities in Europe and America adopted this look. Why? It pointed back to a golden age in Spain, when Jewish life and culture flourished for a time. It also set synagogues apart from the Gothic churches that filled every city. The design carried a quiet message: we have our own history.

Fernbach did not invent this look from nothing. Architectural historians often compare the building to the great Dohány Street Synagogue in Budapest, which was completed shortly before. You can see the family resemblance in the paired towers, the striped stone, and the round window at the centre of the facade. Look at that window now. It sits between the towers like a rose of carved stone. Below it, the arched doorways draw your eye downward, toward the street and toward the entrance.

The congregation itself has deep roots. It began with immigrants from Central Europe, many from Bohemia, who formed a community in the middle of the nineteenth century. Over time, it merged with another congregation, and the combined community took the name Central Synagogue. As the city pushed north, so did its people. Moving to what was then a quieter part of Manhattan was a bold step. Today the same corner sits in the heart of Midtown, surrounded by towers.

Now for something many visitors walking past never realise. In August 1998, this building nearly disappeared. Workers were carrying out a renovation, and a fire broke out on the roof. The flames spread quickly. The roof collapsed into the sanctuary below. Firefighters fought hard, and the outer walls survived. But the interior, with its painted surfaces and wooden details, suffered terrible damage. For a time, many people feared the building could not be saved at all.

The congregation decided to rebuild. Over the next few years, architects, painters, and craftspeople worked to bring the sanctuary back. They studied old photographs and surviving fragments of paint. They recreated the stencilled patterns that cover the walls and ceilings. The restored sanctuary was rededicated in 2001. So the room you may see inside today is both old and new. Its design belongs to the nineteenth century, while much of its paint and plaster belongs to our own time.

If you have the chance to step inside, the change is sudden. The noise of Lexington Avenue fades. The air feels still. Colour fills almost every surface. Deep blues, reds, and ochres climb the walls in repeating geometric patterns. Gold catches the light along the arches. Slim columns rise toward the ceiling, which is painted with stars and designs. The effect is less like a plain hall and more like stepping inside a decorated jewellery box.

At the front of the sanctuary stands the Ark. This is the cabinet that holds the Torah scrolls, the handwritten scrolls of the first five books of the Hebrew Bible. In any synagogue, the Ark is the focus of the room. Here it sits beneath an arch that echoes the shapes outside. As you look around, notice how the patterns on the walls never quite repeat in the same way twice. Each section has its own rhythm, like variations in a piece of music.

Here is a second detail that surprises people. Central Synagogue is not a museum. It is a working congregation with a busy calendar of services, study, music, and community events. Many of its services are also broadcast online, and people around the world follow them from home. So when you visit, you are not looking at a relic behind glass. You are standing in a place where people still gather each week for prayer and celebration.

The building has received formal recognition too. It was designated a New York City landmark in the 1960s, among the earlier buildings to earn that status. Later it was named a National Historic Landmark. That protection matters in a neighbourhood like this. All around you, older structures have given way to office towers. This one stayed. Its survival through demolition pressure, and then through fire, makes it a rare thread connecting today's Midtown to the city of the 1870s.

Before you move on, take one last look from across the street. Notice how the towers frame the sky between the taller buildings behind them. Watch the light move across the banded stone, warm in the afternoon and softer as evening comes. Listen again to the traffic, the voices, the sirens in the distance. Then picture this corner when the stone was fresh and the streets were quieter. The building is still here, still in use... and still keeping its own calm at the edge of the crowd.
