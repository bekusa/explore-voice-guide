The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement of West Forty-Fourth Street for a moment and listen. Taxi horns echo off the buildings. A doorman calls out to the line. Somewhere behind you, a street vendor rattles a cart of pretzels. Above you, a marquee throws warm light onto the faces of people waiting to go inside. This is the Majestic Theatre, and for nearly a century, this strip of sidewalk has been one of the most crowded waiting rooms in New York.

The theatre opened in 1927, near the end of a decade when Broadway was building at a furious pace. In those years, new playhouses appeared in the Times Square area almost every season. Producers needed stages, and investors saw theatre as a sound business. The Majestic arrived late in that boom, and it arrived big. From its first night, it was one of the largest playhouses on the street, and today it is still among the very largest Broadway houses by seat count, with well over 1,600 seats.

The man behind the project was Irwin Chanin, a builder and developer who later became famous for skyscrapers. Chanin had an idea that was unusual for his time. He disliked the way old theatres sorted people by class, with wealthy patrons below and everyone else pushed up into steep, distant galleries. He wanted cheaper seats to feel less like an afterthought. Here, that idea shaped the building itself.

The architect was Herbert J. Krapp, who designed a large share of the Broadway theatres that still stand today. If you walk this district, you will keep meeting his work. Krapp knew how to fit a big auditorium onto a narrow, expensive Manhattan lot. At the Majestic, he and Chanin used a layout sometimes described as stadium style. Instead of stacking several high balconies, the house relies on a long, sloping arrangement, so that many seats sit at a gentler distance from the stage.

Here is a detail most visitors never notice. Because of that layout, many audience members do not enter at the very bottom of the auditorium. They come in partway up the slope and walk down or up to their row. It feels ordinary once you are inside, but it was a deliberate design choice. The goal was to make the journey to a cheaper seat feel less like climbing to the attic.

The Majestic was not built alone. Chanin developed it as part of a group of theatres on this block, along with neighbouring playhouses and a hotel. Look along the street, and you can still sense how this corner was planned as one piece of entertainment real estate. The facades sit shoulder to shoulder, each with its own marquee, each competing for the eye of someone walking past.

Now think about what this stage has carried. In the middle of the twentieth century, the Majestic became a home for major musicals. Rodgers and Hammerstein's Carousel played here in the 1940s, and South Pacific followed at the end of that decade. Later came The Music Man and Camelot. In the 1970s, The Wiz brought a new energy to the house. Each of these shows filled the same seats you see through the doors today.

But for most living New Yorkers, one title is tied to this building above all others. The Phantom of the Opera opened here in 1988. It did not leave until 2023. By then, it had become the longest-running show in Broadway history, with more than 13,000 performances. Think about that span. Children who saw it on a school trip in the late 1980s could have brought their own children decades later, to the same theatre, to the same show.

That long run left marks on the building. The production famously used a large chandelier that dropped toward the audience, and the theatre had to support that effect night after night. Crews, ushers, and stagehands built entire careers inside these walls. Locals say some audience members returned dozens of times. Whether or not every story is exact, the feeling is real: for a whole generation, this address simply meant one musical.

When Phantom finally closed, the moment felt like the end of an era on this block. Crowds gathered outside for the last performances. The marquee changed for the first time in thirty-five years. Today, a theatre of this size is always in demand, because large musicals need deep stages, wide wings, and many seats to pay for themselves. The Majestic has those things, which is why big productions keep wanting to come here.

The building is protected. In the late 1980s, New York City designated the theatre as a landmark, covering both its exterior and its interior. That matters more than it might seem. Many grand old American theatres were torn down or gutted in the twentieth century. This one is still operated by the Shubert Organization, one of the oldest names in Broadway, and it still works as a living theatre rather than a museum.

If you have a few minutes before the doors open, take a short walk. Shubert Alley, a private pedestrian passage lined with show posters, is just a few steps east. Across the street and along the block, other historic theatres glow at the same hour. In the evening, the whole street fills with the sound of ushers, ticket scanners, and quick conversations in many languages. It is one of the best places in the city to simply watch people arrive.

If you are going inside, look around before the lights go down. Notice how the seats sweep back and upward, and how far, or how near, the stage feels from where you sit. Chanin's idea is still at work in that view. And if you are only passing by tonight, look up at the marquee once more. Thousands of people stood on this exact pavement, looking at this exact doorway... waiting for the curtain to rise.
