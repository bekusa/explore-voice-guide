The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, Times Square hums: taxi horns, the shuffle of thousands of feet, the low electric buzz of screens the height of buildings. In front of you, on West 45th Street, sits a doorway that looks almost modest. This is the entrance to the Minskoff Theatre. The theatre does not sit at street level, as most Broadway houses do. It floats above you, tucked inside a tall office tower.

That is the first surprise. Most of Broadway's playhouses were built in the early twentieth century. They are brick and plaster buildings, with ornate lobbies that open straight onto the pavement. The Minskoff belongs to a different age. It opened in 1973, and it was designed from the start as part of a skyscraper. To reach your seat, you ride escalators up through the building. You arrive in a lobby several floors above the street.

The tower around you is called One Astor Plaza. That name carries a memory. For decades, this corner held the Astor Hotel, a grand hotel that was a famous meeting place in the theatre district. Generations of New Yorkers dined and danced there. The hotel was demolished in the late 1960s. Many people mourned it, and its loss became part of a wider argument about what Times Square should become.

Here is the part most visitors never hear. In the late 1960s, New York faced a real fear. Developers wanted to replace old theatres with profitable office towers, and Broadway could slowly disappear. So the city created a special zoning rule for the theatre district. Developers could build taller towers if they included a new theatre inside. The Minskoff is one of the results of that bargain. In a sense, this room exists because of a planning law.

The theatre takes its name from the Minskoff family, the real estate developers behind the tower. That is unusual on Broadway. Many houses honour actors, producers, or playwrights. This one honours the builders who agreed to put a stage inside their skyscraper. It is a quiet reminder that Broadway has always been about business as much as art. Rent, land, and law shape what you see on stage.

When the doors first opened in 1973, the show was a revival of the musical Irene, starring the film actress Debbie Reynolds. It was a warm, old-fashioned musical comedy. That choice was fitting for a brand-new theatre. A modern building, made for an old tradition. In the decades that followed, the stage hosted revivals, concerts, and new musicals of many kinds.

One of the best-known productions here arrived in the 1990s. Andrew Lloyd Webber's Sunset Boulevard played at the Minskoff, with Glenn Close as Norma Desmond, the faded silent film star. The production was famous for its enormous set, a full mansion that moved on stage. A big theatre like this one can hold that kind of spectacle. Its stage and backstage spaces were built for large shows, not intimate dramas.

Then, in 2006, a new tenant moved in, and it has stayed for a very long time. The Lion King transferred here from the New Amsterdam Theatre, a few blocks away on 42nd Street. The Disney musical, directed by Julie Taymor, is known for its puppets and masks. Giraffes walk on tall stilts. Birds fly on poles. The opening song brings animals down the aisles, right past the audience.

That opening tells you something about this room. The Minskoff is wide and open, with more than 1,600 seats. The audience sits in a broad fan facing the stage. This shape lets a big procession move through the house and still be seen from almost everywhere. Many older theatres are tall and narrow, with steep balconies. Here the feeling is spacious and modern, closer to a concert hall than to a jewel box.

Now look at the lobby, if you go inside. The upper lobby has tall windows. From there you can look down on 45th Street and the theatre district below. It is a strange and lovely view. You stand above the crowd, with the lights of other marquees glowing around you. Before a show, people gather at the glass with drinks and watch the street fill with theatregoers. Few Broadway lobbies give you a view like this.

Step back outside and look along 45th Street. This block is one of the busiest theatre streets in the city. Older playhouses stand nearby, with brick facades, fire escapes, and bright marquees. Shubert Alley, a narrow private lane used by actors and audiences, is a short walk east. The contrast is worth noticing. The older theatres feel carved from another century. The Minskoff feels cut into the base of a modern tower.

In the evening, this street changes. Crowds form long lines under the marquees. Ushers call out. Families hold tickets and check the time. Street performers and photographers work the corners nearby. After the show, everyone pours out at once, and the pavement fills with voices humming songs they just heard. If you are standing here at curtain time, you are part of one of the oldest rituals in this city.

So take one more look up at the tower above the doorway. Somewhere behind that glass and stone, a stage waits for tonight's performance. A hotel once stood here. A law helped save a tradition. And a theatre was lifted into the sky. As you walk away toward the lights of Times Square, listen again. Under all the noise, Broadway is still getting ready for the show.
