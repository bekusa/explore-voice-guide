The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Outside, there is the hiss of fountains and the shouts of football games across the grass. Then the doors close behind you, and the air goes cool and still. Somewhere ahead, under soft lights, an entire city is waiting, small enough to see in one look. This is the Queens Museum, inside Flushing Meadows Corona Park. Few buildings in New York have changed roles as often as this one.

Start with the building itself. Its long, pale facade, with its rows of columns, was not built as a museum. It went up for the 1939 New York World's Fair as the New York City Building, the city's own pavilion. The style is clean and stripped-down, typical of public architecture from the 1930s. It is classical in spirit, with almost no ornament. It was meant to look official and lasting, and it has lasted. It is one of the few structures from that fair still standing.

Now the first surprise. After the Second World War, this building briefly hosted the world. From 1946 until 1950, the General Assembly of the newly formed United Nations met here. The permanent headquarters in Manhattan were not finished yet. So delegates from dozens of nations came out to a former fairground in Queens to argue, vote and plan the postwar order. Several important early decisions were debated in these rooms. Most visitors walking in today have no idea.

When the diplomats left, the building went back to the city and to ordinary life. For years, part of it held an ice skating rink, and at times a roller rink. Families came here to skate on weekend afternoons. Then, in 1964, a second World's Fair arrived in the same park. The building became a city pavilion once again. For that fair, it received the object that still draws most people through these doors.

That object is the Panorama of the City of New York. Walk to the railing and look down. Here is every borough, laid out at a scale of one inch to one hundred feet. There are bridges, piers, parks, rail yards and highways. The model contains hundreds of thousands of individual buildings, each one shaped by hand. The urban planner Robert Moses commissioned it for the 1964 fair, and a firm of model makers built it.

Take your time with it, because the eye needs a few minutes to adjust. Find the green rectangle of Central Park first. From there, the grid of Manhattan becomes readable. Look south to the cluster of towers at the tip of the island. Then follow the rivers outward, into Brooklyn, Staten Island, the Bronx, and finally Queens. Many visitors end up hunting for their own street, or the neighbourhood where a grandparent once lived.

Here is the second surprise. The lighting over the Panorama changes. Every so often, the room slowly dims, and the model city passes from day into night. Tiny lights glow along the streets and in the buildings. Small model planes move toward the airports. It is a simple effect, but it changes how the city feels. In daylight it looks like a plan. In darkness it starts to look like a place where people live.

The model is also a historical document, and that is easy to miss. It shows New York as it was when it was built, and as it was when it was last updated. It received a major update in the early 1990s. Since then, the real skyline has kept changing, while this one has mostly stood still. So you are looking at a snapshot of an earlier city, not quite the one outside today.

Step away from the Panorama, and the museum offers another link to this borough. Queens has a strong connection to Louis Comfort Tiffany, the American designer famous for coloured glass. His glass furnaces stood not far from here, in the neighbourhood of Corona. The museum has a display of Tiffany glass, including lamps and other pieces. Look at how the colours deepen when light passes through them. Workers in Queens once made glass like this.

You will also find a large relief map of New York City's water supply system. It traces the long route that brings water from upstate reservoirs into the city's taps. This is not a glamorous subject, but it is a fascinating one. Every glass of water in an apartment in Manhattan depends on that network. Seeing it at this scale helps you understand how far a city reaches beyond its own edges.

The museum itself was founded in the early 1970s. Since then it has become closely tied to the people around it. Queens is one of the most linguistically diverse places on earth. The neighbourhoods near this park are home to families from Latin America, Asia, the Caribbean and many other regions. The museum's exhibitions often reflect that mix. Contemporary art here is frequently about migration, language, labour and community, and much of it is made by local artists.

After a major renovation, the building reopened in the 2010s with more space. The old skating rink area became part of the museum. Notice the large central gallery and the skylights above it. Natural light falls across the floor, and the sounds of the park sometimes come in faintly. The building keeps its civic, slightly formal bones. Inside, though, it feels open and informal. Children run between the exhibits, and grandparents point at the model below.

When you leave, pause on the steps outside. Directly in front of you stands the Unisphere, the giant steel globe built for the 1964 fair. On a warm day, its fountains spray water around its base. Behind you is the building where the world's diplomats once met. Somewhere inside is the whole city in miniature. Take one last look, then walk back out into the real one, the city that model was made to show.
