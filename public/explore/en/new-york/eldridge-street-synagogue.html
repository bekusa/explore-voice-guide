The guide

The story

Stand on the sidewalk of Eldridge Street for a moment and listen. Delivery carts rattle past. Someone calls out in Cantonese from a shop doorway. The smell of roasted pork and dried mushrooms drifts from a nearby storefront. Then you look up, and between the tenements a tall brick and terracotta facade rises, crowned by a great round window. It does not look like anything else on the block. It was never meant to.

This is the Eldridge Street Synagogue. It opened in 1887, and it was the first great house of worship built in the United States by Jews from Eastern Europe. Earlier Jewish immigrants to New York had mostly come from Germany and Central Europe. The newcomers here came from the villages and towns of what is now Poland, Lithuania, Ukraine and Russia. Many arrived with almost nothing. And yet, within a few years, they built this.

The congregation behind it was called Kahal Adath Jeshurun. They hired the architects Peter and Francis Herter, two brothers better known for designing tenements in the same neighbourhood. That detail surprises many visitors. The men who drew the crowded apartment buildings where immigrants lived also drew the grand building where they prayed. The contrast was the whole point. Home might be one dark room, but the synagogue would be full of light.

Look at the front of the building. The style mixes several traditions. There are horseshoe arches and small domed finials in the Moorish Revival manner, a style inspired by the Islamic architecture of medieval Spain. There are round Romanesque arches, the heavy, rounded style of European churches from around a thousand years ago. There are also touches of Gothic detail. In the late nineteenth century, many American synagogues used this blend to create a look that felt distinct, dignified and clearly Jewish.

Notice too that the building sits directly among the tenements. It has no plaza and no grand approach. The congregation built on an ordinary narrow lot, squeezed between neighbours. You come upon it suddenly, the way the original worshippers did, stepping off a crowded street and into another world. That sense of arrival is still part of the experience today.

Inside, the main sanctuary rises to a high painted ceiling. Brass chandeliers hang above the pews. They were first lit by gas and later converted to electricity. The walls are covered in painted decoration, including trompe l'oeil, a French term for painting that tricks the eye into seeing depth or texture that is not really there. Some surfaces that look like marble or carved stone are actually flat plaster and paint.

At the eastern end stands the ark, the cabinet that holds the Torah scrolls. It is made of carved wood and faces toward Jerusalem, as synagogue arks traditionally do. In the centre of the room stands the bimah, the raised platform where the Torah is read aloud. Placing it in the middle, rather than at the front, follows Orthodox practice. The whole room is arranged around the reading of the text.

Look up at the balcony that wraps around three sides. That was the women's gallery. In Orthodox tradition, men and women prayed separately, and the women sat above. From up there, the view down into the sanctuary is wide and open. Many visitors find the balcony the best place to understand the scale of the room and the care that went into every painted surface.

For several decades, this sanctuary was packed, especially on the High Holidays, the autumn festivals of Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. But the neighbourhood changed. Many families prospered and moved to Brooklyn, the Bronx and beyond. In 1924, new United States laws sharply limited immigration from Eastern Europe. Then came the Great Depression. The congregation shrank, and the great hall became too costly to heat and repair.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. By the 1950s, the small remaining congregation stopped using the main sanctuary altogether. They moved downstairs to a modest study and prayer room in the lower level. For years, the grand hall upstairs sat closed. Dust gathered, plaster cracked, and according to accounts from those who reopened it, pigeons had found their way inside. Yet the faithful kept praying below, and the building never stopped being a synagogue.

In the late twentieth century, preservationists and community members began a long effort to save it. The building was named a National Historic Landmark, and the restoration took around twenty years. It was completed in 2007. Today the building houses the Museum at Eldridge Street, and a congregation still holds services here. The restorers chose to leave some areas showing their age, so you can see the difference between repaired and original surfaces.

Now look again at that great round window on the eastern wall. The original window did not survive. In 2010, a new one was installed, designed by the artist Kiki Smith with the architect Deborah Gans. It is a field of deep blue glass scattered with golden stars. It is a contemporary work set inside a nineteenth-century room, and it does not try to copy the past. Many people find that it fits the space precisely because it is honest about being new.

The streets outside tell the next chapter. The Lower East Side was once one of the most crowded immigrant neighbourhoods on earth. Today this block is part of Chinatown, home to a newer wave of families building their lives in the same narrow streets. The synagogue stands as a reminder that this corner of the city has always been a place of arrival, of starting over, and of making something beautiful with very little.

Before you leave, pause on the sidewalk once more. Look at the worn steps, the arched doors, the stars of that window catching the light. Then turn and look at the tenements around you, at the shop signs and the people hurrying past. The building has watched this street change for well over a century... and it is still here, still open, still waiting for the next person to step inside.
