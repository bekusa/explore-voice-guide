The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement of West 44th Street and listen for a moment. Taxis hiss past. A doorman calls out to the line forming under the marquee. Somewhere above you, lights flicker on in a long row, one bulb after another, and the brick front of the Saint James Theatre turns warm in the evening glow. This is a working stage, not a museum. Tonight, like most nights, people will walk through these doors to see a show.

The theatre opened in 1927, near the height of a building boom in Times Square. In those years, new playhouses appeared on these side streets almost every season. This one carried a different name at first. It was called Erlanger's Theatre, after Abraham Erlanger, a powerful producer who had spent decades shaping how American theatre was booked and sold. He wanted a large house that would carry his own name into the future.

Erlanger was a hard figure to love. He was part of a group of businessmen often called the Theatrical Syndicate, who controlled many stages across the country around the turn of the twentieth century. Actors and rival producers complained that the Syndicate decided who could perform, and where. By the time this building rose, his influence had faded. Still, he gave the street a handsome theatre — and that part of his legacy is still standing here.

The design came from the firm of Warren and Wetmore. If that name sounds familiar, it should. The same architects worked on Grand Central Terminal, a few blocks to the east. Here they chose a calmer style. The facade draws on Georgian architecture — the restrained, symmetrical style of eighteenth-century Britain. Look at the brick, the tall arched openings, and the orderly rhythm of the windows. It feels more like a London townhouse than a Broadway palace.

That British feeling connects to the name. After Erlanger died in 1930, the theatre changed hands, and in the early 1930s it was renamed the Saint James. The new name pointed across the Atlantic, to the Saint James's Theatre in London, a famous West End house. So when you say the name today, you are carrying a small echo of the London stage onto a New York street corner.

Now for a surprise most visitors miss. The Saint James is one of the larger theatres on Broadway. It holds well over a thousand people, spread across an orchestra level and a balcony. Its size made it a natural home for big musicals, with large casts, orchestras in the pit, and heavy scenery. Many of the shows that opened here needed exactly this kind of room to breathe.

The most important of those shows arrived in 1943. Its name was Oklahoma!, the first collaboration between the composer Richard Rodgers and the writer Oscar Hammerstein the Second. It ran for years. Historians often point to it as a turning point, because its songs and dances helped tell the story, instead of simply stopping the plot for entertainment. Much of what audiences now expect from a Broadway musical traces back to that opening night on this stage.

Rodgers and Hammerstein came back. In 1951, their musical The King and I opened here, with Gertrude Lawrence and Yul Brynner in the leading roles. Brynner would play the King for much of his life. Then, in 1964, came Hello, Dolly!, with Carol Channing walking down a staircase as the title song played. That moment became one of the best-remembered entrances in Broadway history. All three shows began their stories inside these walls.

The stage kept its reputation in the decades that followed. In 2001, Mel Brooks brought The Producers here, a loud, gleeful comedy about two men who try to stage a flop on purpose. It became one of the most talked-about shows of its time. Later, in 2018, a stage version of the Disney film Frozen opened at the Saint James. The theatre has never belonged to one kind of audience. Grandparents, critics, and children have all filled these seats.

Ownership tells another quiet story. Since 1970, the theatre has been part of the Jujamcyn group, a smaller rival to the larger theatre owners on Broadway. Jujamcyn was known for supporting new work, and its few houses gained a strong name among writers and producers. In the 2020s, the group joined with the Ambassador Theatre Group, a British company. In a way, the London connection in the name has come full circle.

The building is also protected. New York City has designated the Saint James as a landmark, which means changes to its historic fabric are carefully controlled. Inside, the auditorium keeps much of its decorative plasterwork, painted in soft, rich colours, with curved balcony fronts and ornamented ceilings. If you have a ticket, take a moment before the lights go down. Look up, not just at the stage.

From where you stand, notice how the theatre fits into the block. The street is lined with other playhouses, each with its own marquee and its own crowd. Just to the east lies Shubert Alley, a short pedestrian passage where actors and fans have crossed paths for generations. On a show night, the whole street feels like one long lobby. People wait, check their phones, and look for friends in the crowd.

After a performance, a small ritual often plays out here. Fans gather near the stage door, hoping to see the cast leave and perhaps sign a programme. It is informal, a little chaotic, and very old. Audiences have done the same thing on these sidewalks for about a century. If you stay, you become part of that long tradition, even if only for a few minutes.

Before you move on, look once more at the brick front and the lights above the door. This building has watched the musical change shape, again and again. It saw Oklahoma! begin, and a snow queen sing. Tomorrow it will open its doors again. As you walk toward the bright glow of Times Square, listen to the sound of the street behind you — the voices, the traffic, the steady hum of a theatre district still at work.
