The guide

The story

Stand on the corner of Sixth Avenue and Fiftieth Street and look up. A red neon marquee runs along the building, and its letters hum faintly even in daylight. Taxis hiss past. The air carries roasted nuts from a street cart, and somewhere a bus brakes with a long sigh. Above the marquee, the limestone facade rises in clean, plain lines. This is Radio City Music Hall, and the quiet outside gives no hint of the size of the room inside.

The hall opened in December 1932, in the middle of the Great Depression. That timing matters. Banks were failing and unemployment was high, yet the Rockefeller family pushed ahead with a new complex of towers in Midtown Manhattan. This theatre was one of its first public faces. It was built to be the largest indoor theatre in the world at the time, a place where ordinary people could buy a ticket and sit inside something grand.

Why the name Radio City? The answer is in the tenants. The Radio Corporation of America, known as RCA, was a major partner in the new complex. Radio was the exciting technology of the age, the way the internet felt in the 1990s. People began calling the whole development Radio City, and the name stayed with the music hall long after radio stopped being new. So the name you see on the marquee is really a small time capsule.

The driving force behind the hall was a showman named Samuel Rothafel, known to everyone simply as Roxy. He had already run famous movie palaces, and he wanted this one to be bigger and more modern than any of them. The architecture team for Rockefeller Center shaped the building, and the young designer Donald Deskey led the interior design. Deskey won that job in a competition, and the result became one of the clearest expressions of American Art Deco.

Art Deco, if the term is new to you, is a style from the 1920s and 1930s. It loves bold geometry, smooth curves, shiny metals, and a sense of speed and optimism. Inside this hall, you see it everywhere — in the light fixtures, the patterned carpets, the polished railings, and the furniture in the lounges. Deskey used new materials of his time, like aluminium and early plastics, next to traditional wood and marble.

The Grand Foyer is the first surprise for most visitors. It rises several storeys high, with tall mirrors, long gold-toned walls, and two enormous chandeliers. A sweeping staircase climbs toward the mezzanines. Above the stairs hangs a large mural by the painter Ezra Winter, called The Fountain of Youth. It shows an old man looking toward a mountain and a source of eternal life. The message was simple — come inside, and forget your troubles for a few hours.

Then comes the auditorium itself. It holds roughly six thousand seats. The ceiling is a series of huge semicircular bands that step outward from the stage, like ripples. Lights hidden between the bands can wash the whole room in changing colours. Roxy liked to tell a story about this design. According to the popular tale, he got the idea while watching a sunset from the deck of a ship at sea. Whether that is exact or not, the room does feel like a sky.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. The stage sits on a system of hydraulic lifts that can raise and lower whole sections, along with performers, scenery, and even an orchestra. For the 1930s, this machinery was remarkably advanced. It is often said that engineers used similar hydraulic ideas on American aircraft carriers during the Second World War. Whatever the exact link, the stage was built for spectacle on a scale few theatres could match.

And spectacle brings us to the Rockettes. This precision dance troupe began outside New York, and it came to the hall soon after opening. Their trademark is the eye-high kick, performed in a perfectly straight line by dozens of dancers moving as one body. Since the early 1930s, they have been the stars of the Christmas Spectacular, a holiday show that has become a seasonal tradition for many New York families. For generations of visitors, Radio City and the Rockettes mean the same thing.

The first night, by most accounts, did not go to plan. Roxy staged a long variety evening with many acts, and the hall was so large that some performances seemed to disappear in it. The show ran very late, and critics were not kind. Within weeks, the theatre changed course and began showing films with live stage shows in between. For decades after that, it was one of the most important places in America for a big movie premiere.

Here is the second surprise. In the late 1970s, this building came close to closing for good. Movie audiences had moved to smaller cinemas, and the hall was losing money. There was serious talk of shutting it down. New Yorkers protested, and the city gave the interior official landmark protection. That decision helped save it. Later, at the end of the 1990s, a major restoration brought back the original colours, fabrics, and finishes you see today.

Since then, the hall has hosted concerts, comedy nights, television award shows, and graduations, alongside the holiday show. Many of the most famous names in music have played on this stage. Yet the building still feels like Roxy's idea of a palace for everyone. Ticket holders in the highest seats see the same glowing ceiling as people in the front rows. That sense of shared wonder was the point from the very beginning.

Before you go, step back onto Fiftieth Street and look at the wall above the side entrances. Three large round plaques sit there, made of metal and enamel, representing dance, drama, and song. They were designed by the artist Hildreth Meière, and they are easy to walk past without noticing. Look at the colours in the sunlight, then glance back at the red marquee. Listen to the street again — the traffic, the voices, the doors opening. The show, in a way, is still going on.

Reserve Christmas Spectacular seats well ahead of the holidays.
Photograph the marquee at dusk when the neon glows brightest.
