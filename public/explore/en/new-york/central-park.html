The guide

The story

In the middle of the densest part of the city, the buildings stop and a vast green rectangle opens out — lawns, woods, lakes, and winding paths, ringed all around by a wall of skyscrapers. This is Central Park, the great green heart of Manhattan, and one of the most visited parks in the world.

The first surprise is that none of it is natural. The whole landscape — the hills, lakes, meadows, and woods — was designed and built in the second half of the nineteenth century on what was then rough, swampy ground, by the designers Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux. Every view was planned.

Their idea was radical for the time: a great park open to all, rich and poor alike, in a fast-growing city, a place where ordinary people could escape the crowded streets into something like countryside. That democratic purpose still shapes how the park feels and is used.

The park is large, far larger than it looks on a map, stretching for many blocks up the middle of the island. You cannot see it all in one visit, so most people pick a section, and the southern half holds many of the best-known features.

Among them is the Mall, a straight, tree-lined walk that opens onto Bethesda Terrace and its fountain, looking over the lake, one of the most beautiful built spaces in the park. Nearby a graceful iron bridge arches over the water, a favourite of photographers.

The park is full of distinct places with their own moods. There are open meadows for lying in the sun, a wooded ramble laid out to feel wild, a large reservoir ringed by a running path, a castle-like lookout, and a quiet garden corner dedicated to the memory of a famous musician.

It changes completely with the seasons. Spring blossom, deep summer green, the fierce colours of autumn, and snow in winter each remake the park, and locals return through the year to see the same favourite spots transformed.

It is, above all, a working park for the city. On a fine day it fills with joggers, cyclists, picnickers, ballplayers, buskers, and families, far more locals than tourists in many corners. Watching the city at leisure is part of the pleasure of being here.

The skyline is always present. Wherever you are in the park, the towers rise beyond the trees, so you never quite forget the city around you; the contrast between green and glass is part of what makes the place feel so striking.

There is plenty laid on beyond the landscape: a zoo, boating on the lake, an open-air theatre, ice skating in winter, and grand museums lining its edges, so the park anchors a whole day's worth of things to do.

A few practical notes. The park is free and open daily; pick a section rather than trying to cross it all, wear comfortable shoes, and use the numbered entrances and signs to find your way. It is large, so a map or a planned route helps.

Find a bench or a patch of lawn, sit, and watch the city play for a while, with the towers rising beyond the trees. A century and a half ago, people chose to leave this great green space open in the middle of a money-making city. When you step back onto the avenue, the gift of that choice is obvious.
