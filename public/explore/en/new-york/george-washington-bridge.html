The guide

The story

Listen first. Above you, a low, steady hum rolls across the river. It is the sound of thousands of tyres moving over steel, day and night, without a break. The wind comes up the Hudson and pushes against your jacket. Far overhead, the great grey towers rise into the sky, and their open steel lattice lets the light pass straight through. This is the George Washington Bridge, and it is never truly quiet.

The bridge connects two very different shores. On the east side is Washington Heights, at the northern end of Manhattan, a neighbourhood of apartment blocks, bakeries and Spanish heard on every corner. On the west side is Fort Lee, New Jersey, perched on top of the Palisades. Those are the tall, dark cliffs of rock that line the river's western bank. Between them, the Hudson runs wide and grey, carrying barges and the occasional sailboat south toward the harbour.

The bridge opened in 1931, in the early years of the Great Depression. Its chief engineer was Othmar Ammann, a Swiss-born engineer who worked for the Port Authority of New York. Ammann had a bold idea. He believed a suspension bridge could cross the Hudson in one single leap, with no supports standing in the water. Many experts doubted him. When the bridge opened, its main span was roughly twice as long as any suspension span in the world before it.

Think about what that meant at the time. For a few years, this was the longest suspension span on Earth. It held that record until the Golden Gate Bridge opened in San Francisco in 1937. Ammann did not stop here. Decades later, he also designed the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge at the mouth of New York Harbor. In a sense, this bridge was the first chapter of a career that shaped how the whole city is connected.

Now look closely at the towers. Here is the first surprise. They were never meant to look like this. The architect Cass Gilbert, who also designed the Woolworth Building downtown, planned to cover the steel frames in concrete and granite. The towers would have looked like heavy stone monuments. But money was short in the Depression years, and the cladding was never added. Many New Yorkers came to love the bare steel. The skeleton became the finished face.

That choice won some famous admirers. The Swiss-French architect Le Corbusier, one of the founders of modern architecture, praised this bridge as perhaps the most beautiful in the world. He saw honesty in the exposed steel. Nothing was hidden. You can see how every beam carries weight, how the cross-bracing climbs in repeated patterns, how the whole tower is built from simple, logical pieces. It is engineering you can read with your eyes.

Follow the great cables with your gaze. They sweep down from the tops of the towers, dip low over the middle of the river, and rise again. The road deck hangs from them by vertical steel ropes called suspenders. Each main cable is made of thousands of thin steel wires bundled tightly together. At each end, the cables are anchored deep into the ground. On the New Jersey side, they are fixed into the solid rock of the Palisades.

The bridge you see today has two levels. When it opened, there was only the upper deck. Ammann designed the structure to be strong enough for a second level from the very beginning. That lower deck was finally added in 1962, and many drivers call it the Martha Washington, a joke about George's other half. Today, the bridge carries more motor vehicles than almost any other bridge on the planet. It is often described as the busiest in the world.

The name has deep roots in this exact spot. During the American Revolutionary War, two forts faced each other across the river here. Fort Washington stood on the Manhattan heights, and Fort Lee stood on the New Jersey cliffs. Together, they were meant to stop British ships from sailing up the Hudson. In November 1776, British forces captured Fort Washington. It was one of the worst American defeats of the war. The bridge's name remembers the general, and the ground remembers the battle.

Here is the second surprise, and it is easy to miss. Look down at the base of the Manhattan tower, at the edge of the water. There is a small red lighthouse standing there, tiny beside the giant steel legs. It is often called the Little Red Lighthouse. When the bridge was built, the lighthouse seemed no longer needed, and there was talk of removing it. A children's book changed its fate.

That book, published in 1942, is called The Little Red Lighthouse and the Great Gray Bridge. In the story, the lighthouse feels small and useless next to the huge new bridge, until one stormy night it is needed again. Children across the country loved it. When the lighthouse faced removal, people wrote letters and spoke up to save it. Today it still stands in Fort Washington Park, a small red figure with a large following.

If you visit on certain national holidays, look toward the New Jersey tower. On those days, an enormous American flag hangs from it, moving slowly in the river wind. It is one of the largest free-flying American flags in the world. Even on ordinary days, the bridge has a strong presence in city life. Cyclists cross on the south walkway. Joggers climb the approach. Commuters crawl through the morning traffic below.

Take a final moment where you are standing. Notice the colour of the steel against the sky, that soft grey that changes with the weather. Listen again to the hum of the traffic and the sound of the wind in the cables. As you walk away, look back once more. Watch how the towers line up, and how the cables draw a long, gentle curve over the water... one clean line, joining two shores.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk or cycle the south side pedestrian path for river views.
Visit Fort Washington Park to see the bridge from below.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed walkway mid-span.
Come on a national holiday to see the giant flag.
Stand on the Palisades in Fort Lee for side views.
