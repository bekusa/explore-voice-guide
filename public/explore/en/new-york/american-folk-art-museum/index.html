The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you look. Outside, Columbus Avenue carries a steady stream of taxis and buses. Across the street, people cross the plaza of Lincoln Center with instrument cases and shopping bags. Then you step through the glass doors, and the noise drops away. The room is compact and quiet. The light is soft. On the walls you may find a stitched quilt, a carved figure, or a painting made by someone who never went to art school.

That last point is the key to this museum. The American Folk Art Museum is devoted to art made by people outside the official art world. These include farmers, sailors, homemakers, sign painters, and preachers. Some were trained in a craft tradition passed down through families. Others taught themselves entirely. What links them is not a style. It is a way of working, with whatever materials were at hand and with a reason that mattered to the maker.

The museum began in 1961, under a longer name that included the words early American folk arts. In those first years, the focus was on objects from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. These were weathervanes, shop signs, painted furniture, samplers, and portraits of rural families. At that time, many serious collectors still treated such things as charming decoration rather than art. The founders made a different argument. They said these objects deserved the same careful looking as anything in a grand gallery uptown.

Over the decades, the museum widened its view. Today the collection also includes work by twentieth and twenty-first century artists who are often called self-taught or outsider artists. The term outsider art describes work made by people with no formal training, sometimes working in isolation, sometimes in hospitals or prisons. The museum treats these makers not as curiosities but as artists. That shift in language, and in respect, is one of the quiet achievements of this institution.

Here is something many visitors do not know. For about a decade, the museum had a much larger home on West Fifty-third Street, right beside the Museum of Modern Art. It opened in 2001. The architects Tod Williams and Billie Tsien designed it, and its front was covered in panels of a cast bronze alloy with a rough, hand-made surface. Architects admired it widely. The building looked like an object made by a craftsman, which suited the collection inside.

The story did not end well. The new building brought heavy debt, and in 2011 the museum sold it to its neighbour, the Museum of Modern Art. A few years later, that building was demolished to make room for an expansion. Many architects and critics protested, but the decision stood. So the small space you are standing in now is, in a real sense, the place where the museum survived. It kept its collection, its staff, and its mission.

Now look at the work itself. If a quilt is on view, step close. Follow a single line of stitching with your eyes. Each stitch was made by hand, often by many hands at a quilting gathering. The fabrics were frequently cut from old dresses, shirts, and feed sacks. So a quilt is not only a design. It is also a record of the clothes a family wore and the cloth they could not afford to waste.

One of the best-loved paintings in the collection is a portrait by Ammi Phillips, a travelling painter of the nineteenth century. It shows a young girl in a bright red dress, with a cat in her arms and a small dog at her feet. Phillips moved from town to town, painting families in their own homes. Notice the flat background and the strong, simple shapes. This was not a lack of skill. It was a style that gave his sitters clarity and calm dignity.

The museum is also closely linked with Henry Darger, a Chicago hospital janitor who lived a very private life in the twentieth century. After his death, people found an enormous illustrated story he had made in his room over many years. It filled thousands of pages, with large watercolour panoramas of children, storms, and battles. The museum holds one of the most important public collections of his work, and it supports a study centre for researchers.

Another name to know is Martín Ramírez, a Mexican-born artist who made most of his drawings while living in a psychiatric hospital in California. He worked on sheets of paper glued together, and he drew with whatever he could find. His pictures are full of repeating lines, tunnels, trains, and horse riders. If one of his works is on display, stand back first. Then come close and look at how the lines build up, layer on layer, like the grooves on a record.

Because the galleries are small, the exhibitions here change often. One season might focus on a single artist. Another might bring together objects around one theme, such as faith, work, or memory. This means your visit is a little like opening a letter. You cannot know exactly what you will find. Take time to read the wall labels. They often tell you who made a piece, why they made it, and what materials they used.

Before you leave, visit the museum shop near the entrance. It usually carries books on folk and self-taught art, along with objects made by contemporary craftspeople. It is a good place to continue the conversation you started in the galleries. And notice the location too. You are directly across from one of the great performing arts centres in the world. Opera and ballet happen on one side of the street. Hand-made art from ordinary lives fills this side.

When you step back out onto Columbus Avenue, the noise will return. Taxis, footsteps, a siren somewhere further uptown. But perhaps you will look at the city a little differently now. A painted sign above a shop, a carved bench in a park, a mural on a delivery truck... These are made by people who simply had something to say. Carry that eye with you as you walk. The next work of folk art may be just around the corner.
