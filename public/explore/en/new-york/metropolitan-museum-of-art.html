The story

The Met's size can make it feel timeless, but its history is specific. The museum was incorporated on 13 April 1870, opened to the public soon after, and acquired its first object, a Roman sarcophagus, in November 1870. Its current Fifth Avenue site opened in 1880 and has expanded around the original structure many times.

The official Met summary places the collection at more than 1.5 million objects spanning over 5,000 years of global history. That is the safest figure to use. Older or exaggerated claims about two million objects or precise annual visitors should be avoided unless tied to a current Met source.

The best main-text story is not that The Met has everything, but that it turns New York into a route through many civilizations and media: Egyptian temples, European painting, Islamic tilework, Asian scrolls, American rooms, musical instruments, arms and armor, fashion, and contemporary display.

Use the Great Hall as a threshold. From there, every turn is a choice about scale: a whole temple in one gallery, a tiny blue hippo in a case, a room moved from Damascus, a painting that crossed an ocean. The museum rewards visitors who choose a few strong paths and let the rest wait for another day.

The Met should be described as an encyclopedic museum, but that word needs explanation. It does not mean that every culture is represented equally or completely. It means the institution is organized around the idea that art, design, ritual, power, trade, and daily life can be compared across many places and periods.

Its New York identity also matters. Founded in the nineteenth century, The Met grew alongside the city's wealth, philanthropy, collecting networks, and public ambition. The museum became a way for New York to claim cultural seriousness on an international scale, while also building collections through gifts, purchases, excavations, and changing museum practice.

Move through The Met by scale. Some rooms reconstruct entire environments, such as period rooms or architectural interiors. Others ask you to focus on tiny objects, surfaces, inscriptions, brushwork, metalwork, or fabric. The museum is at its best when you let those scales alternate instead of looking only for famous masterpieces.

Be cautious with claims about what is always on view. The Met rotates works, lends objects, renovates galleries, and changes installations. A durable guide should explain the institution's structure and history, then invite visitors to verify the current display through official gallery information.
