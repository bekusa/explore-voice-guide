The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on West 44th Street and listen. Taxi horns echo off the brick. A street vendor calls out. Somewhere a stage door opens and voices drift out, then fade. Overhead, the marquee lights glow even in daylight. You are on one of the most theatre-packed blocks in New York, and the building in front of you is the Shubert Theatre.

Look up at the facade. It is not a plain box with a sign on it. The front is designed in a Venetian Renaissance style, which means it borrows the look of palaces in Venice, with decorative panels, curved details, and warm colours. Many of those panels use a technique called sgraffito, where layers of coloured plaster are scratched through to make a pattern. Most people hurry past without noticing it. Stop and look closely. It has the feel of an old Italian street.

The theatre opened in 1913, a few years before the First World War. The architect was Henry B. Herts, a specialist in theatres. He designed it as a pair with its neighbour, the Booth Theatre, just to the side. The two buildings were planned together and share a similar style. Seen together, they make one long front of pale brick and ornament along the street.

Now for the story behind the name, and it is a sad one. The Shubert family came from humble beginnings and built a theatre business from almost nothing. There were three brothers: Sam, Lee, and J. J. Sam was the driving force in the early years. In 1905 he died after a train accident, while still a young man. His brothers carried on. When they opened this theatre, they named it after him. So the name above the door is a memorial to a lost brother.

Lee and J. J. Shubert went on to build one of the largest theatre empires in American history. At their peak, they controlled a large share of the playhouses on Broadway and many more across the country. Here is a detail most visitors miss. The offices of the Shubert Organization, the company that grew from that family business, sit in this same building. The people who run many Broadway theatres work in the rooms above you.

Turn now toward the narrow passage beside the theatre. This is Shubert Alley. It runs between 44th Street and 45th Street. It is a private lane, not a public street, and it began as a practical passage between the buildings. Over the decades it became something more. Actors once walked here hoping to be seen by producers. Posters for current shows line the walls. Today it is a short cut, a meeting place, and a small stage of its own.

On a busy evening, this alley fills with people. Groups wait for friends. Couples check their tickets. Fans gather near the stage doors hoping for an autograph after the show. On special days, the alley also hosts charity events for the theatre community. Even on a quiet morning, the walls of posters show you what is playing now. It is a good place to feel the daily life of Broadway, not just its history.

Inside, the theatre is a classic Broadway house. The auditorium has a large orchestra floor and balconies that curve around toward the stage. The decoration follows the same Italian theme as the outside, with painted panels and gilded detail. The space feels grand, but it also feels close. Even from the upper levels, you are not far from the performers. That closeness is one reason actors and audiences have loved this room for more than a century.

The stage has held a long list of famous productions. In 1939, Katharine Hepburn starred here in The Philadelphia Story, a play that helped revive her career. In the 1950s, Judy Holliday lit up the stage in the musical Bells Are Ringing. Later came Promises, Promises, with songs by Burt Bacharach. Each show left its mark on the building and on the people who saw it.

But one show stands above all the others in this theatre's story. In 1975, A Chorus Line opened here. It was a musical about dancers auditioning for a show, people who usually stand at the back, finally given the spotlight. It ran on this stage for about fifteen years, until 1990. For a time, it was the longest-running show in Broadway history. Picture it: one stage, one line of dancers, night after night, for thousands of performances.

The years since then have brought more hits. The musical Crazy for You played here in the 1990s. The long-running revival of Chicago spent several years on this stage. Monty Python's Spamalot made audiences laugh here in the 2000s. Later came Memphis, Matilda, and a revival of Hello, Dolly! with Bette Midler. A stage adaptation of To Kill a Mockingbird also opened here. The building keeps changing its show, but not its role.

In the late 1980s, New York City gave the theatre official landmark status, both for the outside and for the interior. This matters more than it may sound. Many old theatres in this city were torn down when land became valuable. Landmark protection means the facade you see and the room inside cannot simply be destroyed or stripped. The Shubert stays, as a working theatre, and not only as a memory.

Before you go, look once more at the front. Notice the warm brick, the painted panels, and the light of the marquee. Look down the alley at the posters, and at the people passing through. Somewhere inside, a crew may be preparing the stage for tonight. As you walk away along 44th Street, listen again to the city around you. The street sounds the same as when you arrived, but now you know the name above the door, and the brother it remembers.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk through Shubert Alley from 44th to 45th Street.
Check the alley's poster walls to see current shows.
Visit in the morning for a quieter look at the facade.
Wait near the stage door after evening shows for autographs.
Book tickets ahead, as popular shows sell out quickly.
