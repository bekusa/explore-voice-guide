The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the walkway and feel the steel hum under your feet. Every passing truck sends a small tremor through the deck. Below you, the East River moves fast and grey-green, pulled by the tide. The air smells of salt, diesel and wet metal. This is the Roosevelt Island Bridge. It is not the tallest or the oldest crossing in New York, but for the island beside it, it has mattered a great deal.

First, a little geography. Roosevelt Island is a long, thin strip of land in the middle of the East River, between Manhattan and Queens. The river splits around it into two channels. The wider western channel faces the towers of Midtown. The narrower eastern channel, the one under your feet, separates the island from the Queens neighbourhoods of Astoria and Ravenswood. This bridge crosses only that eastern channel. Here is the surprise: it is the only road onto the island. Every car, delivery van, fire engine and moving truck that reaches Roosevelt Island comes this way.

Before this bridge opened in 1955, the island had a stranger way in. A vehicle could drive onto the Queensboro Bridge, which passes high overhead, and stop at its midpoint. There, a large elevator lowered cars and people down to the island below. Trolleys also stopped up on the bridge, and passengers rode the elevator down. It was a slow and awkward arrangement. When this lower bridge from Queens opened, the old elevator lost its purpose. The elevator building was later taken down.

When the bridge opened, the island had a different name. For a long time it was called Blackwell's Island, after a family who farmed it in the colonial era. In the nineteenth century the city used it for institutions that it wanted to keep apart from the rest of town. A penitentiary stood here, along with an asylum, a workhouse and several hospitals. In the 1920s the city renamed it Welfare Island. So at first this crossing was the Welfare Island Bridge.

Now look at the shape of the bridge itself. It is a vertical lift bridge. In the middle is a section of roadway that can rise straight up, like a platform in an elevator shaft. Tall steel towers stand on either side of that section. Inside the towers, heavy counterweights and cables balance the load, so motors can lift the whole span without enormous effort. When the span rises, taller vessels can pass beneath it. Then the span lowers again, and traffic flows across.

This kind of bridge solves a particular problem. A fixed bridge high enough for every ship would need very long ramps on both ends. There is little space for that on a narrow island. A lift bridge can stay low for cars and still open for boats when needed. Today the lift operates far less often than in the bridge's early years, because heavy shipping on this channel has declined. Many residents have never seen it rise at all.

In the 1970s, the island changed character completely. The old institutions had mostly closed, and planners designed a new residential community here. In 1973 the island took the name Roosevelt Island, in honour of President Franklin Delano Roosevelt. The bridge took the new name too. Apartment towers rose along the main street. Later came the aerial tramway to Manhattan, and later still, a subway station. But for drivers, this bridge from Queens remained the only option.

The planners made an interesting choice about cars. They wanted the island to feel calm and walkable. So at the island end of this bridge they built a large parking garage, known as Motorgate. The idea was simple. Residents and visitors could drive across, park near the landing, and continue by foot or by local bus. Look toward the island end, and you will see how the road delivers traffic straight toward that garage. It is a quiet piece of urban design that shapes daily life on the island.

Now turn and face Queens. The shoreline here is industrial and working. Close to the bridge stands the Ravenswood power station, with its tall smokestacks. For decades it has supplied a large share of electricity for New York City. Its stacks are one of the most familiar shapes on this stretch of riverbank. Behind the plant, the streets of Astoria spread out, with low brick buildings, corner shops and the sound of traffic on Vernon Boulevard.

Look north along the river, too. The water here carries a strong tidal current. The East River is not truly a river. It is a tidal strait, a channel of sea water that connects New York Harbor to Long Island Sound. Twice a day the tide pushes the water one way, then pulls it back. From the walkway, you can often see the current churning around the bridge piers and carrying debris quickly downstream.

If you look up and to the south, you will see the Queensboro Bridge crossing high above the island. Its dark steel truss stretches from Manhattan to Queens. Comparing the two bridges tells a story. The older giant overhead was built to carry the city's traffic past the island, almost ignoring it. This humbler bridge was built for the island alone. One bridge passes over the island, and the other connects to it.

The walkway is a good place to slow down. Joggers and cyclists cross here, and so do residents carrying groceries home from Queens. Fishermen sometimes stand along the riverbanks on either side. On a clear evening the light turns the water copper, and the Manhattan skyline glows beyond the island's trees. On a foggy morning the towers of the lift span fade into the grey, and the bridge feels like a quiet harbour structure again.

So take a last moment where you stand. Feel the slight vibration of the deck as another car rolls over the lift span. Listen for the water against the piers, the gulls, and the distant sound of the city. As you walk on, toward Queens or toward the island, remember that this ordinary bridge carries nearly every vehicle that reaches the island. It does its job quietly, every hour of the day.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cross on foot for river and skyline views from the walkway.
Come at sunset for warm light over the East River.
Pair your crossing with a ride on the Roosevelt Island Tram.
Bring a windproof layer; the open channel is often breezy.
Watch for cyclists sharing the walkway in both directions.
