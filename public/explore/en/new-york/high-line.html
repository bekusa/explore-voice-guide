The guide

The story

A ribbon of garden runs high above the streets of the West Side, carried on the steel of an old railway, with wildflowers and grasses growing where freight trains once ran. People stroll between the planting with the city all around and the river beyond. This is the High Line, one of the most admired modern parks in the world.

The structure beneath the garden is a disused elevated freight railway, built in the twentieth century to carry goods above the busy streets and warehouses of the West Side. When the trains stopped, it sat abandoned for years, rusting and overgrown, and was nearly torn down.

Local residents fought to save it, and instead of demolition the old line was reborn as a public park, opening in stages from 2009. The idea of turning derelict infrastructure into a green walkway has since been copied by cities around the world.

The gardens were inspired by the wild plants that had colonised the abandoned tracks, so the planting is loose and naturalistic, full of grasses and perennials that change through the seasons. In places, lengths of the original rail are left among the plants as a memory of what it was.

The park is a long, narrow walk rather than a place to spread out, running for well over a mile above the streets. You move past planted stretches, seating, and viewpoints, with the buildings pressing close on either side and the Hudson River opening out to the west.

Along the way are art installations, clever benches and loungers, and spots where the path widens into small plazas. One section has stepped seating looking down through a window onto the traffic below, turning the ordinary street into a kind of stage.

Because it runs above the streets, the High Line gives unusual views — into windows, down avenues, across rooftops, and out over the river. It threads past old warehouses and bold new buildings, so the walk is also a tour of the changing architecture of the West Side.

The park helped transform the district around it. Galleries, restaurants, and striking new buildings have clustered along its length, and at its northern end it meets a huge modern development of towers and public spaces, a sign of how much the area has changed.

Its fame is also its drawback: the High Line can be very crowded, especially on warm weekends and at sunset, and being narrow, it can feel like a slow-moving stream of people at peak times. Early morning is far calmer and good for the light.

You can join or leave at stairways and lifts along its length, so you need not walk the whole thing. Many people pair it with the food halls, market, and galleries at one end, making an easy half day out of the walk and its surroundings.

A few practical notes. The park is free and open daily, walking only, with no cycling; there is little shade, so bring sun cover in summer, and water. Come early or late to avoid the thickest crowds and the harshest midday sun.

Pause at a railing where the path widens, with the gardens around you, the river ahead, and the traffic flowing far below, and consider that all of this was once an abandoned railway bound for demolition. The High Line is a quiet argument that cities can reuse and rewild their old bones. When you come down to street level, you look at other forgotten structures differently.
