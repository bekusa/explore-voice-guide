The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on one of Chelsea's side streets. You might hear the low rumble of traffic on the avenue, the scrape of a gallery door, a dog's claws clicking on the pavement. The brownstone stoops rise in neat rows. Ginkgo and locust trees throw thin shadows across the brick. Further west there is a different sound, the wind coming off the Hudson River. Chelsea sits between the old city and the water, and you can feel both here.

The name came from London. In the eighteenth century, a retired British army officer named Captain Thomas Clarke bought farmland along the Hudson. He called his estate Chelsea, after the Royal Hospital Chelsea in London, a home for old soldiers. At that time this was open country. There were orchards, meadows, and a house on a rise above the river. Manhattan's crowded streets were still a long carriage ride to the south.

Clarke's grandson changed everything. His name was Clement Clarke Moore, a scholar of Hebrew and a professor. In the early nineteenth century, the city's new street grid came straight toward the family land. Avenues cut through his fields. Moore did not fight the grid for long. Instead, he divided the estate into building lots and sold them with strict rules. Buyers had to build handsome houses. Stables, factories, and other noisy trades were kept off the residential blocks.

Here is the first surprise. Moore is best remembered today for a Christmas poem, not for property. He is the traditional author of "A Visit from Saint Nicholas," the poem that begins "Twas the night before Christmas." That poem shaped how many Americans imagine Santa Claus, with his reindeer and his sleigh. But you should know that the authorship is still debated. Some scholars argue that a different writer, Henry Livingston, wrote it first. The question has never been fully settled.

Moore also gave away a full city block. He donated it to the General Theological Seminary, a school that trains Episcopal priests. You can find it between Ninth and Tenth Avenues, around West 20th Street. The grounds hold a quiet close of brownstone and Gothic buildings, set around lawns and trees. Gothic style means pointed arches and tall windows, as in medieval churches. Even with the city all around, the garden there feels calm and enclosed.

Just across the street, look for the row houses on West 20th Street. Many of the blocks here are built in the Greek Revival style. That style borrowed ideas from ancient Greek temples, so look for simple columns, heavy doorframes, and iron railings with clean lines. Some houses still have small attic windows under the cornice. Walking these blocks, you can see the plan Moore had for this part of the city... orderly, quiet, and made for families.

That quiet did not last everywhere. By the later nineteenth century, the waterfront to the west had filled with piers, warehouses, and freight yards. Ships docked along the Hudson. Rail lines carried goods through the streets. Workers lived in crowded tenements close to the docks. So Chelsea became two places at once. The east side kept its townhouses and churches. The west side became a working district of brick, iron, and smoke.

On West 23rd Street stands the Hotel Chelsea, a tall red-brick building with long iron balconies across its front. It opened in the 1880s and later became a home for writers, musicians, and artists. The poet Dylan Thomas stayed there. Arthur C. Clarke worked on "2001: A Space Odyssey" there. Leonard Cohen, Janis Joplin, Patti Smith, and Robert Mapplethorpe are all part of its story. Look up at those balconies and imagine the conversations that drifted out of them.

Now to the second surprise. Walk over to Ninth Avenue and Fifteenth Street, and you will find Chelsea Market. Today it is a long indoor hall of food stalls and shops. But this brick complex was once a factory for the National Biscuit Company, better known as Nabisco. The Oreo cookie was first produced here in the early twentieth century. Inside, you can still see the old brick walls, pipes, and rough industrial floors.

Above the western streets runs the High Line. It was built in the 1930s as an elevated railway, so freight trains could deliver goods directly into factories and warehouses. After the trains stopped, the tracks sat empty for years, and wild plants grew between the rails. Local residents fought to save it. It opened as a public park in 2009. Today you walk on a path of concrete planks, with grasses and trees planted along the old track bed.

From the High Line, you can look down into the gallery district. In the 1990s, many art dealers moved here from SoHo. They took over old garages and warehouses in the West 20s, between Tenth and Eleventh Avenues. Those big open rooms with high ceilings worked well for large paintings and sculpture. Today you can walk in off the street, often without an appointment. One block can hold several galleries, each with a completely different show.

There are other sides to Chelsea too. It has long been an important neighbourhood for the city's gay community, especially from the later twentieth century onward. You will see that history in the bars, bookshops, and small businesses along Eighth Avenue. Down by the river, Chelsea Piers has turned old shipping piers into sports and recreation spaces. And the Hudson River Park runs along the water, with benches, bike paths, and long views to New Jersey.

So as you walk on, notice how the layers sit side by side. A Greek Revival doorway stands a few blocks from a glass tower. A biscuit factory now sells coffee and tacos. A freight railway is now a garden in the sky. Listen again for that wind off the river, and look for the old brick under the new paint. Chelsea is still changing, one block at a time, right in front of you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the High Line north from Chelsea Market.
Visit galleries on weekdays for quieter, unhurried viewing.
Enter the Seminary close for a calm break from traffic.
Finish at Hudson River Park to watch the sunset.
Wear comfortable shoes for long avenue-to-river walking blocks.
