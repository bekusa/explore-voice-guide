The guide

The story

Listen first. Traffic hums along Riverside Drive, and somewhere below the trees the Hudson River moves slowly toward the harbour. Under your feet the paving is wide and pale, and the air up here often carries a breeze off the water. Ahead of you rises a great block of light grey granite, crowned by a round, columned drum. It looks less like a New York building and more like something carried here from the ancient Mediterranean.

This is the General Grant National Memorial, known to almost everyone simply as Grant's Tomb. It holds the remains of Ulysses S. Grant, the general who led the Union armies to victory in the American Civil War, and who later served two terms as President of the United States. Beside him lies his wife, Julia. It is the largest mausoleum in North America, and it still sits on the high ground of Morningside Heights, looking over the river.

Grant died in 1885, after a long and painful illness from throat cancer. In his final months, close to broke, he raced to finish his memoirs to support his family. He completed them just days before his death. The book became a huge success, and many readers still consider it one of the finest military memoirs ever written in English. So the man inside this tomb was not only a soldier and a president... he was also, at the very end, a writer working against the clock.

Why New York, and not Washington or his home state? The answer is a quiet and human one. Grant wanted Julia to rest beside him. Some of the most obvious national burial grounds of the time would not have allowed that. New York offered this site, and the family accepted. That single wish shapes everything you see here — this is a tomb built for two, not one.

Now for the first surprise. This enormous monument was not paid for by the government. It was funded by public subscription. Around 90,000 people sent in donations, many of them small sums from ordinary working families. At the time, it was one of the largest public fundraising efforts in American history. When you look at all this cut stone, you are looking at the pooled coins of tens of thousands of people who wanted to say goodbye.

The design came from the architect John Hemenway Duncan, who won a competition for the commission. He looked to the ancient world for ideas. The shape echoes the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus — a famous tomb in what is now Turkey, counted among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world. The result is formal and calm. There is no flag-waving drama in the stone. Just weight, symmetry, and a sense of rest.

Lift your eyes above the entrance columns. You will see four short words carved in the granite: Let Us Have Peace. Grant wrote those words in 1868, when he accepted the nomination to run for president. The war was over, but the country was still deeply wounded. Of all the things that could have been carved here — battles, victories, titles — the builders chose a plea for reconciliation.

The tomb was dedicated in 1897, on what would have been Grant's seventy-fifth birthday. Records describe an enormous parade and crowds numbering in the hundreds of thousands, filling the streets and the riverbank. Ships gathered on the Hudson below. For a generation that remembered the war, this was a national event, and for many years afterwards this was one of the most visited places in New York.

Step inside, and the light changes. The interior is cool and hushed, finished in pale marble. In the centre, the floor opens into a circular well. Look down, and you will see two dark red sarcophagi, side by side, one for Ulysses and one for Julia. The idea of viewing the coffins from above was inspired by Napoleon's tomb in Paris, at Les Invalides. You are meant to bow your head simply by looking.

Here is the second surprise, and it answers an old joke. For decades, comedians asked: who is buried in Grant's Tomb? The trick answer is — nobody. Grant and Julia are not buried in the ground at all. They are entombed above ground, in those sarcophagi. Down at the crypt level, small side rooms hold bronze busts of generals who served under Grant, among them William Tecumseh Sherman and Philip Sheridan. They were added in the twentieth century, like an honour guard that never leaves.

The memorial has not always been well cared for. By the late twentieth century, the tomb suffered from graffiti, neglect, and decay. The situation became serious enough that some of Grant's descendants publicly suggested moving his remains elsewhere. That pressure helped bring a major restoration. The monument was cleaned and repaired in time for its centennial in 1997, and today the National Park Service looks after it.

Before you leave, walk around the outside. Along the plaza you will find long, curving benches covered in bright mosaic tiles. They were made in the 1970s as a community art project, with local people helping to build them. They look nothing like the solemn granite above — and that contrast is the point. Close by, a short walk north along the drive, there is a tiny fenced grave known as the Amiable Child Monument. It marks a young boy who died in the late eighteenth century, long before this neighbourhood was a city.

So take one more look. The grey drum against the sky. The four words over the door. The red stone waiting in the quiet below. Behind you, the city carries on — joggers, buses, students from the nearby universities. This tomb was built by thousands of strangers to hold one couple together. As you walk away along the river, you might listen again for the traffic and the wind... and think about what a country chooses to carve over its doorways.
