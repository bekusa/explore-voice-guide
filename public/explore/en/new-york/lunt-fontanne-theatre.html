The guide

The story

Stand on West 46th Street for a moment and listen. Taxi horns, the hiss of a bus braking, footsteps from every direction. Then look up at the pale stone front of the Lunt-Fontanne Theatre. Its calm, classical face sits among glowing signs and digital billboards, like a guest in evening dress at a street party. This is one of the older working playhouses in the Broadway district. For more than a century, crowds have gathered on this pavement before the curtain rises.

The building did not begin with its current name. It opened in 1910 as the Globe Theatre. It was built for Charles Dillingham, a powerful producer of the era who staged lavish musical shows. He hired Carrère and Hastings, a respected architecture firm, to design it. The same firm designed the New York Public Library's main building on Fifth Avenue. So the dignified stone you see here is related, in spirit, to one of the city's best-known civic buildings.

Here is a detail that most visitors never hear. The original Globe had a section of ceiling that could open to the sky. On warm evenings, the panel was drawn back, and audiences could watch a show under fresh air. Before modern air conditioning, a crowded theatre in a New York summer could become very hot. Opening the roof was a clever answer to that problem. Today the ceiling stays closed, but it is worth imagining summer air drifting down over the seats.

In its early decades, the Globe hosted musical comedies and revues, the light, song-filled entertainments that Dillingham loved. Then the economy changed. During the hard years of the Great Depression, many Broadway houses struggled, and some turned to film. The Globe was one of them. For a period in the 1930s and after, it worked as a movie theatre. It was a common fate on these streets, and several neighbouring playhouses followed the same path.

The turning point came in the late 1950s. The building was rebuilt inside and returned to live theatre. It also received a new name. It honoured Alfred Lunt and Lynn Fontanne, a married couple who were among the most admired stage actors of their time. They often performed together. Audiences loved the way they spoke over each other and moved as a pair, almost like one performer with two voices.

The renamed theatre opened in 1958 with the Lunts themselves on stage. They starred in The Visit, a dark play by the Swiss writer Friedrich Dürrenmatt. It was an unusual choice for a celebration. The story is a bitter tale about money, revenge, and a town that sells its conscience. Still, it suited the couple well. It also signalled that the newly named house was meant for serious work, not only for glitter.

Just a year later, the theatre welcomed a very different kind of show. The original Broadway production of The Sound of Music opened here in 1959. The songs written for that stage by Richard Rodgers and Oscar Hammerstein later travelled around the world, especially after the film version. Many people who hum those melodies have no idea that the story first reached Broadway audiences inside these walls.

In later decades, the theatre became a home for big family musicals. Disney's Beauty and the Beast moved here from another Broadway house and played for years. It was followed by Disney's The Little Mermaid. More recently, Tina, the Tina Turner Musical filled this stage with rock and soul. That range tells you something useful. A large Broadway house like this one has to adapt to whatever the city wants to watch.

Now look again at the façade. Notice the symmetry, the tall windows, and the restrained ornament. Carrère and Hastings trained in the Beaux-Arts tradition, a French school of design that valued order, balance, and classical detail. Compared with the flashing screens nearby, this front feels almost quiet. That contrast is part of the pleasure of Times Square. Old stone and new light share the same few blocks.

The building also carries legal protection. In the late 1980s, New York City named many Broadway theatres as official landmarks, and this house was among them. Landmark status means changes to protected features must be reviewed before they happen. For a theatre district where buildings were once torn down without much debate, this was an important shift. It is one reason you can still stand here and see a playhouse from 1910.

If you are attending a show, step inside and pause in the lobby before finding your seat. Broadway theatres pack a great deal into narrow city lots. Hallways can feel tight, and the auditorium opens up suddenly once you pass through the doors. Look at the balcony fronts, the ceiling, and the frame around the stage. The details changed during the 1950s rebuilding, so the room you see is a layered mix of eras.

Outside, the street has its own rhythm. Before an evening performance, the pavement fills with ticket holders, families, and couples checking their phones. Ushers and staff prepare the doors. After the final bow, the crowd pours back out, still talking about the show. These two tides, arriving and leaving, happen almost every day. Locals who work nearby learn to time their walks to avoid them.

It is easy to pass a building like this and see only a marquee and a show title. But this theatre has been an open-air summer playhouse, a cinema, a tribute to two great actors, and a home for some of Broadway's most famous songs. Each chapter changed the building a little. Each one also kept the basic promise of a theatre — people sitting together in the dark, waiting for something to begin.

Before you walk on, take one more look at the stone front. Notice how the evening light, or the glow from the signs, falls across it. Listen to the street again, the horns and the voices. Somewhere behind those walls, a stage crew may be setting up for tonight. As you move toward the next corner, you may find yourself looking more closely at the other theatres on this street, wondering what stories they hold too.
