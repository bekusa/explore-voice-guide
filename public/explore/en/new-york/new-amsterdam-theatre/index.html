The guide

The story

Stand on the south side of West 42nd Street for a moment and let the noise wash over you. Taxi horns, the hiss of bus brakes, a costumed character calling out for photos, the glow of digital billboards even in daylight. Now look for a narrow entrance between the giant signs. It is easy to miss. Behind that modest doorway sits one of the oldest working theatres on Broadway, and one of the most unusual rooms in New York.

This is the New Amsterdam Theatre. It opened in 1903, when Times Square was still finding its identity. The theatre district was moving north up Manhattan, and 42nd Street was becoming the place to be seen. The architects were the firm of Herts and Tallant, specialists in theatre design. They wanted something new. Instead of copying the grand European opera houses, they turned to a fresh style that was sweeping Europe at the time.

That style was Art Nouveau. It is a decorative movement built on flowing curves, plant shapes, and forms taken from nature. Very few buildings in New York used it so fully. Inside the New Amsterdam, almost every surface carries vines, leaves, flowers, and stylised animals. Wood, plaster, and terracotta all twist into organic shapes. For audiences in 1903, walking in must have felt like entering a forest made of craftsmanship rather than trees.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. The narrow façade on 42nd Street is not the front of a large hall. The auditorium actually sits further back on the block. The street entrance is a slim office building with a long passage leading inward. Builders used this trick because land on the busy street was expensive. So the grand room is tucked away, and the entrance works like a corridor that slowly prepares you for the show.

The name that defines this building is Florenz Ziegfeld. He was the showman behind the Ziegfeld Follies, a lavish annual revue full of comedy, music, and elaborate costumes. The Follies moved into the New Amsterdam in 1913 and stayed for well over a decade. The Ziegfeld Girls, dressed in feathers and towering headpieces, became a symbol of glamour across America. Performers like Fanny Brice and Will Rogers appeared on Ziegfeld stages and became household names.

Now for the second surprise. The New Amsterdam once had a theatre on its roof. Above the main auditorium, a smaller rooftop venue hosted late-night entertainment. Ziegfeld used it for his Midnight Frolic, a show that began after the regular Broadway performances ended. Wealthy New Yorkers came up after dinner for music, dancing, and spectacle. Picture it — a second stage high above 42nd Street, lit up long after midnight.

The theatre also has a ghost story, and it is worth hearing as exactly that — a story. Olive Thomas was a Ziegfeld performer who later became a film actress. She died young, in 1920, in Paris. Legend has it that her spirit still walks the New Amsterdam. Some staff over the years have said they sensed her presence backstage. Nobody can prove it, of course. But the tale has become part of the building's personality.

The glamour did not last. Through the Great Depression and the decades that followed, live theatre struggled, and the New Amsterdam became a movie house. Meanwhile, 42nd Street itself changed. By the later twentieth century, the block was known for grindhouse cinemas and adult businesses rather than glittering premieres. The theatre eventually closed in the 1980s. Water damage spread through the building. Plaster fell. Plants grew in places where audiences once sat.

Even in decline, people recognised what was here. New York City designated both the exterior and the interior as official landmarks. That protection matters. It meant the decorative interior could not simply be torn out. Still, a protected building needs someone willing to pay for its rescue. For years, the New Amsterdam sat waiting, a damaged room behind a shuttered entrance, while the city debated the future of Times Square.

That rescue came from The Walt Disney Company. In the 1990s, Disney agreed to restore the theatre as part of a wider plan to revive 42nd Street. Many people point to this deal as a turning point for the whole block. Other companies followed, and the street slowly shifted from neglect toward family entertainment. Restorers worked carefully from surviving fragments and photographs to recreate lost plasterwork and colours.

The restored theatre reopened in 1997. Later that year it became the first home of The Lion King on Broadway, a show that went on to run for decades. The Lion King later moved to another theatre nearby, and the New Amsterdam hosted Mary Poppins. In more recent years it has been the home of Aladdin. So a building made famous by Ziegfeld's feathers now belongs to flying carpets and singing animals — a strange but fitting continuity of spectacle.

If you go inside for a performance, give yourself time before the curtain. Look at the lobby walls and the ceilings, not just your ticket. Notice how the decoration is not random. Leaves curl into frames. Panels tell small stories. The balconies curve gently, and the boxes beside the stage are dressed in carved detail. It is a room designed to be explored with your eyes, almost like a museum that happens to stage a musical every night.

From where you stand now, take one more look at that slim entrance and the busy pavement around it. Thousands of people pass this spot every day, most of them looking up at screens. Very few stop to think that a rooftop cabaret once glowed above them, or that this street nearly lost its oldest stage. As you walk on toward Seventh or Eighth Avenue, listen to the crowd for a moment. Times Square is loud today. A century ago, some of that noise was coming from right here.
