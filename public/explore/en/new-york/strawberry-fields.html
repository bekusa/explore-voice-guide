The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere nearby, someone is probably strumming a guitar, softly, almost under their breath. Feet shuffle on stone. A camera clicks. And at the centre of it all lies a circle of black and white stone, often scattered with fresh petals. You are standing in Strawberry Fields, a quiet corner of Central Park in New York City. It is a garden, a memorial, and for many visitors something close to a pilgrimage, all at once.

This memorial honours John Lennon, the songwriter and former member of the Beatles. Lennon made New York his home in the 1970s. He loved the anonymity the city gave him. Here, he could walk the streets and sit in cafés without the crowds that followed him in Britain. That freedom shaped the final chapter of his life. And it explains why this particular patch of park carries his memory today.

Look up and across the road to the west. That grand, dark building with its steep roofs and ornate gables is the Dakota. It is one of the oldest luxury apartment buildings in Manhattan, built in the late nineteenth century. Lennon lived there with his wife, Yoko Ono, and their son, Sean. On December 8, 1980, Lennon was shot outside the building's entrance. He was forty years old. The news travelled around the world within hours.

In the days after his death, people gathered in the park across from the Dakota. They held candles. They sang his songs. That instinct to come to this spot never really faded. Yoko Ono, who still lived in the Dakota, wanted to give that grief a permanent place. She worked with the city to create a living memorial. She also funded the project herself, including the long-term care of the landscape.

The garden was dedicated on October 9, 1985. That date was chosen with care. It would have been Lennon's forty-fifth birthday. The landscape architect Bruce Kelly shaped the design. He did not build a statue or a monument in the usual sense. Instead, he created a gentle, teardrop-shaped section of park with sloping lawns, winding paths, and trees. The idea was a place to sit and reflect, not just a place to look.

Now, the name. Strawberry Fields comes from one of Lennon's best-known songs, Strawberry Fields Forever, released by the Beatles in 1967. Here is the detail many visitors miss. Strawberry Field was a real place. It was a Salvation Army children's home in Liverpool, close to where Lennon grew up. As a boy, he played in its overgrown garden. So this New York memorial carries the name of a small Liverpool garden from his childhood.

Let your eyes settle on the mosaic at your feet. It is a circle of small stone tiles, set in a radiating pattern of black and white. In the very centre is a single word... Imagine. That is the title of Lennon's most famous solo song, a quiet plea for a world without division. The mosaic was a gift from the city of Naples, in Italy. Its design draws on the patterns of ancient Greek and Roman floor mosaics.

Here is a second surprise. This garden is not only an American project. When it was created, countries around the world were invited to support it as a garden of peace. Many responded. Somewhere nearby you can find a plaque that lists the more than one hundred nations that endorsed the memorial. Some countries also contributed plants and trees. So the greenery around you is, in a small way, international.

You may notice how people behave here. Voices drop. Phones are held up quietly. Strawberry Fields is officially designated as a quiet zone within the park. That means no loud sports, no amplified sound, no rowdy games. It is one of the few places in Manhattan where silence is part of the design. Even on a busy afternoon, the mood stays calm. The city's noise seems to stop at the edge of the trees.

Musicians often gather around the mosaic. They play Beatles songs and Lennon's solo work, sometimes for hours. Visitors join in on the choruses. Others leave flowers, often arranged on the mosaic in patterns. You might see a peace symbol made of rose petals, or candles, or handwritten notes. These tributes change from day to day. No two visits look quite the same, and that is part of what keeps people coming back.

Two dates draw the largest crowds. On October 9, people come to celebrate Lennon's birthday. On December 8, they come to mark the anniversary of his death. On those days, the mosaic can disappear under flowers. Crowds sing together into the evening, and the paths fill with people standing shoulder to shoulder. If you prefer stillness, an early morning on an ordinary weekday is a very different experience.

Take a moment to look at the landscape itself. The ground rises and falls gently. Tall trees shade the paths and benches. In spring and summer the lawns turn deep green, and in autumn the leaves turn gold and copper overhead. Bruce Kelly wanted the garden to feel natural, as if it had always been here. Walk a few steps uphill, away from the mosaic, and you will find quieter benches where you can simply sit.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Notice the soft music, the low voices, the petals on the stone. Then look back toward the Dakota, its dark gables rising above the trees. This is a small place, but it holds a great deal. A song about a Liverpool garden. A gift of stone from Naples. A wish for peace endorsed by nations around the world. As you walk on into the park, you may find yourself humming.

Practical tips

Enter from Central Park West near West 72nd Street.
Come early on a weekday for a quieter visit.
Keep voices low; this is an official quiet zone.
Expect large crowds on October 9 and December 8.
