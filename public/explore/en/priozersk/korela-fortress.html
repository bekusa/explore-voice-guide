The guide

The story

Listen first. Water moves on every side of you here. The Vuoksi River slides past the old earthen walls, dark and quick, and somewhere a gull calls over the reeds. Underfoot there is grass, then packed gravel, then the cool grey stone of a round tower. The air smells of pine and river weed. You are standing on a small island at the edge of the town of Priozersk, in a place people have defended for many hundreds of years.

This is Korela Fortress. The name comes from the Karelians, the Finnic people who lived around these lakes and rivers long before any border was drawn here. Their settlement sat on this island because the island made sense. Water was the road, the wall, and the larder all at once. Anyone who controlled this crossing of the Vuoksi controlled trade between the great lake Ladoga and the forests and lakes to the north and west.

That is the key to everything you see. Korela was never a palace or a cathedral town. It was a gate. Furs, fish, and goods moved along these waters, and armies moved along them too. So the story of this island is a story of rivals. For centuries, the medieval Republic of Novgorod to the east and the Kingdom of Sweden to the west pushed against each other across this region, and Korela sat right on the line between them.

The early fortifications here were made of timber and earth. Historians generally agree that Novgorod built a new wooden fortress on this site in the early fourteenth century, after a period of conflict with Sweden and unrest among the local people. Wood burns, rots, and is rebuilt, so almost nothing of those first walls survives above ground. What remains is the shape. The low ramparts and the outline of the island still follow the logic of those early builders.

Now look around at the walls themselves. Much of what stands today is earth, banked up into broad grassy slopes. These are not decorative hills. Earthworks like these absorbed cannon fire far better than tall stone walls could. As gunpowder changed warfare in Europe, fortresses everywhere became lower and thicker. Here you can feel that change with your own feet as you walk up the gentle slopes and look out over the water.

In the late sixteenth century, the balance tipped. Swedish forces captured the fortress, lost it again by treaty, and then took it once more in the early seventeenth century, during the chaos known in Russia as the Time of Troubles, a period of civil war and foreign invasion. A peace settlement in 1617 left the area in Swedish hands. For roughly a century, this was a Swedish stronghold, and it carried a Swedish name — Kexholm.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. This one small island has carried at least three names. Karelians and Russians called it Korela. The Swedes called it Kexholm. In Finnish, the town and its fortress were known as Käkisalmi. Each name belongs to a different chapter of rule, and each language left its traces on the maps of this region. Today the town is Priozersk, a Russian name that simply means a place beside the lakes.

The Swedish period gave the fortress its most recognisable feature: the massive round stone tower in front of you. Its walls are thick, its windows are few and narrow, and its shape is built to deflect blows rather than meet them flat. Run your hand along the stone if you can. These are rough, irregular blocks of local granite, set with heavy mortar. The builders used what the land gave them, and this land is made of hard northern rock.

In 1710, during the Great Northern War, the armies of Peter the Great besieged the fortress and took it. Peter was building his new capital, Saint Petersburg, on the Neva River to the south, and he wanted to secure the lands around Lake Ladoga. With Russian victory, Kexholm lost much of its value as a frontier post. The border moved west, and the fortress slowly found a new and darker purpose.

It became a prison. In the eighteenth century, after the great peasant uprising led by Yemelyan Pugachev, members of his family were sent here and held for many years. They had committed no crime of their own. They were kept far from the south, where the rebellion had burned, so that no one could rally around them. For this reason, locals often call the round tower the Pugachev Tower. Later, several men involved in the Decembrist revolt of 1825 were also briefly held here.

Think about that for a moment as you look at those narrow windows. A building made to keep enemies out became a building made to keep people in. The same thick walls served both purposes. Inside the tower today there is a museum, and the rooms are small and close. Even on a bright summer day, the stone holds a chill. It is easy to imagine long northern winters passing slowly behind these walls.

The twentieth century brought yet another turn. After the Russian Empire collapsed, this area became part of independent Finland, and the town of Käkisalmi grew around the old fortress. Wars between Finland and the Soviet Union in the 1940s moved the border again, and the town became Soviet. Its Finnish population left. So when you walk the streets nearby, you are walking through a place that changed hands, languages, and populations within a single lifetime.

Before you leave, walk to the edge of the ramparts and face the river. Watch how quickly the water moves past the island. Notice the low wooden bridges, the boats, the line of pines on the far bank. Then turn back to the round tower, grey against the sky. The people who fought for this crossing are long gone, but the river still runs exactly where it did. That is why they came here... and in a way, it is why you came here too.
