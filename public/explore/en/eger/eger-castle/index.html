The guide

The story

Start with the stone under your feet. It is uneven, worn smooth in places, and slightly warm if the sun has been on it. Above you, thick walls of pale brown stone curve along the ridge. Below, the red roofs of Eger spread out toward green hills covered in vineyards. On a clear day you can hear church bells rising from the town, and somewhere a child running along the ramparts. This is a quiet place now. It was not always quiet.

Eger Castle sits on a hill above the town centre in northern Hungary. Its story begins in the Middle Ages, when a cathedral and a bishop's residence stood on this site. In the thirteenth century, after the Mongol invasion devastated Hungary, many towns understood that wooden defences were no longer enough. Stone walls began to rise here. Over the following centuries, the bishops of Eger built and rebuilt, and the hilltop became both a church centre and a fortress.

As you walk through the grounds, look for the low outlines of old foundations in the grass. These traces belong to the medieval cathedral that once stood inside the walls. Today it is mostly gone, but the foundations show how large it was. It is a surprise for many visitors. They come for a military castle, and they find the remains of a great church at its heart. For centuries, prayer and defence shared the same hill.

The building most people notice first is the Gothic Bishop's Palace. Its pointed arches and arcaded walkway are some of the finest late medieval details in the castle. Today the palace holds the István Dobó Castle Museum, named after the man whose courage made this place famous. Inside, you can see weapons, armour, and objects from daily life. They help you picture the people who lived and fought on this ridge.

Now to the story that every Hungarian schoolchild knows. In 1552, a large Ottoman army arrived at Eger. The Ottoman Empire had already taken much of central Hungary, and several other fortresses had fallen that year. Eger stood in the way of a further advance to the north. The commander of the castle was István Dobó, a Hungarian nobleman. Behind these walls he had a small garrison, usually described as fewer than two thousand defenders.

The attacking army was many times larger. The siege lasted for more than a month. The Ottomans used cannon fire, mining under the walls, and repeated assaults. The defenders answered with cannon of their own, with fire, and with stubborn repairs at night. One of the defenders, Gergely Bornemissza, became known for his skill with explosives and improvised weapons. In the end, the Ottoman army withdrew. Eger had held.

The siege also produced some of Hungary's best-loved legends. According to one popular tale, the women of Eger joined the fight. They carried stones and poured hot liquids from the walls onto the attackers. Painters and sculptors returned to this image again and again. Historians debate how much of it happened as told, but the image of the women on the walls is now part of the town's identity.

Here is another legend — and you can taste this one. Locals say the defenders drank strong red wine during the siege. The attackers saw red stains on the defenders' beards and armour, and they believed the Hungarians were drinking the blood of bulls. The story gave its name to Egri Bikavér, which means Bull's Blood of Eger. It is the region's famous red blend. Treat the tale as folklore. The wine, however, is very real, and the vineyards are on the hills you can see from here.

Victory in 1552 did not last forever. Later in the sixteenth century, in 1596, Eger fell to the Ottomans after another siege. The town remained under Ottoman rule for about ninety years, until 1687. You can still see a trace of that period in the town below. Look for the slender minaret rising from the rooftops. It is one of the northernmost surviving minarets from the Ottoman era in Europe.

Before you leave the walls, go below them. Under the castle runs a network of underground passages called casemates. These are protected chambers and tunnels built inside the defences. Soldiers used them to move around safely, to store supplies, and to listen for enemy miners digging beneath the walls. Inside, the air is cool and damp, and the sound of your steps is close and heavy. It is a very different feeling from the bright ramparts above.

There is a second surprise on this hill — a grave. The writer Géza Gárdonyi is buried inside the castle. Around 1900 he published a historical novel about the 1552 siege. In English it is usually called Eclipse of the Crescent Moon. The book turned Dobó, Bornemissza, and the defenders into national heroes for new generations. Few writers rest inside the place they made famous. Gárdonyi does.

After the Ottoman period, the castle lost much of its military importance. Parts of the walls were later demolished, and for a long time the hilltop was half forgotten. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, archaeologists and historians returned. They excavated, restored, and opened the site to visitors. What you see today is a mix of original medieval stone, later repairs, and careful reconstruction. Look closely at the walls, and you can often spot changes in colour and texture where old meets new.

Now find a place on the ramparts and stand still for a moment. Look down at the square below, where a statue of Dobó stands among his defenders. Look out at the minaret, the church towers, and the vineyard hills. As you walk back down into the town, listen for the bells again, and notice the stone under your feet. Defenders once crossed these same paths, waiting for the next attack.
