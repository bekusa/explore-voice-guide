The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and breathe in. Around Dörtyol the air often carries the sweet smell of citrus from the orange groves. Behind you, the Amanus Mountains rise in a dark green wall. In front of you, the flat coastal plain runs down to the quiet water of the Gulf of İskenderun. The ground is soft, farmed, and ordinary. Yet this narrow strip of land between mountain and sea saw one of the most famous battles of the ancient world.

The name you are hearing is Issus. It was an ancient city on this coast, at the inner corner of the gulf. In antiquity, sailors and geographers even named the whole bay after it. The Romans called these waters the Gulf of Issus. Today the name survives mostly in history books. Here on the ground, the city itself has nearly disappeared into the farmland.

Here is the first thing many visitors do not expect. Scholars still debate exactly where the town of Issus stood. Several mounds and ruins along this plain have been suggested. One strong candidate is Kinet Höyük, a large settlement mound close to Dörtyol. A höyük, in Turkish, is a hill built up over centuries by layer upon layer of human settlement. Archaeologists have excavated Kinet Höyük and found occupation stretching back thousands of years.

Why would a town grow here? Look at the landscape again. The mountains press close to the sea, and only a few routes cross them. Anyone travelling between Anatolia to the north and Syria to the south had to pass along this coast. Traders, armies, and pilgrims all funnelled through. A harbour town at this corner could watch the road, trade by sea, and draw water from the mountain streams.

The Greek writer Xenophon passed this way around 400 BCE. He was marching with an army of Greek soldiers hired by a Persian prince. In his account, he described Issus as a large and prosperous city by the sea. It is one of the earliest written mentions of the place. For a moment, it gives us a picture of a busy coastal town, long before its most famous day.

That day came in 333 BCE. Alexander the Great, the young king of Macedon in northern Greece, had crossed into Asia to fight the Persian Empire. The Persian king, Darius the Third, gathered a huge army to stop him. In a surprising twist, the two armies missed each other. Darius came down behind Alexander, and the Macedonians had to turn back and face him on this narrow plain.

The narrowness of the ground mattered enormously. Darius had far more soldiers, but here he could not spread them out. The armies met along a small river, which ancient writers called the Pinarus. Which modern stream that was is, once again, still argued over. Several rivers cross the plain from the mountains to the sea. Whichever it was, the fighting happened somewhere close to where you are standing.

The ancient historians tell the story with drama. Alexander led a charge of cavalry directly toward the Persian king. Darius turned and fled, leaving his chariot and even his royal bow behind. The Persian army broke. In the camp, the Macedonians found the king's mother, his wife, and his daughters. According to the ancient accounts, Alexander treated these royal women with respect, and the story became famous across the Greek world.

Here is the second surprise. You may already know this battle without realising it. A great Roman floor mosaic found at Pompeii shows Alexander charging and Darius turning in his chariot, his face full of fear. It is now in the archaeological museum in Naples. Many scholars believe it shows the Battle of Issus, though some think it could show a later battle. Either way, an image of this kind of moment travelled across the empire.

After the victory, Alexander continued south along the coast. Not far from here, a city was founded in his name, Alexandria near Issus. Its modern descendant is İskenderun, the port city you can reach easily along the gulf. The name İskenderun comes from Iskandar, the form of Alexander's name used across the Middle East. So the memory of that march still sits inside a living city's name.

The plain did not stay quiet. More than five centuries later, near the end of the second century, two Roman generals fought here for control of the empire. Septimius Severus defeated his rival Pescennius Niger in this same region, and historians also call it a Battle of Issus. The geography had not changed. The mountain pass and the narrow coast still forced armies to meet in the same tight space.

Today, the old city does not offer grand columns or a ticket gate. What remains is quieter. You see low mounds rising from the fields, irrigation channels, orchards, and the shape of the land itself. That is the honest truth of this place. Its importance was always the landscape: a gateway between two worlds. Standing here, you can still read that gateway with your own eyes.

Before you go, turn slowly in a full circle. Notice how close the mountains come to the water. Imagine tens of thousands of men squeezed into the space you can see. Then listen to what is around you now... a tractor, birds in the orange trees, perhaps traffic on the coastal road. The same narrow route still carries people north and south. As you walk away, you are following one of the oldest roads on this coast.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in spring when the orange groves around Dörtyol bloom.
Bring water and sun protection for walking open farmland.
Respect private orchards and stay on tracks and field edges.
Combine your visit with a trip to İskenderun's seafront.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, muddy ground after rain.
