The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps against grey stone, and the wind comes straight off Lake Ladoga with nothing to stop it. Under your feet the ground is uneven, packed earth and old masonry worn smooth by centuries of boots. You are standing on a small island at the exact point where the lake pours out and becomes the Neva River. Every boat, every army and every trader moving between the lake and the Baltic had to pass this spot. That one fact explains almost everything about Oreshek Fortress.

The name comes from the Russian word for a nut. Locals say the island got its name because its rounded shape looks like a hazelnut sitting in the current. It is a modest image for a place with such a hard history. Over the centuries this nut became a frontier post, a royal stronghold, a feared prison and, finally, a battlefield. Each of those lives left marks in the stone around you, and you can still read them if you walk slowly.

The story begins in 1323. That year the city-state of Novgorod, then a powerful trading republic in northern Russia, built a wooden fort here. Its founder was Prince Yuri Danilovich, a grandson of the famous medieval prince Alexander Nevsky. In the same year, Novgorod and Sweden signed a peace treaty on this island. It was the first agreed border between the two powers. So before it was ever a battleground, this rock was a place where enemies sat down and drew a line on a map.

The wooden walls did not last. In the fourteenth century Novgorod replaced them with stone. Later, after Moscow absorbed Novgorod, the fortress was rebuilt again on a much larger scale, around the turn of the sixteenth century. The thick walls and heavy towers you see today come mostly from that Moscow rebuilding. Notice how the walls rise almost directly from the water's edge. There was very little beach for an attacker to stand on. The river itself was the first line of defence.

In the early seventeenth century, during a period of chaos and civil war in Russia, Swedish forces took the fortress. They renamed it Nöteborg, which also means nut town, in Swedish. For roughly ninety years this was a Swedish possession, guarding Sweden's hold on the eastern Baltic. Then, in the autumn of 1702, the young Tsar Peter the Great arrived with his army. After a fierce siege and a costly assault, the Swedish garrison surrendered.

Peter understood exactly what he had won. He renamed the fortress Schlüsselburg, a German word meaning key fortress. To him, this island was the key that opened the Neva and the road to the sea. Within a year he founded Saint Petersburg at the river's mouth. Look for the large key fixed above the entrance tower. It refers to that new name, and to Peter's belief that this rock unlocked the future of his empire.

Here is something many visitors miss. Once the border moved west, the fortress lost its military purpose. But the thick walls and the cold water made it ideal for something else... a prison. From the eighteenth century onwards, the Russian state used Schlüsselburg to hold people it considered dangerous. Some were political rivals. Some were members of the royal family itself. Escape across the fast, icy current was nearly impossible, and that was the point.

The most tragic of those prisoners was Ivan the Sixth. He was proclaimed emperor as a baby, then overthrown before he could walk properly. He spent most of his short life in confinement, much of it here, under strict secrecy. In 1764 a young officer tried to free him. The guards had orders to kill the prisoner if anyone attempted a rescue, and they followed those orders. Ivan died inside these walls, still in his early twenties, having barely seen the world beyond them.

In the nineteenth century the fortress became the empire's harshest political prison. Members of the Decembrist uprising were held here, and later the revolutionaries of a group called People's Will. In 1887 Alexander Ulyanov was executed here for his part in a plot to kill the tsar. He was the older brother of Vladimir Lenin. Historians often point to that execution as a moment that hardened the younger brother's views. Inside, you can visit the prison blocks and see how small and silent the cells were.

Now look at the ruins in the centre of the island. The broken walls and shattered brick belong mostly to the twentieth century. During the Second World War, German forces captured the town of Shlisselburg on the southern bank, just across the narrow channel. A small Soviet garrison held this island for roughly five hundred days, under constant shelling. They kept the enemy from closing the river completely during the siege of Leningrad, the wartime name of Saint Petersburg.

When you walk among the remains of the old cathedral, you are standing inside a war memorial. The Soviet defenders are honoured here, and the damage was deliberately left as it is. Look closely at the walls facing the southern shore. You can still see scars and holes left by artillery fire. It is a striking contrast. Medieval stone built to stop arrows and battering rams had to survive modern guns, and much of it did.

As you explore, pay attention to the towers and the way the entrance is arranged. Medieval builders often made gateways bend or turn, so that attackers could not charge straight through. Climb onto the walls where access is open, and turn north. The lake stretches away almost like a sea. Turn south and you see the town, the river mouth and the old canal. From up here the strategic logic of the island becomes obvious, without any explanation at all.

Before you leave, stand for a moment by the water and look back at the walls. Gulls circle overhead, and the river keeps moving past at the same steady pace it always has. Treaty makers, Swedish soldiers, Peter's army, prisoners and wartime defenders all saw this same water. Now it is your turn. As your boat pulls away, watch the fortress shrink into its nut shape again... small, grey, and much tougher than it looks.

Practical tips

Bring a windproof layer even on warm summer days.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground and steep wall stairs.
Allow at least two hours to see prisons and walls.
Climb the walls to compare views of lake and river.
