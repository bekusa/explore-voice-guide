The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the great mound in the heart of Kirkuk and feel the slope rise under your shoes. The earth here is not a natural hill. It is layer upon layer of human life, packed down over thousands of years. Dust drifts across the path. Traffic hums from the city streets below. Somewhere beneath your feet lies Arrapha, one of the oldest cities in this part of Mesopotamia.

Arrapha is the ancient name for the settlement that became Kirkuk. Most scholars place it under the citadel mound, the raised ground that still dominates the old centre of the city. What you see on the surface today is much younger. But the height of the mound itself tells the story. Every metre of extra elevation is a record of houses built, collapsed, levelled and built again. In Mesopotamia, a mound like this is called a tell, which simply means a hill made by people living in one place for a very long time.

To understand Arrapha, it helps to picture the land around it. This is the edge of the Mesopotamian plain, where flat farmland meets the first rising ground toward the Zagros Mountains in the east. A small river runs past the citadel. Trade routes crossed this country, linking the cities of the south with the highlands and the north. A settlement here could watch the roads, collect from the fields, and hold a strong position. That geography explains why people kept returning to this exact spot.

In the second millennium before the common era, Arrapha was the centre of a small kingdom. Its people were largely Hurrians — a people who spoke a language unrelated to the Semitic languages of their neighbours, and who spread across northern Mesopotamia and Syria. For a period, the kingdom of Arrapha sat under the influence of Mitanni, a powerful Hurrian state to the west. It was a local power, not an empire, but it had its own rulers, its own towns and its own record-keepers.

Here is the first surprise. Much of what we know about Arrapha does not come from Arrapha at all. It comes from Nuzi, a provincial town of the kingdom, southwest of modern Kirkuk. In the 1920s and 1930s, archaeologists dug at Nuzi and found thousands of clay tablets. They were written in cuneiform, the wedge-shaped script pressed into wet clay with a reed. These tablets turned a little-known kingdom into one of the best-documented societies of its age.

And what do the tablets contain? Not great battles or royal poetry, but ordinary life. Loans, land sales, adoptions, marriage contracts, lawsuits, lists of livestock and grain. Families arranged inheritance with great care. Some contracts used adoption as a legal tool, so that land could pass to someone outside the family line. Reading them, you meet neighbours arguing over boundaries and fathers planning for their children. It is a very human archive, and it all orbits the capital that lies buried here.

The second chapter of Arrapha belongs to Assyria. In the first millennium before the common era, the city came under Assyrian rule and served as a provincial centre in the empire's eastern lands. Assyria ruled from cities along the Tigris to the west and north, and Arrapha guarded an important approach from the mountains. When Assyria began to fall, this region felt the pressure early. A Babylonian chronicle records that the Medes, a people from the Iranian plateau, came down against Arrapha in 615 BCE, in the years before the great Assyrian capitals fell.

After the empires, the name changed. In later centuries, under Greek-speaking rulers and then in Syriac Christian sources, the city appears with a name built on the word Karkha, meaning a walled town. Many historians connect the modern name Kirkuk to that word. So when you say Kirkuk today, you may be repeating an echo of an old description — simply, the fortified city on the mound. The place kept its role, even as its language and its rulers changed again and again.

Now look at the surface of the citadel. The buildings here belong to the medieval and modern city, not to the Hurrian kingdom. There are old mosques and shrines, including a shrine that local tradition links to the Prophet Daniel and his companions. Tradition, not excavation, makes that connection, and it has drawn pilgrims for generations. The Christian community of Kirkuk also has deep roots in this area. Different faiths have shared this high ground for a very long time.

For centuries, the citadel was a living neighbourhood, crowded with houses, lanes and courtyards. In the later twentieth century, much of that residential fabric was cleared, and many families left. What remains feels quieter, with open spaces where streets once ran. As you walk, notice the old brick, the arches, and the walls that drop toward the lower city. The emptiness is part of the history too. It shows how fragile a place can be, even one that survived for thousands of years.

Here is a final surprise, and it is close by. Not far from the city, at a place called Baba Gurgur, natural gas has burned from the ground for as long as people remember. Locals call these the eternal fires. Some old tales link them to the fiery furnace in the Book of Daniel, though that is legend, not record. In 1927, oil burst from the ground nearby, and the modern oil industry transformed Kirkuk. The ancient city and the modern boom sit on the same restless earth.

It is worth being honest about what you can and cannot see. Arrapha is not an open-air site of excavated palaces. The ancient city is sealed inside the mound, beneath later buildings and centuries of debris. Its story lives in tablets held in museums, in the shape of the land, and in the height of this tell. Your imagination does part of the work here. That is not a weakness. It is what makes standing on a buried capital so unusual.

Before you go, find a spot near the edge and look out over the city. Follow the line of the river. Watch the roads leading east toward the hills. These are the same directions the traders, scribes and soldiers of Arrapha looked toward. As you walk back down the slope, feel the ground under your feet once more. You are stepping across the roof of a city that has been here, in one form or another, for close to four thousand years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the citadel slope slowly to feel the mound's height.
Visit in early morning to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven paths and loose dust.
Dress modestly if you plan to enter shrines or mosques.
Combine your visit with a trip to Baba Gurgur's fires.
