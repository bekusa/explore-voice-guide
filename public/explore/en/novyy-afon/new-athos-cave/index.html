The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the air. It is cool, damp and perfectly still, and it carries a faint mineral smell, like wet stone after rain. Somewhere in the dark, water drips at its own slow pace. Overhead, the rock arches away into shadow, far higher than your eyes expect. You are standing inside a mountain, in one of the largest cave systems in the Caucasus, beneath the town of New Athos on Georgia's Black Sea coast.

The town is known in Georgian as Akhali Atoni, and it lies in Abkhazia, in northwestern Georgia. Above you rises Iverian Mountain. Its name comes from Iveria, an old name for Georgia. On its summit stand the ruins of the Anakopia fortress, and at its foot sits the New Athos Monastery. Most people who climb the mountain have no idea that a vast hollow lies inside it. For centuries, almost nobody did.

This is a karst cave. Karst is a landscape carved by water out of soluble rock, usually limestone. Over a very long time, rainwater picks up a little natural acid from the soil. It seeps into cracks in the stone and slowly dissolves it. Cracks become channels, channels become passages, and passages collapse into enormous halls. Nothing here was dug by human hands, apart from the tunnel you arrived through. Everything else is the patient work of water.

Locals did know about one strange feature on the mountain — a deep, narrow shaft that dropped into darkness. According to local stories, it was bottomless, and people threw stones into it and listened for a landing that never seemed to come. For generations, the hole kept its secret. Then, in 1961, a young man from the area named Givi Smyr decided to find out what was really down there.

He lowered himself into the shaft on a rope. What he found was not a bottomless pit but the edge of an underground world. Speleologists, the scientists who study caves, soon followed with proper equipment. Georgian cave researchers spent years mapping the system. They found one hall after another, each larger than the last. The mountain turned out to be hollow on a scale that surprised even experienced explorers.

The next challenge was practical. How do you bring ordinary visitors into a cave that was first entered by rope down a shaft? The answer was an engineering project. A tunnel was cut into the side of the mountain, and a small electric train was installed to carry people inside. The cave opened to the public in the 1970s. Few caves anywhere in the world have their own underground railway.

That ride is part of the experience. You sit in narrow carriages and roll into the rock, with the daylight shrinking behind you. The walls of the tunnel pass close, rough and grey. Then the train stops, and you step out into the cool. Children often go quiet at this moment. Adults do too. The change from the warm, green coast to this silent underground space happens in just a few minutes.

The cave is divided into a series of halls, and many carry names. Some honour places, such as Abkhazia and Anakopia. One honours the Georgian speleologists who explored it. Walking from hall to hall, you move along paths and stairways, past railings and carefully placed lights. The scale is hard to judge. A person standing on the far side of a hall can look surprisingly small, like a figure in a painting.

Look closely at the stone formations. Stalactites hang from the ceiling, and stalagmites rise from the floor. Both grow from mineral-rich water. Each drop leaves behind a tiny trace of calcite, a pale crystal mineral. Over thousands of years, those traces build into columns, curtains and spikes. Where a stalactite and a stalagmite finally meet, they form a single pillar. Their growth is so slow that a human lifetime barely registers.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Some of the thinnest formations do not point straight down at all. They twist sideways, curl and branch in odd directions. These are called helictites, and scientists still debate exactly why they grow this way. One hall in this cave is named after them. If you see a strand of stone that seems to ignore gravity, you are probably looking at one.

Water is still active here. In parts of the cave there are underground lakes, dark and very clear, with surfaces so still they act like mirrors. Under the lights, the reflections can double the height of a hall. The temperature stays cool all year, whatever the weather outside. On a hot summer day, the cave can feel like a cold room. In winter, it can feel strangely mild by comparison.

It helps to think of the cave as a living system, not a museum. Every drip you hear is adding a little more mineral to some formation somewhere. The paths and lights are a thin human layer over a process that began long before people lived on this coast. That is also why visitors are asked not to touch the formations. Oils from human skin can stop the mineral growth on that spot permanently.

So pause for a moment before you move on. Listen for the drip of water, and notice how sound behaves in a space this large. Look up into the dark above the lights, where the ceiling disappears. When you ride the little train back toward daylight, the warm air and the smell of the sea will meet you at the tunnel mouth. Above you, the mountain will look ordinary again — and now you know what it holds.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a light jacket because the cave stays cool year-round.
Wear shoes with good grip for damp paths and stairs.
Stay with your group since tours follow a fixed route.
Combine the visit with New Athos Monastery nearby.
Climb to Anakopia fortress on the mountain another time.
