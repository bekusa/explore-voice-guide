The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Below you, the Neman river moves wide and slow, and the wind comes up the slope carrying the smell of water and wet grass. Under your feet the ground rises into a steep hill. Its sides fall away sharply toward the river on one side and a narrow ravine on the other. People chose this spot for one simple reason: it is very hard to attack.

This is the Old Castle of Hrodna, also known by its older name, Grodno. The hill sits where a small stream, the Haradnichanka, meets the Neman. Water on two sides and a deep cut on the third made a natural fortress. Long before anyone laid a stone here, people built earth banks and wooden walls along the edge. The city grew around this hill, and the chronicles first name the city in the early twelfth century.

Those first defences were timber, and timber burns. Archaeologists working on the hill found layer after layer of settlement. They found charred wood, everyday objects and the remains of houses packed close together. Each layer marks a phase of building and rebuilding. Fires, raids and repairs stacked the ground higher over the centuries. The hill you are standing on is partly natural, and partly the slow work of many generations of people.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. Excavations uncovered the foundations of a stone church from the twelfth century, often called the Lower Church. Its builders set glazed ceramic tiles into the floor. They also built clay jugs into the walls, with their mouths turned toward the inside of the church. Historians believe these jugs helped the sound of chanting carry through the space. A stone church on a frontier hill at that early date was a sign of real wealth and ambition.

In the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries, Hrodna belonged to the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. That was a large state that once stretched across much of today's Belarus, Lithuania and Ukraine. Its ruler Vytautas, a grand duke remembered as one of its strongest leaders, replaced much of the wooden fortress with stone. Thick walls and towers now followed the edge of the hill. From here, armies gathered and rulers watched the river crossing below.

The next great change came in the sixteenth century. Stefan Batory was King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania at the same time. He made Hrodna one of his favourite residences. He wanted more than a fortress — he wanted a palace. Under his rule the castle was rebuilt in the Renaissance style. That means the European style of the time, which borrowed calm proportions and clean lines from ancient Rome.

And here is the second surprise. Batory did not only live here — he also died here, in 1586. A king of a large European union ended his life in this hilltop palace, far from the capital cities people usually connect with royal history. For a short time, decisions that shaped a large part of Eastern Europe were made inside these walls. The town below grew busy with courtiers, merchants, soldiers and messengers.

Look now toward the way you came in. The castle is reached by a bridge across the ravine. A bridge like this was a weak point and a strong point at once. It was the only easy path in, so defenders could control it closely. As you cross it, notice how the ground drops away on both sides. You can feel how narrow the approach was, and how exposed any attacker would be.

The following centuries were hard on the castle. Wars between neighbouring powers swept through this region again and again. Parts of the walls fell, and buildings were damaged and patched. In the eighteenth century, a new royal residence went up just across the open ground. People call it the New Castle. From then on, the old fortress lost much of its role, and it slowly became a place of memory rather than power.

In modern times the Old Castle became home to a museum of history and archaeology. Many of the objects found in the hill are kept here. In recent years the castle went through a large reconstruction that brought back the shape of Batory's palace. The work drew real debate. Some people welcomed the restored silhouette. Others felt that too much new material now stands where old ruins once stood. As you walk around, you can judge for yourself.

Try to read the walls closely. In some places the stone and brick look rough and uneven, with darker tones and worn edges. In other places the surfaces are clean, sharp and even in colour. The difference is not an accident. It shows where old masonry survives and where new building has filled the gaps. A castle like this is never one single moment in time. It carries many centuries of loss and repair at once.

Now walk to the edge and look out over the river. From this height the Neman bends through the city, and the far bank opens wide. Downriver, on the same side as the castle, stands the old Kalozha Church, one of the oldest buildings in the city. Its walls also date back to the twelfth century. Seeing the two together helps you picture medieval Hrodna: a fortress, a church and a river trade route, all within sight of each other.

Before you leave, stay here a moment longer. Feel the slope under your feet and the wind off the water. Listen to the city behind you — traffic, voices, perhaps a church bell. Nearly every age of this region has passed across this small hill: wooden walls, stone towers, a royal palace, ruins, and now a museum. As you cross the bridge again, look down into the ravine. It still guards the castle, just as it always has.

Practical tips

Visit the museum to see objects found in the hill.
Walk the outer edge for the best views of the Neman.
Pair your visit with the New Castle across the square.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep slopes and uneven paths.
Come in late afternoon for softer light over the river.
