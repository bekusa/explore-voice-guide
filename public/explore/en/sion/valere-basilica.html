The guide

The story

Feel the path change under your feet. The smooth paving of Sion's old town gives way to worn stone and loose grit, and the climb begins to pull at your legs. Above you, the walls of Valère rise straight out of the rock. Somewhere below, a church bell rings across the valley. The air up here is drier, and it carries the scent of warm stone and dry grass. Take a breath. You are walking toward one of the oldest fortified churches in Switzerland.

First, look around you. Sion sits in the Rhône valley, in the canton of Valais, in the south of Switzerland. Two rocky hills rise from the middle of the town like a pair of islands. On one hill stand the ruins of Tourbillon castle. On the other, the hill you are climbing now, stands Valère. For centuries, these two summits held the power of the region. One belonged to the bishop as a stronghold. The other became the home of the church's canons.

Who were the canons? They were a community of clergy attached to the cathedral of Sion. They prayed together, managed land and income, and ran much of the daily business of the church. Here on Valère, they lived, worshipped and kept their treasures. That explains the shape of the place. This is not a quiet village church. It is a church wrapped in walls, with gates, towers and narrow passages. Its builders expected trouble, and they built for it.

The church was built mainly across the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. That long building period shows in the stone itself. The eastern end, around the choir, is Romanesque. Romanesque is the heavy, rounded style of medieval Europe before the Gothic age, with thick walls and round arches. The nave, where the congregation stood, came later and leans toward the Gothic style, with pointed arches and a more vertical feeling. As you walk inside, try to sense the moment when one style hands over to the other.

Now step through the doorway and let your eyes adjust. The light inside is soft and uneven. It falls in pale shafts from small windows and pools on the stone floor. The air is cool, even on a hot summer day. Listen to how your footsteps travel. Sound here moves slowly, bouncing from wall to wall. Many visitors lower their voices without being asked. The building does that to people.

Look up at the tops of the columns near the choir. These are carved capitals, the decorated blocks where a column meets the arch above. Medieval sculptors filled them with figures, animals and plant forms. Some scenes are clear. Others are puzzling, even to experts. For people who could not read, carvings like these were a way to show stories and warnings in stone. Take your time with them. Each one rewards a slow look from different angles.

Here is the detail that surprises most visitors. Turn toward the western end of the church and look high on the wall. That small organ, perched in its painted case, is widely counted among the oldest playable organs in the world. It dates from the late Middle Ages, around the fifteenth century. Think about that for a moment. Its pipes were sounding long before most of the great organs of European cathedrals were even planned. And it still plays.

Look closely at the organ case. It has painted wooden wings, like the doors of a cabinet. When they are open, you see the pipes standing in rows. When they are closed, the painted panels protect the instrument and turn it into a picture. Because this organ is so old and so rare, specialists treat it with great care. In the summer months, organ concerts are often held here, and musicians come from far away for the chance to play it.

The walls themselves carry a second layer of history. Look for patches of colour on the stone. These are traces of medieval wall paintings. Once, much of this interior would have been bright with saints, borders and scenes from scripture. Over the centuries, many paintings faded, were covered or were lost. What remains are fragments, some strong and some barely visible. Try to imagine the whole space in colour. It changes how the church feels.

In the choir you will also find carved wooden stalls, the seats where the canons sat for their daily prayers. They come from a later period than the stone around them, and the woodwork is rich with figures and ornament. Run your eyes along the rows. The wood has darkened with age and with the touch of many hands. Picture the canons here before dawn, chanting in a cold church, with candles as their only light.

When you step back outside, notice the buildings around the church. The fortified complex also includes the old living quarters of the canons. Today, part of this space is home to the Valère Museum, which belongs to the history museum of Valais. It holds objects that tell the long story of the region, from early times to the modern age. If you have time, it adds depth to what you have seen in the church. It may need a separate ticket.

Now find a spot along the walls and look out. Below you, the rooftops of Sion spread toward the river. Across the gap, the broken walls of Tourbillon stand on their own hill. Beyond them, vineyards climb the slopes of the valley in long terraces. The Valais is one of the great wine regions of Switzerland, and on a sunny day you can see why. The mountains frame everything. On clear days, snow often shines on the higher peaks.

Pause here for a moment. For many centuries, people climbed this same path for prayer, for safety, or for business with the canons. Pilgrims, merchants, soldiers and children all walked these stones. The view they saw was different in its details, but the shape was the same. The two hills. The river. The mountains closing the valley on both sides. You are standing in a place that has watched the valley for a very long time.

As you start back down, slow your steps. Notice the wind moving across the hilltop, and the sound of the town rising toward you again. Look back once at the church walls, pale against the sky. Somewhere inside, behind those thick stones, the old organ is waiting in its painted case for the next concert. The path will take you back to the streets of Sion. But keep a little of this quiet with you as you go.

Practical tips

Check the summer schedule for organ concerts.
Allow extra time for the Valère Museum next door.
Visit Tourbillon hill too for the reverse view.
