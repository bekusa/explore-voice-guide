The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Traffic hums along Battonnstraße, trams rattle somewhere nearby, and office windows reflect a grey Frankfurt sky. Below your feet, under concrete and glass, lie the stone foundations of a street that was once one of the most crowded places in Europe. For more than three centuries, the entire Jewish community of Frankfurt lived along one narrow, curving lane. This was the Judengasse, the Jews' Lane, and its story is still here, just beneath the surface.

The lane began with an order. In 1462, the city council forced Frankfurt's Jews to leave their homes near the cathedral. The reason given was simple: Christian clergy did not want Jewish neighbours so close to the church. The community was moved to a strip of land just outside the old city wall, on the eastern edge of town. It was meant to be a separation. It became a world of its own.

Picture the shape of it. The Judengasse was a single long street, gently curved, following the line of the old wall. Gates stood at its ends. At night, on Sundays, and on Christian holidays, those gates were locked from the outside. Residents could not simply walk out when they wished. Their movement through the rest of the city was limited by strict rules, and for long periods they had to wear marks on their clothing that identified them as Jews.

The space never grew, but the people did. The city would not give the community more land, so families built upward and inward. Houses were divided, then divided again. Rooms were added at the back, and storeys were stacked on top. By the eighteenth century, around three thousand people lived on this one lane, in fewer than two hundred houses. Light struggled to reach the ground. Historians often describe it as one of the most densely populated places in Europe at that time.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The houses in the Judengasse had no numbers. Instead, each house had a name and often a painted sign. There was the House of the Golden Swan, the House of the Red Shield, the House of the Green Shield, and many more. Families were often known by the house they lived in. Over generations, some of these house names became family surnames... and one of them would become famous across the whole world.

That name is Rothschild. It comes from a house called zum roten Schild, the Red Shield, where the family's ancestors once lived. Mayer Amschel Rothschild was born in the Judengasse in 1744. He started as a dealer in coins and antiques, working from this cramped street. From here, he built a banking business, and he sent his sons to London, Paris, Vienna, Naples and beyond. The financial dynasty that shaped nineteenth-century Europe began in a lane where the gates were locked every Sunday.

Life inside was never quiet or safe. In 1614, a guild uprising led by a man named Vincenz Fettmilch turned violent. A mob stormed the Judengasse, plundered the houses, and drove the Jewish residents out of the city. Two years later, under the protection of the emperor, the community returned. Fettmilch was executed. For generations afterwards, Frankfurt's Jews marked the date of their return with a yearly celebration, a small festival of survival.

Fire was another danger. With wooden houses packed so tightly, a single spark could destroy everything. In 1711, a great fire swept through the lane and burned almost all of it. The community rebuilt, on the same small plot, because there was nowhere else to go. Then, in 1796, French troops bombarded Frankfurt, and the northern part of the street burned again. Each time, people returned and built once more.

The walls came down slowly. In the early nineteenth century, under new rulers influenced by the French Revolution, the duty to live in the Judengasse was lifted. Full legal equality took decades longer. Wealthier families moved to other parts of the city. Later in that century, most of the old houses were demolished as unhealthy and run down. The street was renamed Börnestraße, after Ludwig Börne, a writer and critic who was born here.

Then came the darkest chapter. A large synagogue stood nearby at Börneplatz. During the November pogrom of 1938, it was set on fire and destroyed. In the years that followed, the Jews of Frankfurt were stripped of their rights, deported, and murdered in the Holocaust. After the war, the area was rebuilt with roads and modern buildings. For a long time, little above ground showed that a Jewish world had ever stood here.

The past came back in 1987. Workers digging foundations for a new municipal building uncovered the remains of houses and ritual baths from the old lane. The discovery started a passionate public debate. Many citizens argued the ruins should be protected, not built over. In the end, a compromise was reached. Part of the remains were kept inside the new building, and they became the core of the Museum Judengasse, today part of the Jewish Museum Frankfurt.

Inside, you can walk among the foundations of several houses. You will see low stone walls, cellar spaces, and the remains of two mikvehs. A mikveh is a ritual bath, used for spiritual purification in Jewish tradition. These are not reconstructions. They are the real stones that once held up the crowded homes of the lane. Look closely, and you can follow the lines of tiny rooms where whole families once lived, worked and prayed.

Next to the museum lies the Old Jewish Cemetery, one of the oldest in Germany. Its weathered gravestones lean in all directions under the trees. Along its outer wall runs a memorial. Thousands of small metal blocks carry the names of Frankfurt Jews murdered in the Holocaust. Visitors often place a small stone on top of a block. In Jewish custom, a stone on a grave shows that someone came, and someone remembered.

As you leave, look again at the ordinary street around you. The curve of the road, the busy crossing, the office windows. It is easy to pass through without a thought. But you now know what lies below. A lane of named houses, locked gates, fires and rebuilding, and a community that endured for centuries. Walk slowly for a few steps... and let the city feel a little deeper than before.
