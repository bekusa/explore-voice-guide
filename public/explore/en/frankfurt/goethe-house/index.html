The guide

The story

Listen for the creak first. Old oak floorboards shift under every step here. They make a dry, patient sound, like the house is keeping count of its visitors. Light comes in through small-paned windows and falls across polished wood and painted walls. Outside, Frankfurt's glass towers rise only a few streets away. Inside, you are standing in an eighteenth-century family home, and the air feels quieter, closer and more domestic.

This is the Goethe House, on the street called Großer Hirschgraben. On 28 August 1749, Johann Wolfgang von Goethe was born here. He became the most celebrated writer in the German language, the author of Faust and The Sorrows of Young Werther. But before all that, this was simply where a clever, restless boy grew up. He had a strict father, a lively mother and a younger sister he adored.

Start with the shape of the building. From the street it looks like one tall, orderly townhouse. In fact, it began as two older half-timbered houses standing side by side. In the mid-1750s, Goethe's father, Johann Caspar Goethe, had them rebuilt and joined into a single home. He wanted a house that showed his status as a wealthy, educated citizen. The result has a grand staircase, generous rooms and a clear sense of order on every floor.

Johann Caspar was a lawyer with a title but no real public office. So he turned his energy inward — into his collections, his library and his children's education. He taught the young Goethe himself and hired tutors for languages, drawing and music. As you climb from floor to floor, you can feel the ambition of that household. Every room was meant to be useful, cultured and improving.

On the ground floor, look for the kitchen. It is easy to rush past, but this is where the house worked. Copper pots, heavy hearth fittings and a water pump show daily life before running water. Servants cooked and cleaned here, and the warmth of the stove would have risen through the house. It is a useful reminder. Behind every polished salon upstairs stood a lot of hard labour downstairs.

Now for the first surprise. The house you are walking through is largely a reconstruction. In March 1944, Allied bombing raids destroyed much of Frankfurt's old town, and this building was reduced to ruins. However, many of the furnishings had been moved to safety before the raids. After the war, there was a long debate about whether to rebuild at all. In the end, the house was carefully reconstructed and reopened around 1951.

So the walls around you are postwar, but much of what fills the rooms is genuine. Many paintings, pieces of furniture and objects survived because they had been packed away in time. That gives the house an unusual double character. It is a careful copy of a lost building, filled with real things from the Goethe family's world. Look closely at the wear on chair legs and table edges. That kind of age is hard to fake.

The first upper floor holds the more formal rooms. Here the family received guests, made music and showed off their taste. One room is known as the Peking Room, decorated in the European fashion for Chinese-style design. In the eighteenth century, wealthy households loved this exotic look. It said that the owners were modern, worldly and well connected — even if few of them had ever travelled far.

On the staircase, watch for a large old astronomical clock. Clocks like this showed more than the hour. They could track the phases of the moon and other movements of the sky. For a household that valued learning so highly, it fits perfectly. Imagine the boy Goethe passing it every day on the stairs. Time, the heavens and knowledge were all part of the furniture.

The second surprise is small and charming. Look for the puppet theatre. According to Goethe's own memories, his grandmother gave the children a puppet theatre one Christmas. He became fascinated by it. He staged plays, built scenes and gave voices to the little figures. Many readers see this toy as the first spark of his lifelong love of theatre. Years later, the old puppet play of Doctor Faustus would grow into his greatest work.

Higher up is the room most visitors come to find — the poet's room, where the young Goethe wrote. In the early 1770s, back in Frankfurt after his studies, he produced some of his first famous works here. The Sorrows of Young Werther appeared in 1774. It was a sensation across Europe, the story of a young man destroyed by love. Early drafts of Faust also began in these years. Stand by the desk and picture a young man in his twenties writing fast, late and with great feeling.

Then, in 1775, Goethe left for Weimar, a small court town in central Germany. He stayed there for most of his long life. His mother remained in Frankfurt, and she became a well-known figure in her own right. Visitors and friends loved her warmth and humour. Through her letters, the house stayed connected to her famous son for many years.

Since the nineteenth century, the house has been looked after by the Freies Deutsches Hochstift, a cultural foundation devoted to Goethe and German literature. Next door, a modern museum explores German Romanticism — the movement of writers, painters and thinkers who put emotion, nature and imagination at the centre of art. If you have time, the two buildings work well together. One shows where Goethe started, and the other shows the wider world of ideas around him.

Before you leave, pause on one of the upper landings. Listen again to the floorboards and the soft voices of other visitors. Look out of a window at the modern city and then back at the painted walls. A child once ran up and down these stairs, played with puppets and filled notebooks with stories. As you walk out onto the Großer Hirschgraben, notice how ordinary the street feels. Great writers often begin in ordinary places.
