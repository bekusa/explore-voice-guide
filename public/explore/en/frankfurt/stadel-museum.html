The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, the River Main moves slowly past the embankment. Cyclists hum along the riverside path, and somewhere a tram bell rings on the far bank. In front of you rises a long, pale stone façade with tall windows and a calm, symmetrical face. This is the Städel, one of Germany's oldest museum foundations. It holds paintings that span roughly seven hundred years of European art.

The museum did not begin with a king or a government. It began with one man and his will. Johann Friedrich Städel was a Frankfurt banker and merchant who collected paintings, drawings, and prints throughout his life. When he died in the early nineteenth century, he left his collection and his fortune to found an art institute for the citizens of his city. The idea was simple and quite radical for its time. Art would belong to the public, not to a palace.

That civic spirit still shapes the place. Frankfurt was a free city of merchants, not the seat of a royal court. So the collection grew through private gifts, donations, and the energy of local supporters. Even today, the museum depends heavily on private patrons and friends' associations. When you walk through these galleries, you are walking through two centuries of Frankfurt citizens deciding that great art mattered to them.

The building in front of you dates from the late nineteenth century, when the collection moved to this spot on the south bank of the river. This stretch of water is now known as the Museumsufer, the Museum Embankment. Along this one riverside road, more than a dozen museums sit almost side by side. The Städel was here long before the others, and it remains the anchor of the whole row.

Inside, the collection is arranged in three great chapters. The Old Masters fill the upper floor, with works from the fourteenth to the eighteenth century. Modern art, from the nineteenth century into the early twentieth, occupies the middle level. And contemporary art lives below ground, in a space many visitors do not expect at all. Most people start at the top and work their way down — a journey through time in a single afternoon.

Upstairs, look for a small, glowing panel by the Flemish painter Jan van Eyck. It is known as the Lucca Madonna. Van Eyck worked in the fifteenth century and helped develop the rich, layered technique of oil painting. Lean in close. The fabrics, the light on metal, and the tiny details of the room are painted with astonishing precision. It is easy to walk past because it is small. Do not.

Nearby, you will find a quiet masterpiece by Johannes Vermeer, the Dutch painter of the seventeenth century. It is called The Geographer. A scholar pauses at his table, dividers in hand, while soft daylight falls through a window. Vermeer painted only a small number of works in his lifetime, so seeing one here is a real privilege. Notice how the light, not the man, seems to be the true subject of the scene.

The Städel also holds a very different side of Rembrandt. His painting The Blinding of Samson is large, violent, and theatrical. It shows the Biblical hero at the moment he is overpowered. Many visitors know Rembrandt for gentle portraits and warm shadows. This canvas is a shock. Stand back and watch how he uses darkness and a single burst of light to pull your eye straight into the struggle.

Now for a painting every German schoolchild knows. Tischbein's portrait of the writer Goethe shows him reclining in the Italian countryside, wrapped in a wide cloak and a broad hat. Goethe was born here in Frankfurt, and his family home still stands in the city centre. Here is a small surprise. Look closely at his feet. Many viewers claim that he appears to have two left feet. Decide for yourself.

The museum's story also has a painful chapter. In 1937, the Nazi regime removed hundreds of works from German museums, branding modern art as degenerate. The Städel lost many important paintings, among them Van Gogh's Portrait of Doctor Gachet. That canvas was later sold abroad and, decades afterwards, became one of the most expensive paintings ever auctioned. In the years since the war, the museum has worked hard to rebuild its modern collection, especially German Expressionism and the painter Max Beckmann, who lived and taught in Frankfurt.

Finally, there is the underground world. In 2012, the museum opened a large extension beneath its own garden. From outside, you only see a gently curving lawn dotted with round skylights. Below, those skylights become a ceiling of circular openings that pour daylight into the galleries of contemporary art. It is one of the quiet surprises of Frankfurt. A whole museum wing hides under the grass, and most passers-by never notice it.

The Städel is not only a museum. Johann Friedrich Städel's foundation also gave rise to an art school, the Städelschule. It is still one of Germany's respected academies for fine art. So the legacy works in two directions. Here, the past is preserved on the walls, while young artists nearby are making what may one day hang in these rooms. That link between old and new runs through everything here.

Before you move on, turn back toward the river. Look across the water at the Frankfurt skyline — glass towers of banks and offices rising above the old town. Then look again at this patient stone building. A banker's fortune made both views possible in different ways. As you walk along the embankment, notice how the city keeps its money on one bank of the Main, and its memory on the other.

Practical tips

Start with the Old Masters upstairs and work your way down.
Allow at least half a day for all three collection levels.
Walk the Museumsufer path afterwards for skyline views across the Main.
Check ahead whether special exhibitions need a separate timed reservation.
Use the museum café as a break between collection floors.
