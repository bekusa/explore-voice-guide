The guide

The story

Stand at the railing and listen. Far below, too far to see clearly, the Gunnison River is moving. On a quiet morning the sound climbs up the cliffs as a low, steady rush, like wind in a distant forest. The air at the rim smells of sagebrush and dry juniper. Then you lean forward a little, look down, and the ground seems to fall away into shadow.

This is the Black Canyon of the Gunnison, in western Colorado, a short drive from the town of Montrose. The name is not poetry. It describes what you see. The canyon is so deep and so narrow that some parts of the floor get only a short spell of direct sunlight each day. The walls are dark grey rock, and they stay in shade for much of the day. Even at noon, the bottom can look like evening.

Other canyons in the American West are wider, and a few are deeper. What makes this one unusual is the combination of depth and narrowness. In places the rim on one side is only a few hundred metres from the rim on the other. Yet the drop to the river is far greater than that gap. Your eyes are not used to this. The canyon looks less like a valley and more like a crack split through the earth.

The rock itself is very old. Geologists describe most of it as Precambrian gneiss and schist, which are hard metamorphic rocks. Metamorphic rock started out as other stone and was changed by enormous heat and pressure deep underground. Much of what you see formed close to two billion years ago. That makes these walls far older than the dinosaurs, older than almost any life on land.

So how did a river cut through stone this hard? The answer is patience, and a little bit of luck. The Gunnison first flowed across softer rock that once lay on top. It settled into its course. Then, as the region slowly rose, the river was trapped in its own channel. It had no choice but to keep cutting straight down into the hard rock beneath. Geologists estimate this took around two million years.

The river does its work with force. Through the park, the Gunnison drops steeply, far more steeply than most large rivers in North America. Fast water carries sand, gravel, and boulders, and these act like a grinding tool. Floods in past centuries would have been especially powerful. Today, a series of dams upstream controls much of the flow, so the river you hear is calmer than the one that carved these walls.

If you visit the South Rim, find the overlook for the Painted Wall. It is the tallest cliff in Colorado, rising roughly 2,250 feet, close to 700 metres, from the river. Look at its face and you will see pale, pinkish stripes running across the dark rock in crooked lines. These are called pegmatite dikes. Long ago, hot liquid rock forced its way into cracks and then cooled into these light bands of crystal.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Those stripes are not layers stacked like a cake. They cut across the cliff at angles, branching and crossing each other. Each one is a frozen record of molten rock pushing through older stone. Once you notice them, the wall stops looking like a single surface. It starts to look like a map of ancient pressure and heat.

For most of human history, people lived around this canyon rather than inside it. The Ute people knew this high country well, and the land around the rims carries their long presence. When surveyors came through in the nineteenth century, including an expedition led by Captain John Gunnison in 1853, they found the gorge almost impossible to cross. The river and the canyon later took his name.

The canyon's most famous early story is about water. Farmers in the Uncompahgre Valley, near Montrose, needed it badly. The Gunnison River had plenty, but it was locked deep inside the canyon. In 1901, Abraham Lincoln Fellows and William Torrence travelled through part of the gorge, partly on a rubber mattress, to study the route. Their journey helped make the case for a tunnel.

That tunnel was finished in 1909. The Gunnison Tunnel carries river water nearly six miles through solid rock to the valley on the other side. It turned dry land into farmland, and it still delivers water today. When you drive through Montrose and see green fields, part of that green comes from this canyon. It is a quiet surprise — a wild place that also feeds a whole valley.

Protection came in stages. The canyon became a national monument in 1933 and a national park in 1999. Today the South Rim road links a string of overlooks, each with its own view. The North Rim is quieter and more remote. Pay attention to the birds, too. Swifts dart along the cliffs, and peregrine falcons are known to nest in the canyon walls, diving at remarkable speed.

The park keeps its character after dark. It has been named an International Dark Sky Park, which means the night sky here is protected from light pollution. On a clear night, the Milky Way can stretch across the gap between the rims. The canyon becomes a black space beneath a sky full of stars. The rock you are looking at now will still be here, almost unchanged, long after tonight.

Before you walk on, look down one more time. Find the thin line of the river, if you can see it at all. Notice how the shadow moves along the opposite wall as the sun shifts. Listen again for that low rush of water. It has been cutting this canyon for longer than our species has existed... and it is still working, one grain of sand at a time.

Look closer

What to look for

Pale pink pegmatite stripes crossing the Painted Wall.
The thin silver line of the river far below.
Shadows sliding along the opposite canyon wall.
Swifts darting close to the vertical cliff faces.
Twisted juniper trees clinging to the rim rock.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Painted Wall overlook on the South Rim.
Bring layers because rim weather changes quickly.
Stay after dark for clear, unpolluted stargazing.
Keep children close at unfenced cliff edges.
Allow extra time for the quieter North Rim.
