The guide

The story

Listen first. Traffic hums along the wide avenues of Makhachkala, and somewhere close the call to prayer rises above it all. Then look up. A broad grey dome sits over a ring of smaller domes, and slender minarets rise like sharpened pencils against the sky. The air off the Caspian Sea carries a faint salt edge. You are standing in front of the Grand Mosque of Makhachkala, also called the Central Juma Mosque, the main Friday mosque of Dagestan.

If the silhouette looks familiar, there is a good reason. The mosque was designed with the Sultan Ahmed Mosque in Istanbul in mind, the building most travellers know as the Blue Mosque. That is an Ottoman design, the style of the Turkish empire that once ruled from Istanbul. It has cascading domes, a central prayer hall, and needle-thin minarets with small balconies. Here, beside the Caspian, that Istanbul profile appears far from the Bosphorus, the strait that runs through Istanbul.

The building is young by the standards of the region. It went up in the 1990s, in the years after the Soviet Union ended. For most of the twentieth century, religious life in Dagestan was tightly restricted. Many mosques closed, and others became warehouses or simply fell apart. When those restrictions lifted, communities across the republic began to rebuild. This mosque became one of the clearest public signs of that return. Turkish partners supported its construction, which helps explain its Ottoman look.

Here is something many visitors miss. The mosque you see today is larger than the original. In the 2000s it was significantly expanded, because the first building could not hold the Friday crowds. Even after the expansion, worshippers on busy Fridays often fill the courtyard and the surrounding pavements. They lay prayer mats wherever they find space. The mosque is often described as one of the largest in Russia, and on a Friday at midday you can feel why.

To understand why this building matters, it helps to know a little about Dagestan itself. The name means something close to land of mountains. It is one of the most diverse places in the Caucasus, home to dozens of ethnic groups and many languages. Avars, Dargins, Kumyks, Lezgins, Laks and others all live here. For many of them, Islam is a shared thread. A mosque like this one brings people together who might not share a mother tongue.

Islam has deep roots in this corner of the world. It reached the southern city of Derbent, on the Caspian coast, very early, within the first centuries of the faith. The old Juma Mosque in Derbent is often named among the oldest mosques in Russia. So although the building in front of you is modern, the tradition it serves is more than a thousand years old in Dagestan. The new walls carry a very old story.

Now take a closer look at the exterior. The surfaces are pale and clean, and the main dome sits on a stepped base of half-domes and smaller cupolas. Each layer leads your eye upward, toward the crescent at the top. The minarets have narrow balconies near the top. In the past, a muezzin, the person who calls the faithful to prayer, would climb up and call from there. Today loudspeakers usually carry the voice across the city.

If you are able to go inside, the change is immediate. The noise of the street drops away. Soft carpet runs underfoot, and the space opens upward into the dome. Large chandeliers hang low over the prayer hall, so their light falls close to the people praying below. Look for the mihrab, a decorated niche in the wall. It shows the direction of Mecca, toward which every worshipper faces. Near it stands the minbar, a raised pulpit with steps.

Pay attention to the people around you, because they are the real life of this place. You may see elderly men in sheepskin hats talking quietly by the entrance. Young men may rush in from work with their sleeves rolled up for washing. Children may follow their fathers, trying to copy each movement of the prayer. Before praying, worshippers wash their hands, face and feet at the ablution area. The sound of running water is part of the rhythm here.

A few words on visiting with respect. This is a working mosque first and a sight second. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. Women are generally expected to cover their hair, so carry a scarf. Shoes come off before you step onto the carpet. Try to avoid the five daily prayer times, and above all Friday midday prayer, when the hall belongs to the worshippers. Ask before you photograph anyone, and keep your voice low.

Step back out and look at where the mosque stands. It sits in the heart of Makhachkala, a city that has grown quickly and loudly over the past decades. Apartment blocks from the Soviet era stand beside newer towers and small shops. Minibuses stop and start along the roads. Against all of this, the domes look calm and deliberate. That contrast between the city's rush and the mosque's stillness is part of what makes standing here memorable.

The Caspian Sea is not far away. It is the largest inland body of water on Earth, though people often call it a sea. Its shore shapes the climate and the mood of the city. On a clear evening, the low sun turns the pale walls of the mosque warm and golden. On a grey day, the domes almost merge with the sky. The same building can feel very different from hour to hour.

So before you leave, pause for a moment in the courtyard or on the pavement outside. Notice the domes stepping up toward the crescent. Notice the shoes lined up at the door, and the quiet voices of the people heading home. Then listen once more for the call to prayer, rising above the traffic, as you walk back into the city.
