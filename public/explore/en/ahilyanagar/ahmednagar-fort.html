The guide

The story

Listen first. Beyond the gate, the traffic of Ahilyanagar fades into a softer sound. Wind moves across dry grass, a bird calls from the old walls, and now and then a vehicle hums somewhere inside the military grounds. Under your feet the ground is hard and dusty. Ahead of you, dark stone walls curve away in a long, patient arc. This is Ahmednagar Fort. For more than five centuries it has been a seat of sultans, a prize for empires, and, in the end, a prison for some of the men who shaped modern India.

Start with the name. Ahmednagar means, roughly, the city of Ahmad. The man behind that name was Ahmad Nizam Shah, who founded a new kingdom on the Deccan plateau in the late fifteenth century. The Deccan is the high, dry tableland that fills much of southern and central India. In those years it was divided between rival sultanates, each one formed from the pieces of an older empire. Ahmad chose this site for his capital, and the city and its fort grew together from that choice.

The dynasty he founded is known as the Nizam Shahi. For about a century, its rulers held power from this place. They built palaces, gardens, mosques, and water systems across the region. The first fort here was reportedly made of earth. Later rulers rebuilt it in stone, and that stone fort is the one you see today. As you look at the walls, notice their weight and their thickness. These are walls built by people who expected a siege.

That expectation was correct. The fort's shape helps explain how it was meant to work. It is roughly circular, ringed by a series of round bastions that push outward from the wall. A bastion is a projecting tower that lets defenders fire along the face of the wall, not only straight ahead. Around the walls runs a wide moat. Together, these features made any direct assault slow, exposed, and very costly for an attacking army.

Now for the story that most people in this region know by heart. In the 1590s, the armies of the Mughal Empire marched south to take Ahmednagar. The Mughals ruled much of northern India from cities like Delhi and Agra, and they wanted the Deccan. The kingdom here was weak and divided. Into that gap stepped a remarkable woman — Chand Bibi, a royal widow who took charge of the defence.

In 1596, Chand Bibi held these walls against the Mughal siege. Accounts describe her directing the defenders herself and organising repairs where the attackers had breached the wall. Some details of her story have grown in the retelling, and popular tales show her in armour at the gap with a sword in her hand. What is clear is that her defence forced the Mughals to negotiate. For a short time, Ahmednagar survived.

The ending is a sad one. Within a few years, the court was split by quarrels again. Chand Bibi died in 1599 amid that internal conflict, and the fort fell to the Mughals in 1600. The Nizam Shahi state continued for some decades from other bases, but it never fully recovered. As you walk along the walls, it is worth remembering that the greatest danger to this fort came from inside as well as outside.

After the Mughals, the fort passed through many hands. Over the following centuries, the Maratha powers of western India gained control of it. The Marathas were a confederacy of Hindu rulers and generals who challenged the Mughals and, later, the British. Then, in 1803, a British force under Arthur Wellesley captured the fort. Wellesley would later become the Duke of Wellington, the general who defeated Napoleon at Waterloo. Few visitors expect to find his name in this part of Maharashtra.

Under British rule, the fort became a military base and, at times, a place of detention. That leads to the chapter that surprises many visitors most. In 1942, after the Quit India movement began, the British arrested the leaders of the Indian National Congress. Quit India was a mass campaign that demanded an immediate end to British rule. A group of its most senior leaders was brought here, far from the crowds of Bombay.

Among them was Jawaharlal Nehru, who would become India's first prime minister. During his imprisonment here, he wrote much of The Discovery of India, his long reflection on the country's history and culture. Other prisoners included Maulana Abul Kalam Azad and Vallabhbhai Patel. Azad also wrote here, composing a collection of letters that became a well-known work of Urdu prose. So this fort, built for war, also became a place where ideas for a new nation were written down.

Today, the fort sits inside an area managed by the Indian Army, and access can depend on military rules. Parts of the interior connected to the prisoner period have been kept as a memorial. If you are allowed inside, look for the rooms linked to the Congress leaders. They are plain and small. That plainness is part of their power. Big ideas were written in small spaces like these, with little more than a table, a chair, and time.

As you move around, look at the details that tell the fort's age. You may see stone in different colours and finishes, a sign of repairs made over many periods. The bastions carry the marks of weather and time. The moat, which once held water to stop attackers, changes with the season. After the monsoon rains it can look green and full. In the dry months it shows its depth and shape much more clearly.

The surrounding city is worth a thought too. For centuries it was called Ahmednagar, after its founder. In 2024, the name was officially changed to Ahilyanagar, in honour of Ahilyabai Holkar, an eighteenth-century Maratha queen known for her rule and her building of temples and public works. So the fort now carries one name, and the city around it carries another. Both names belong to the long story of this region.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look along the curve of the wall. Sultans looked out from here. Mughal soldiers looked in. A queen organised a defence, and a future prime minister wrote about a country that did not yet exist. Now it is quiet, with wind in the grass and dust on the path. As you walk back toward the city, listen to the noise of modern Ahilyanagar returning. It is the newest layer of a very old place.

Before you go

Practical tips

Carry photo ID because the fort lies in military grounds.
Ask guards before photographing any military buildings or personnel.
Visit in early morning for softer light and cooler air.
Bring water and a hat for exposed walks along walls.
Allow time to explore the city's other Nizam Shahi monuments.
