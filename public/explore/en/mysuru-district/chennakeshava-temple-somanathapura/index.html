The guide

The story

Take off your shoes at the gate and feel the stone underfoot. In the morning it is cool and smooth, polished by centuries of bare feet. Then the heat builds, and the courtyard begins to glow. Ahead of you, on a raised platform, sits a temple that looks almost carved from a single dark block. It is not large. But step closer, and every surface fills with figures, animals, flowers and gods, cut so finely that the stone looks soft.

This is the Chennakeshava Temple, in the quiet village of Somanathapura. The name Chennakeshava means the handsome Keshava, a form of the Hindu god Vishnu. The village sits in farm country, a drive of under an hour from Mysuru, near the Kaveri river. There are no grand avenues here. There are coconut palms, paddy fields and a few tea stalls. That stillness is part of the experience. You meet one of the finest works of medieval Indian craft with almost nothing around it.

The temple belongs to the Hoysala dynasty. The Hoysalas ruled much of what is now the state of Karnataka between roughly the eleventh and fourteenth centuries. They were great patrons of temple building. Their style is easy to recognise once you know it — star-shaped plans, low and wide towers, and walls covered from base to cornice in carving. Historians now speak of a distinct Hoysala school of architecture, and this temple is often called one of its most complete examples.

An inscription records that the temple was consecrated in 1258. It was not built by a king. It was commissioned by a general named Somanatha, who served the Hoysala ruler Narasimha the Third. The general founded the settlement itself, and the village still carries his name. Look for the inscribed stone slab near the entrance, standing under its own small pavilion. It is the reason we know this temple's date with such confidence. Few monuments of the period are documented so clearly.

Now look at the shape of the building. It is not a simple rectangle. The temple has three shrines, joined to a single central hall. Architects call this a trikuta plan, which simply means three peaks. Each shrine has its own tower above it. From the corners of the courtyard you can see all three towers at once. Their outlines are not smooth. They step in and out, again and again, so the shadows break into many sharp vertical lines.

That stepping comes from the star-shaped platform beneath your feet. The whole temple stands on a raised terrace called a jagati, which follows the zigzag outline of the walls. Walk slowly around it. The platform was designed for this. Worshippers once circled the shrine clockwise, and the path gave them a close view of the carvings at eye level. Every turn of a corner shows the sculptures in a new angle of light.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. The material is not granite. It is a soft, greenish-grey stone, usually described as soapstone or chloritic schist. When it is freshly quarried, this stone can be cut with fine tools almost like wood. Over time, exposed to air, it hardens. That is how Hoysala carvers achieved such depth and delicacy. Some of the jewellery on the figures has tiny pierced openings, and the beads look as if they could move.

Look now at the bottom of the outer wall. The carving runs in long horizontal bands, like stacked ribbons. The lowest band shows a parade of elephants. They suggest strength, the animals that carry the temple. Above them run horsemen, then rows of scrolling foliage, then more creatures and decorative motifs. Higher still are narrative panels. They tell stories from the great Hindu epics, the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, and from the Bhagavata Purana, a sacred text about the life of Krishna.

Take a moment with one band of elephants and count a few of them. They are not all the same. Some lift their trunks, some turn their heads, some carry riders. The carvers worked across hundreds of figures, yet many small differences remain. Above them, the larger sculptures show gods and goddesses with tall crowns and heavy ornaments. Vishnu appears in many forms. Here is the second surprise: a number of these panels carry the signatures of the artists who made them, cut into the stone below their work.

One name you may hear from local guides is Mallitamma, a sculptor whose signature appears on many figures here. It is unusual in medieval Indian art to know a carver by name. At Somanathapura, the craftsmen claimed their work in writing. Standing here, you are not only looking at a dynasty's piety. You are looking at the pride of individual people, who wanted to be remembered for what their hands did.

Now step inside the central hall. After the bright courtyard, your eyes need a moment. The pillars are thick and rounded, with rings stacked one above another. Many people believe they were turned on a lathe, and their polished, banded surfaces do look machine-made. Then look up. The ceiling is divided into panels, and each one holds a deep carved design, often a lotus opening downward. No two ceiling panels are quite alike. A small torch or phone light helps you see their depth.

The shrines at the back hold images of Vishnu. The central figure of Keshava, which gave the temple its name, is no longer here. Today the side shrines hold forms of Vishnu known as Janardhana and Venugopala, the second showing Krishna playing the flute. The temple is now protected as a monument by the Archaeological Survey of India. In 2023 it became part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing, together with the better-known Hoysala temples at Belur and Halebidu.

Before you leave, walk the inner edge of the courtyard. A covered cloister runs around it, lined with many small cells. Some have lost their sculptures, and their emptiness gives the place a calm, almost hollow sound. Sit on the steps for a while. Listen to the birds in the trees outside the wall, and watch the light move across the platform. The stone changes from grey to green to warm brown as the sun shifts. As you walk back to the gate, turn once more. The three towers stand quietly together, exactly where the general Somanatha placed them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early morning before the courtyard stone gets hot.
Wear socks you can keep on after removing shoes.
Walk the platform clockwise to follow the carving sequence.
Bring a small torch to see the ceiling panels.
Combine the visit with a day trip from Mysuru.
