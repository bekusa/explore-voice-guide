The guide

The story

Listen first. Gulls call over the estuary, and the water of the River Conwy slides beneath you, pulled by the tide. Under your feet the deck feels solid, yet slightly alive, as if it knows it is hanging in the air. Ahead, grey stone towers rise with battlements, like a gate into a medieval town. Behind them stands the real thing: Conwy Castle, darker and older, watching over the bridge. You are standing on one of the early suspension bridges of Britain.

This is the Conwy Suspension Bridge, a short drive or a pleasant ride west from Colwyn Bay. It was designed by Thomas Telford, a Scottish engineer of extraordinary range. He built canals, harbours, churches and roads. By the time he came to this estuary, he was among the most respected engineers in Britain. He completed this crossing in 1826. That is the same year his larger bridge over the Menai Strait opened, further along this coast.

Why build a bridge here at all? Before it existed, travellers crossed the river by ferry. The estuary is wide, and the tide moves fast. Crossings could be slow, and in bad weather they could be dangerous. Early in the nineteenth century, the road along this coast mattered more and more. It carried mail and passengers towards Holyhead, and from there across the sea to Ireland. A fixed crossing would make that journey safer and more reliable.

Telford chose a suspension design. In simple terms, the road deck does not rest on arches. Instead, it hangs from great chains of wrought iron. These chains pass over the towers at each end and are anchored firmly into the rock and masonry. Look up at the chains now. They are made of long flat iron links, joined together like a giant bracelet. Vertical rods drop from them and hold up the deck below.

Now look at the towers themselves. Here is something many visitors miss. Telford did not simply build plain stone supports. He gave them turrets and battlements, so that they would match the castle beside them. The bridge was designed to sit politely next to a medieval fortress. Even in the 1820s, engineers thought about how new structures would look in an old landscape. This was a modern machine dressed in a costume of the Middle Ages.

The castle deserves a moment of its own. It was built in the late thirteenth century for King Edward the First of England, during his conquest of Wales. Its walls and towers still dominate the rock above the river. Notice how the eastern tower of the bridge connects almost directly to the castle rock. From certain angles, the bridge seems to grow out of the fortress. Five centuries of building history meet in a few steps.

At the town end of the bridge, find the small toll house. For many years, anyone who wanted to cross paid a toll here. A keeper lived in this modest building, close to the traffic and the tide. Today the National Trust looks after the bridge, and the toll house has been furnished to show daily life in an earlier era. It is a reminder that this structure was not only engineering. It was also somebody's home and workplace.

Here is a second surprise. For well over a century, this narrow deck carried all the road traffic across the river. Horses and carriages came first. Later came motor cars, lorries and buses, all squeezing through the stone gateways. Telford never imagined engines on his bridge. Eventually, the traffic became too heavy. In 1958 a new road bridge opened right beside it, and this old crossing was saved for people on foot.

Turn and look upriver, just beside you. That long, box-shaped structure is the railway bridge, designed by Robert Stephenson in the late 1840s. Trains run inside a great iron tube, which was a bold new idea at the time. Stephenson also gave his bridge castle-style towers, so it too would fit beside the fortress. So within a few metres you can see two famous engineers, two different technologies, and one shared respect for the castle.

Take a moment at the middle of the deck. This is a good place to feel the scale of the estuary. When the tide is high, water fills the channel and boats float near the quay. When the tide is low, mudflats and sandbanks appear, and wading birds search for food. The light changes quickly here. Clouds roll in from the sea, then break, and the stone towers turn from grey to warm gold.

Think about the people who crossed here in the early years. Mail coaches would have rattled through the towers on their way west. Travellers who once feared the ferry could now cross in minutes. For the town of Conwy, the bridge brought a steady stream of visitors and trade. It also changed the view forever. Paintings and early photographs often show the castle and the bridge together, as if they had always belonged side by side.

When you walk back towards the town, look at the stonework closely. The blocks are cut and laid with care, and the surfaces show long exposure to wind, salt and rain. Patches of lichen grow on the shaded sides. Inside the town, the medieval walls still circle the old streets. From the quay, you can look back and see the whole composition at once: the river, the castle, the two bridges, and the hills beyond.

So, before you leave, pause once more on the deck. Feel the gentle firmness under your feet, the iron above you, the stone at each end. Listen to the gulls, the water, the footsteps of other walkers. This bridge was built to move people quickly. Today it asks you to slow down instead. As you step off, glance back at the towers... and notice how quietly they stand beside the castle they were made to match.

Practical tips

Walk across slowly, then view it again from the quay.
Visit at high tide for water filling the estuary.
Combine your visit with Conwy Castle next door.
Bring a windproof layer for gusts off the estuary.
Step inside the toll house when it is open.
