The guide

The story

Put your hand on the wall beside you. The stone is cool, rough, and darker than you might expect. Grey and brown, with flecks that catch the light. Above you, the wind comes in off the estuary and moves around the towers. You may hear gulls, and the low rumble of a train crossing the river. This is Conwy Castle, a short journey west of Colwyn Bay. For more than seven centuries, it has stood on this rock at the mouth of the River Conwy.

The castle was built for King Edward the First of England in the 1280s. The work moved fast. Most of the fortress you see went up in about four to five years. That speed is the first surprise. Thousands of labourers, masons, carpenters, and diggers were brought in from across England. They quarried stone, carried it, cut it, and raised it into these walls. It was one of the largest building projects in medieval Britain.

Why here, and why so fast? Edward had just conquered the native Welsh princes of Gwynedd, the mountain kingdom of north-west Wales. He wanted to hold that land for good. So he built a chain of great castles across the north. Conwy was one of the most important links. It sat on the river, so ships could bring in supplies by sea. A castle that could be fed by water was hard to starve out.

The man behind the design was Master James of Saint George. He was a master builder from Savoy, a region in the western Alps. He had worked on castles for the counts of Savoy before Edward brought him to Wales. Look at how the castle follows the shape of the rock. It is long and narrow, because the rock itself is long and narrow. Master James did not fight the ground. He used it.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Before the castle, this was a holy place. A Cistercian abbey stood here, called Aberconwy. Cistercians were a strict order of monks who valued simple living and hard work. Llywelyn the Great, one of the most powerful Welsh princes, was buried in that abbey. Edward moved the monks several miles up the valley and built his fortress over their ground. For the Welsh, that choice carried a clear message about who now ruled.

Now look at the towers. There are eight of them, round and heavy, set along the outer walls. Round towers had a practical purpose. They had no sharp corners for attackers to break with picks or rams. They also gave defenders a wider field to shoot from. At each end of the castle, there is a smaller walled entrance yard called a barbican. These forced any attacker through extra gates before reaching the main walls.

Inside, the castle is split into two parts. The outer ward held the great hall, the kitchens, and the garrison. The inner ward, further along, was the private space for the king and queen. The royal rooms here are among the best surviving medieval royal apartments in Wales. Try to imagine painted walls, fires, wooden floors, and cloth hangings. Today the rooms are open to the sky, but their shape is still easy to read.

And here is the second surprise. These walls were probably not always grey. Experts believe the castle was once coated in limewash, a white mix of lime and water. Look closely at sheltered corners and you may spot faint pale traces. Picture the whole fortress gleaming white above the river. To anyone sailing into the estuary, it would have looked bright, new, and impossible to ignore.

The castle was soon tested. In the winter of 1294 and 1295, a Welsh uprising broke out. Edward himself was trapped at Conwy. Floods on the river cut him off from his army, and supplies ran low. Accounts tell of the king sharing his last wine with his men. Relief ships finally arrived, and the castle held. Its design had done exactly what it was meant to do.

Later centuries brought more drama. In 1399, King Richard the Second came here during his final crisis, shortly before he lost his throne. Then in 1401, Welsh rebels seized the castle in a daring move on Good Friday, while much of the garrison was at church. In the English Civil War of the seventeenth century, the castle was held for the king until it fell to Parliament. Afterwards, parts of it were deliberately damaged so it could not be used again.

Walk to the walls facing the river and look out. You will see three bridges side by side. One is a graceful suspension bridge designed by Thomas Telford in the 1820s. Its towers were styled to match the castle. Beside it runs Robert Stephenson's railway bridge from the 1840s, a long iron tube. The modern road bridge sits nearby. Few places show so many ages of engineering in one view.

From the tower tops, you can also follow the town walls. They curve away from the castle and wrap around the whole old town of Conwy. They run for well over a kilometre, with more than twenty towers along the way. Castle and walls were built as one plan. Together they formed a fortified English town. They are now part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, shared with other castles Edward built in north-west Wales.

Before you leave, look down at the town below. The streets still follow much of their medieval layout. Down on the quay, near the water, you can find a tiny red house often called the smallest house in Great Britain. Fishing boats rest in the harbour. On a clear day, the hills of Snowdonia rise behind the estuary, green and grey under moving cloud.

Take one more moment here. Feel the wind again, and listen. The gulls, the water, the distant train. These stones were raised to control a land and its people. Today they hold visitors, families, and quiet walkers instead. As you head back down the narrow stairs, run your hand along the wall once more. Hundreds of hands have worn it smooth, one visit at a time.
