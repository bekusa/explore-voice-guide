The guide

The story

Listen first. Gulls call overhead, rigging taps against masts, and the tide pulls at the stones of the quay. The air smells of salt, seaweed, and sometimes fresh fish. Then you see it: a tiny house painted bright red, squeezed into a terrace of older buildings. It looks almost like a stage prop, or a doll's house someone forgot to bring inside. This is the Smallest House in Great Britain, and it is real, lived-in history.

A quick note on where you are standing. Although many visitors look for this house in Colwyn Bay, it actually stands on the quayside of Conwy, a walled market town a short drive west along the North Wales coast. Conwy sits at the mouth of the river of the same name, where the water meets the sea. So if you have made the trip from Colwyn Bay, you are in exactly the right place. The quay in front of you is the old working heart of the town.

Now look at the size of the front. The house is a little under two metres wide and just over three metres tall. That is roughly the height of a single room in an ordinary home. If you stretch out your arms, you can almost span the whole frontage. The red paint makes it stand out against the grey stone around it. The house is often called Quay House, a plain name for a building that is anything but ordinary.

The house is generally dated to the sixteenth century. At that time, Conwy was a busy trading port, and space inside the town was valuable. Builders filled every gap they could find. This house was squeezed into a narrow space between other buildings, using walls that were already there. In a sense, it is a house made from the spaces between other houses. That simple idea explains why it is so small.

Inside, there are only two tiny rooms, one above the other. The ground floor holds a small living space with a fireplace and a narrow bench. A steep ladder climbs to the upper room, which held a bed and very little else. There was no space for a kitchen or a bathroom as we know them. Daily life here meant cooking by the fire, sleeping close under the roof, and doing almost everything else outdoors on the quay.

Here is the first surprise. The last person to live here was a fisherman named Robert Jones. According to the story often told about him, he was a very tall man — around six foot three, or about 1.9 metres. Picture him in that upper room. He probably could not stand up straight anywhere in the house. People say he had to stoop to move around. Somehow, he made this tiny space his home.

Robert Jones lived here until around 1900. At that time, the local council decided that the house was unfit for people to live in. This was a period when authorities across Britain were inspecting crowded and unhealthy housing. Many small, damp dwellings were cleared. This one was emptied of residents, but it was not demolished. That decision is the reason you can still stand in front of it today.

The second surprise is about ownership. The house is still owned by descendants of Robert Jones. It has stayed in the family for well over a century. Over time, the family has opened it to visitors, and people now pay a small fee to step inside and look around. On many days, a guide in traditional Welsh dress stands at the door. She usually wears a tall black hat, a shawl, and a long skirt, the costume that became a symbol of Wales in the nineteenth century.

The house became famous after it was recognised by the Guinness Book of Records as the smallest house in Great Britain. That title brought visitors from far beyond North Wales. Today, a sign on the front proudly states its claim. It is worth remembering that size records are often debated, and other places have made similar claims. But this little red house is the one most people think of when they hear the phrase.

Now turn around and look along the quay. Behind and around you rise the town walls of Conwy. They were built in the late thirteenth century for King Edward the First of England, after his conquest of this part of Wales. The walls run almost all the way around the old town, with many towers along the way. Conwy Castle stands at one end, its dark stone towers above the river.

The quay itself has always been a working place. Boats still tie up here, and Conwy has a long tradition of gathering mussels from the estuary. You may see fishing gear, ropes, and crates along the edge of the water. On a calm day, small boats rock gently on the tide. On a windy day, the water turns grey and choppy, and you understand why a fisherman wanted his home so close to his boat.

Take a moment to look at the details. The tiny front door sits beside an equally small window. The roof slopes steeply, and the walls of the neighbouring buildings press in on both sides. If you look up, you will see how the house fits under the town wall behind it. Compare it with the houses next door, which suddenly seem enormous by contrast.

Before you walk on, stand still for a moment. Listen again to the gulls and the slap of water against the quay wall. Imagine Robert Jones stepping out of that small red door in the early morning, ducking his head, and walking down to his boat. The tide comes in and goes out just as it did then. As you leave, glance back once more. From a few steps away, the little red house almost disappears among the stones — and that is part of its charm.
