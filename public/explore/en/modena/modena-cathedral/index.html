The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Bicycle tyres hum across the paving of Piazza Grande. Somewhere a café cup meets a saucer. In front of you rises a wall of pale, warm stone, soft gold in some places and grey in others, with marks where the weather has worn the edges. This is Modena Cathedral, and its stones have stood in this exact spot for more than nine centuries.

The cathedral is dedicated to Saint Geminianus, a fourth-century bishop and the patron saint of this city. Locals call him San Geminiano. His remains lie in the crypt below the main altar. In the late eleventh century, the people of Modena decided their saint deserved a far greater home. Work on the new building began in 1099. The building you see now is the result of that decision.

Here is the first surprise. We know the name of the architect, which is unusual for a church of this age. He was called Lanfranco, and a contemporary account of the founding describes him as a master builder of rare skill. That account, known as the Relatio, tells how the townspeople, the clergy, and the bishop all worked together. For a medieval building, that kind of record is uncommon.

The style is Romanesque. That is the building language of Europe before the Gothic age — thick walls, rounded arches, and a sense of weight and calm. Look along the façade and you will see rows of small arches, like a stone gallery, running across the upper level. The proportions feel steady and grounded. Nothing here tries to pull your eye upward too quickly. The building simply stands, broad and confident, facing the street.

Now move closer to the main front. Across the façade run long horizontal panels of carved stone. These are the work of a sculptor named Wiligelmo, who worked alongside Lanfranco. The panels tell the story of Genesis, read from left to right. You can follow the creation of Adam, the making of Eve, the temptation, the expulsion from paradise, Cain and Abel, and finally Noah and the ark. The figures are stocky, serious, and full of life.

Look for a carved inscription near these reliefs. It praises Wiligelmo by name and celebrates the quality of his sculpture. In an age when most craftsmen stayed anonymous, this was a remarkable thing. A sculptor's name was set into the building itself, for every visitor to read. Many scholars see these panels as a turning point for sculpture in Italy, after centuries when large stone figures were rare.

And here is something many visitors miss. Look carefully at the stones in the walls. Some of them are older than the cathedral. Modena stands on the site of a Roman town called Mutina, and medieval builders reused Roman stone they found in the ground. Here and there you may notice a fragment with different carving or lettering. The city of the Romans and the city of the Middle Ages share the same wall.

Walk now along the side of the building, toward the north and the street. Here you will find the Porta della Pescheria, often called the Fishmarket Door. Look up at the curved band of stone above the doorway. It shows knights on horseback attacking a castle. The names carved beside them include Artus, which is King Arthur. This is one of the earliest known images of the Arthurian legends anywhere in Italy, carved long before many famous versions of those stories were written down.

Return to the square side of the cathedral, facing Piazza Grande. In the thirteenth century, a group of builders known as the Campionese masters arrived here. They came from Campione, a village near the lakes of northern Italy. They added the large rose window on the main front and the grand south doorway called the Porta Regia, the Royal Door. Their work blends with Lanfranco's design, so the building feels unified even though many hands shaped it.

This wall also served the market that once filled the square. According to the city's long tradition, official measures were set into the stone here, so traders could check the size of bricks, tiles, and cloth. Imagine merchants arguing over a sale, then walking to the cathedral wall to settle the matter. Faith and daily trade stood side by side. Nearby in the square, look for a large stone block, once used for public announcements.

Now step inside. The light drops, and the air cools. The interior is built mostly of warm red brick, with stone columns and arches. Ahead of you, a raised platform stretches across the church, separating the nave from the choir. It is supported by columns resting on carved lions. Along its front, the Campionese masters carved scenes of Christ's final days. Below it, steps lead down into the crypt, a forest of slender columns, where the saint rests.

Back outside, look up at the bell tower beside the cathedral. Locals call it the Ghirlandina, perhaps after the decorative railings, like garlands, around its top. It grew in stages over many generations, and you may notice it leans slightly. Local tradition links the tower to a wooden bucket captured from rival Bologna in the fourteenth century — a story that inspired a famous comic poem. In 1997, the cathedral, the tower, and Piazza Grande together became a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Before you walk on, turn back and take in the whole scene once more. The Genesis panels, the lions at the doors, the leaning tower, the café tables at its feet. People still cross this square every day on their way to work or school, often without looking up. As you leave, run your eyes along the stone one last time, and notice how the afternoon light changes its colour from grey to gold.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the north side to see the Fishmarket Door carvings.
Come in late afternoon for warm light on the façade.
Read the Genesis panels from left to right.
Go down into the crypt to see the saint's tomb.
Bring binoculars or zoom lens for high relief details.
