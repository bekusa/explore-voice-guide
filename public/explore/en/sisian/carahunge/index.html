The guide

The story

Listen first. On this high plateau above Sisian, the wind rarely stops. It moves through the dry grass, presses against your jacket, and whistles faintly as it passes the stones around you. Underfoot, the ground is rough and uneven, scattered with small rocks and tufts of hardy plants. In front of you stand the stones. There are hundreds of them, dark and weathered, some upright, some leaning, some lying in the grass. This is Carahunge, and nobody can fully tell you what it was.

The site has two names, and each one tells a different story. The older name, Zorats Karer, is usually translated as the stones of the army or the warriors' stones. Locals say the stones look like soldiers standing in ranks, frozen on the hillside. The name Carahunge is more recent. It comes from Armenian words for stone and for sound, and it is often explained as speaking stones. Both names are in use today, and both stay with the place.

Look closely at the stones themselves. They are basalt, a hard volcanic rock that is common in this part of southern Armenia. Many stand taller than a person. Their surfaces are rough and pitted, and most are covered in patches of lichen... pale green, grey, and in places a warm orange. These lichens grow very slowly. Each patch is a sign of a very long time without disturbance. Run your eyes along the edges, and you will see how wind and frost have rounded them.

Now comes the first surprise. Walk along the lines, and you will notice that many stones have a round hole cut through them near the top. The holes are smooth and deliberate — someone made them with real effort, using tools far simpler than ours. Not every stone has one. But dozens do, and they are the reason this site became famous. Why would people bore a clean tunnel through solid basalt? That single question has shaped every debate about Carahunge.

One answer came from an Armenian scientist named Paris Herouni. He was a radiophysicist, known for his work on radio telescopes. In the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, he and his team studied the holes and their directions. Herouni argued that some holes pointed toward the rising and setting sun at important times of year, and toward certain stars. He concluded that Carahunge was an ancient observatory. He also proposed that it was many thousands of years old.

Herouni's ideas travelled far. Newspapers and travel writers began to call this place the Armenian Stonehenge, after the famous stone circle in England. The comparison is easy to understand. Both are rings and lines of standing stones in open country, and both have been linked to the sky. But it is important to say this clearly: many archaeologists do not accept the observatory theory, and they strongly question the very early dates. The debate is still open today.

So what do archaeologists see here? Excavations have found burial structures among the stones. At the centre of the site there is a large ring of stones, and inside it are the remains of a burial mound with a stone chamber. Other graves, made from stone slabs, lie across the area. Many researchers connect the site to the Bronze Age and the Iron Age, roughly the second and early first millennium before Christ. In this view, Carahunge was first of all a place for the dead.

Some scholars go further. They suggest that some of the standing stones were once part of walls, perhaps around a settlement or a burial complex. Others think the holes had practical uses, such as tying or moving the heavy stones. Here is the honest answer: the evidence supports more than one story. Burial ground, ritual place, a site that watched the sky... it may have been several of these things over many centuries. That uncertainty is part of what you are standing in.

Take a moment to find the central ring. From there, the lines of stones run outward across the plateau, like arms reaching into the grass. Walk slowly along one of them. Notice how the spacing changes, how some stones lean toward each other, and how some have fallen flat. Stand behind a stone with a hole and look through it, keeping your eye close. The hole frames a small circle of sky or hills. This is the view that started all the theories.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a quiet one. The holes are not all at the same height or angle. Some tilt upward, some run almost level, and some are worn or partly broken. This variety is exactly why the debate continues. A supporter of the observatory theory sees careful alignments. A sceptic sees ordinary variation in hand-made work. When you look through two or three holes yourself, you join a conversation that is still not finished.

Now lift your eyes beyond the stones. The plateau opens in every direction, with rolling hills and, on clear days, distant mountains on the horizon. In late spring and early summer, the grass fills with wildflowers. In autumn, it turns gold and brown. The light here changes quickly as clouds pass, and the stones change with it, from almost black to soft grey. Early morning and late afternoon bring long shadows that make the lines of stones easier to read.

The town of Sisian below is worth your time after the visit. This whole region, called Syunik, is full of old stone. In and around the town you can see carved stone gravestones in the shape of rams, a tradition found across southern Armenia. You will also find old churches built from the same dark volcanic rock you see here. Seen together, they show how people in this land have shaped basalt for thousands of years, for the living and for the dead.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment among the stones. Feel the wind again, and notice how it sounds as it moves past the nearest stone. Look at the lichen on its surface, and at the small hole near its top. Whatever this place was — observatory, cemetery, sacred ground — people carried these stones here, raised them, and returned to them. As you walk back across the grass, glance over your shoulder once. The stones will still be standing in their lines, as they have for a very long time.

Look closer

What to look for

Smooth round holes cut cleanly through the upper stones.
Orange and pale green lichen patches on the basalt.
The central stone ring around a ruined burial chamber.
Lines of stones running outward like arms from the centre.
Flat stone slabs marking smaller graves in the grass.
Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a windproof layer, since the open plateau is often windy.
Wear sturdy shoes for rough, uneven ground between the stones.
Come early morning or late afternoon for long, clear shadows.
Pair the visit with a walk through nearby Sisian town.
Stay on paths to protect graves and slow-growing lichen.
