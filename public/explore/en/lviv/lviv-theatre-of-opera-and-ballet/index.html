The guide

The story

Stand at the end of the long avenue and let your eyes travel upward. Pale stone, rows of columns, and at the very top a winged figure holding a golden palm branch toward the sky. Around you the pigeons lift and settle on the paving. Someone is playing an accordion near the fountain. In the evening the façade glows warm and honey-coloured. Few buildings in Lviv command a space the way this one does.

This is the Lviv Theatre of Opera and Ballet, the grand stage of western Ukraine. It closes the northern end of Freedom Avenue, the broad promenade at the heart of the city. Everything here points toward it. The avenue opens out like a long foyer under the open sky, and the theatre waits at the end like a stage set. This was a deliberate choice. The building was meant to be seen from far away, and to be approached slowly.

The theatre opened in 1900, at the very end of the nineteenth century. At that time Lviv belonged to the Austro-Hungarian Empire, and the city was known in German as Lemberg. Its theatre-going public was large and ambitious. They wanted a stage to rival those in Vienna, Prague, and Kraków. The project became a matter of civic pride, and the city funded a building on a scale it had never tried before.

The architect was Zygmunt Gorgolewski, a Polish architect who had trained and worked in Germany. He won the design competition and shaped the building in a grand, eclectic style. Its base is Neo-Renaissance — a revival of the calm, ordered forms of Italian palaces from the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Above that, he added the curves and drama of the Baroque, the more theatrical style that followed. The result feels both solemn and festive at once.

Here is something most visitors walk past without knowing. Under your feet, beneath the theatre and the avenue, runs a river. It is called the Poltva. For centuries it flowed openly through the city. By the late nineteenth century it was polluted and troublesome, and the city enclosed it in an underground channel. The land above became the promenade, and the theatre rose on top of the buried river.

Building on wet ground was a serious engineering challenge. The foundations had to hold a heavy stone structure over shifting, waterlogged soil. Gorgolewski used a thick concrete platform to spread the weight. Now comes the city's favourite story. Legend has it that cracks appeared in the walls after opening, and that the architect, in despair, took his own life. Locals still tell this tale. Historical records, however, say Gorgolewski died a few years later of natural causes, and the theatre still stands firmly today.

Look up again at the façade. The sculpture is dense and layered, like a stone programme for the evening. The winged figure at the top represents Glory. She is flanked by bronze groups that celebrate the arts. Lower down, in niches and along the cornice, more figures stand guard. Allegories of music, drama, and comedy appear across the front. The whole surface tells you, before you step inside, what kind of experience this building offers.

If you go in, the first surprise is the Mirror Hall. This upper foyer was designed for the interval, for seeing and being seen. Tall mirrors, gilded mouldings, and chandeliers multiply the light. In the late nineteenth century, a night at the opera was as much about the audience as the performance. People came to promenade, to show a new dress, to meet the right people. This room was built for exactly that.

The auditorium itself follows the classic horseshoe shape of European opera houses. Tiers of boxes curve around the hall in red and gold. The ceiling carries painted allegories, and the acoustics carry a single voice to the very back. But the real treasure hangs at the front. It is the stage curtain, painted by Henryk Siemiradzki, a celebrated academic painter of the era. It is known as Parnassus, after the mountain where Greek myth placed the home of the Muses.

That curtain is an artwork in its own right, crowded with allegorical figures representing the arts and the passions of human life. Many guidebooks point out that it is lowered only on certain occasions, so not every visitor sees it. If you catch it, take your time. It is one of the great painted curtains of Central Europe, and it has survived wars, changes of borders, and changes of rulers across more than a century.

Those changes left their mark on the theatre's name. Over the twentieth century, the city passed from Austrian to Polish to Soviet rule, and finally into independent Ukraine. Each era renamed and reshaped the company. Since 2000, the theatre has carried the name of Solomiya Krushelnytska, a Ukrainian soprano with close ties to Lviv. She sang on great stages across Europe, including Milan's La Scala, and the city remains proud of her.

Today the company performs opera and ballet, from Ukrainian works to the classic European repertoire. The building is also a meeting point in daily life. Students sit on the steps. Couples take photographs by the fountain. Street musicians play for coins, and on warm evenings the avenue fills with families walking slowly toward the light. You do not need a ticket to feel part of this place. The square in front works like an open-air stage of its own.

Before you move on, turn around for a moment. Look back down Freedom Avenue, the way the theatre itself looks at the city. Somewhere beneath the paving, the Poltva still runs in the dark. Above it, the stone, the gilding, and the winged figure of Glory hold their place. Listen to the fountain, the footsteps, the music. Then walk on slowly, and glance back once more as the façade catches the last of the light.
