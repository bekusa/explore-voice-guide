The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel shifts under your shoes, and somewhere above you a crow calls from the old trees. The air here is cooler than in the streets you just left. It smells of damp earth, moss, and candle wax. The paths slope uphill and curve away into shade. Stone angels, iron crosses and weathered portraits stand among the roots on every side.

You are standing in Lychakiv Cemetery, on the eastern edge of central Lviv. It is one of the oldest cemeteries still in use in this part of Europe, and it is also a museum. People come here to walk, to remember, and to look at sculpture. Many visitors arrive expecting a short stop. They often stay for hours, because the paths keep leading somewhere new.

The cemetery began in the late eighteenth century. The date usually given is 1786. At that time Lviv belonged to the Habsburg Empire. Austrian authorities ordered that burials should move out of crowded churchyards inside the city, mainly for reasons of public health. New cemeteries opened on the edges of town, and this hillside in the Lychakiv district was one of them. Over time it became the most important of them all.

That Habsburg rule shaped the city for more than a century, and you can read it in the stones. Look at the inscriptions as you walk. You will find Polish, Ukrainian, German, Latin, and sometimes Armenian or Hebrew lettering. Lviv was a city of many communities, and all of them left their names here. A single path can take you past a Polish count, a Ukrainian priest, and an Austrian official.

Now notice the sculpture itself. In the nineteenth century, wealthy families in Lviv paid local and foreign sculptors to carve elaborate tombs. The result is an open-air gallery. There are grieving women in marble, sleeping children, broken columns, and draped urns. A broken column was a common sign of a life cut short. Many figures are now dark with age and green with lichen. The forest has slowly grown around them.

Here is something many visitors miss. The cemetery is divided into numbered fields, and these numbers help you find specific graves. Small signs mark them along the paths. The ground is also steep in places, so the oldest graves sit on terraces and slopes rather than in neat rows. This is not a flat lawn of headstones. It feels more like a hillside forest that happens to hold thousands of graves.

One of the most visited graves belongs to Ivan Franko. He was a Ukrainian poet, novelist and thinker of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. His monument shows a powerful figure of a stonebreaker, swinging a hammer against rock. The image comes from one of Franko's best-known poems. In that poem, the stonebreakers clear a path through solid rock for future generations. People often leave fresh flowers here.

Not far away, you can look for the grave of Solomiya Krushelnytska. She was a Ukrainian soprano who sang on the great opera stages of Europe, including La Scala in Milan. She returned to Lviv later in life and taught at the city's conservatory. The opera house in Lviv now carries her name. Her grave is a quiet place, and music lovers come to stand beside it.

Another name draws many younger visitors. Volodymyr Ivasyuk was a composer who wrote the song Chervona Ruta, a much-loved Ukrainian pop song from the Soviet era. He died in 1979, in circumstances that many people still consider suspicious. His funeral became a quiet moment of protest. Even today, people sing his songs, and his grave is often covered with flowers and candles.

Polish culture is also deeply present here. The painter Artur Grottger, known for his dramatic drawings of the Polish uprising of the 1860s, has a tomb with a carved figure. The writer Gabriela Zapolska is buried here too. Nearby lies the Cemetery of the Defenders of Lwów. It holds young Polish soldiers who died in the fighting for the city around 1918 and 1919. It was damaged in the Soviet years and restored much later.

This is where the cemetery becomes more than history. Lviv has lived through empires, wars and border changes. Poles and Ukrainians fought over this city, and their memories do not always agree. Both stories lie on the same hill, sometimes only a short walk apart. Standing here, you can see how a city keeps many pasts at once. The cemetery holds them side by side, without asking them to match.

And the story is not finished. In recent years, a memorial area known as the Field of Mars has filled with graves of Ukrainian soldiers. Many died in Russia's war against Ukraine. Here the colours change. You will see fresh flags, photographs of young faces, and many flowers. Families often visit. If you go there, please move quietly and give people space. This part of the cemetery is grief that is still very new.

As you walk, take time for the small details. Look for old enamel photographs on the headstones, faded to pale blue and brown. Notice iron fences with rusted leaves and crosses. Watch how the light falls between the tall trees and lands on a single marble face. In autumn, fallen leaves cover the paths in gold. In winter, snow sits on the shoulders of the stone angels.

Before you leave, stop for a moment and look back up the slope. Behind you are more than two centuries of Lviv: poets, singers, soldiers, children, and families whose names nobody remembers anymore. Listen again to the gravel, the birds, the wind in the branches. Then walk slowly down toward the gate, and toward the living city, which is still writing its next chapter.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for steep, uneven gravel paths.
Ask for a map at the entrance to find specific fields.
Visit early morning for quieter paths and soft light.
Speak softly near the Field of Mars soldiers' graves.
Allow at least two hours to explore without rushing.
