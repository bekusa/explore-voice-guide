The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look at the ground. The soil here is pale and dry, and the hills around you show chalky slopes under thin grass. On a warm day the air feels clear and mineral. Under your feet, and in the rock below, lies the reason this place exists at all. It is salt. Long before Rome, long before the pyramids of Egypt, people came to this valley because the earth gave them something more valuable than gold.

This is Solnitsata, an archaeological site just outside the town of Provadia in north-eastern Bulgaria. The name comes from an old Bulgarian word for a salt works, or a salt cellar. That name is an honest summary of the site. For many centuries in the Copper Age, people here produced salt on a scale that was unusual for its time. They then traded it across a wide region.

To understand why that mattered, imagine life without a supermarket. Salt preserved meat and fish through the winter. It kept livestock healthy. It made food last on long journeys. In much of prehistoric Europe, salt was hard to get away from the sea. Here, a deep salt deposit sits under the valley, and salty springs rise to the surface. Whoever controlled those springs controlled a resource that every community needed.

Archaeologists from the Bulgarian National Institute of Archaeology began serious excavations here in the mid-2000s. The team was led by Professor Vassil Nikolov. Their work showed that salt making on this spot started very early, back in the Neolithic, the late Stone Age. It reached its peak in the Chalcolithic, also called the Copper Age, roughly in the fifth millennium BCE. That is more than six thousand years ago.

The method was simple, but it required hard work and organisation. Workers collected brine from the springs. They poured it into large, shallow clay vessels and heated them inside domed kilns. As the water boiled away, a crust of salt remained. The salt could then be shaped into solid blocks, which were easy to carry and easy to trade. Excavators found many fragments of these vessels. In places they lie in thick layers, the waste of many years of production.

Here is the first detail that surprises most visitors. Salt was so valuable that the settlement needed protection. Archaeologists uncovered the remains of massive stone walls around the site. These walls are widely described as some of the earliest known stone fortifications in Europe. In the Copper Age, most villages in this region used wood, earth and clay. A wall of heavy stone tells us that the people here had something worth defending, and the means to organise that defence.

Inside the walls, the excavations found houses, some of them apparently built with two levels. Researchers also found pits used for ritual activity, and a small cemetery nearby. The settlement was not large by modern standards. It probably held a few hundred people at most. But it shows planning, specialised work and clear social order. It looks less like a farming village and more like an industrial centre.

In 2012, the archaeologists announced that Solnitsata could be called the oldest prehistoric town in Europe. That claim travelled quickly around the world. It is worth knowing that scholars still debate it. The word town means different things to different researchers, and other sites in south-eastern Europe also have early claims. What is not in doubt is this: Solnitsata is one of the earliest places in Europe where a single product shaped an entire community.

Now for the second surprise. About forty kilometres east of here, near the city of Varna, archaeologists found a Copper Age cemetery in the 1970s. Some of the graves held large amounts of worked gold. Many specialists consider it the oldest processed gold found anywhere in the world. Researchers have suggested that the wealth of Varna may be connected to the salt trade from this valley. Salt went out, and gold and prestige goods came back. It is a theory, not a proven fact. But it is a very attractive one.

The settlement did not last forever. Around the end of the Copper Age, life across this region changed sharply. Many settlements in the eastern Balkans were abandoned, and the reasons are still discussed. Possible factors include climate change, conflict and new groups moving into the area. At Solnitsata, the archaeological layers show traces of destruction. After that, the great age of prehistoric salt in this valley came to an end.

The salt itself stayed in the ground. That is perhaps the quietest lesson of this place. Salt extraction around Provadia continues in the modern era, using very different technology. As you look around, notice how the valley still carries signs of industry. The same geology that drew Copper Age workers here still has economic value today. Few landscapes show such a long thread between the deep past and the present.

As you walk the site, take your time with the stone foundations. They are low and modest, and it is easy to pass them quickly. But try to picture the scene. Smoke rose from the kilns. Workers carried heavy clay vessels of brine. Traders arrived with goods from far away. The landscape around you was the same shape then, with the same hills and the same pale soil. Only the people and the buildings have gone.

Before you leave, look at the ground once more. Somewhere beneath the surface there are still fragments of those old salt vessels, the broken tools of a very early industry. Breathe the dry air and listen to the wind across the slopes. Six thousand years ago, someone stood very near where you are standing. They were thinking about salt, about trade and about the winter ahead. Walk back slowly, and carry that thought with you.
