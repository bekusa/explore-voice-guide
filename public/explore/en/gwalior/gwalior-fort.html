The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. Up here, on the flat top of a long sandstone hill, the air moves freely above the city. Below you, the honking and engine noise of Gwalior rise as a soft hum. Under your feet the stone is warm and pale, worn smooth by centuries of sandals, hooves and wheels. The cliffs drop away sharply on every side. That steep drop is the whole reason this fort exists.

The hill stands alone above the plains of northern Madhya Pradesh, in central India. Its sides are almost vertical, and its top is long and narrow, like a ship of rock. For a ruler, this shape was a gift. A small force on top could watch every road for a great distance. Many dynasties held this plateau over more than a thousand years. Each one added walls, gates, temples and palaces.

The story of how the city got its name is a legend, so treat it as one. Locals say that a chieftain called Suraj Sen once came here suffering from a terrible illness. A hermit named Gwalipa gave him water from a pond on the hill, and he was cured. In gratitude, the chieftain named the place after the sage. You can still see a large stepped tank on the plateau, called Suraj Kund, which the tale connects to that miracle.

Now turn toward the palace with the coloured walls. This is Man Mandir, built by Raja Man Singh Tomar around the end of the fifteenth century. The Tomars were a Rajput clan, a family of warrior kings, who ruled here for generations. Look at the outer face of the palace. Bands of glazed tiles in blue, green and yellow run across the sandstone. Even after five centuries, the colours catch the sun.

Look more closely at those tiles, and you will find animals. There are rows of ducks, elephants, and what look like banana trees. In other places you may spot peacocks and crocodiles. Few royal buildings of this period in India carry this much colour on the outside. For many visitors, this is the first surprise. They expect a grey military fortress, and instead they find a palace decorated like a painted box.

Inside, the mood changes. The palace has several levels below ground, built partly to stay cool in the fierce summer heat. Later, under the Mughal emperors, these lower chambers had a darker use. They became a state prison for political rivals and even members of the royal family. Walk down slowly. Notice how the temperature drops, how the light fades, and how quiet the stone becomes.

The Mughal founder Babur came here in the early sixteenth century. He is often quoted as calling Gwalior the pearl among the fortresses of India. Whatever his exact words, the Mughals clearly valued the fort. They used it as a stronghold and a prison for generations. After them came the Marathas, and later the Scindia family. Each ruler left a layer behind, and you are walking across all of them at once.

Now for a fact that most visitors walk straight past. On this plateau stands a small rock-cut shrine known as the Chaturbhuj temple, dedicated to Vishnu. It carries an inscription from the ninth century. In that inscription, the number zero is written as a small circle. It is one of the oldest known examples of zero written this way. The same zero now sits on every phone screen and every calculator in the world.

Further along the hill rises a tall temple called Teli ka Mandir. It dates from around the eighth or ninth century, which makes it one of the oldest buildings here. Its roof is unusual. Instead of the pointed tower common in northern Indian temples, it has a long barrel-shaped top, more often seen in southern India. Stand at its base and look up. The walls are covered with carved figures and window-like decorations.

Then there are the Sas-Bahu temples, a pair of carved temples from the eleventh century. In Hindi, the name means mother-in-law and daughter-in-law, and visitors often assume a family story lies behind it. In fact, the name most likely comes from Sahasrabahu, meaning the thousand-armed one, a title of Vishnu. Over time, people shortened the word into something familiar. Look at the ceilings and pillars, where almost every surface carries ornament.

As you leave the plateau, look at the cliffs themselves. Along the roads up the hill, huge figures are carved directly into the rock face. These are Jain sculptures, mostly from the fifteenth century, showing the Tirthankaras, the great spiritual teachers of the Jain faith. Some stand many times taller than a person. Several faces are damaged, a reminder of later conflicts. Yet the calm, upright bodies still look out across the city.

The hill also matters to the Sikh community. Sikh tradition holds that the sixth Guru, Guru Hargobind, was held prisoner here by the Mughal emperor Jahangir. When he was freed, the story says, he insisted that fifty-two imprisoned princes be released with him, each holding a fringe of his cloak. A gurdwara, a Sikh temple, called Data Bandi Chhor now marks the memory. You may hear hymns from it drifting across the plateau.

In 1858, during the great uprising against British rule, fighting came to Gwalior. Rani Lakshmibai, the queen of Jhansi, died in battle near the city, and the British then took the fort. Today, the plateau feels peaceful. Students from the Scindia School, which has stood on the hill since the late nineteenth century, cross paths with pilgrims and families on day trips.

Before you go, find a spot along the wall and look out. The city spreads below you in every direction. Behind you, the tiles of Man Mandir hold their blue and yellow in the light. Listen for a moment to the wind and the distant traffic. Then, as you walk back down, look again at the cliffs, and the stone faces watching you leave.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the steep roads up the hill.
Bring water and a hat for the exposed plateau.
Visit Man Mandir's lower chambers to feel the cool air.
Allow a full half day to see the temples properly.
Walk down past the cliffs to view the Jain carvings.
