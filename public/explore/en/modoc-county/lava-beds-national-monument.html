The guide

The story

Listen to the ground for a moment. Under your boots the rock crunches like broken pottery. It is dark, sharp, and full of tiny holes, as if the earth froze while it was still boiling. The air smells of sagebrush and dry juniper. On a still day, the loudest sound may be the wind moving across miles of high, open country. Welcome to Lava Beds National Monument, in the far northeastern corner of California.

The land around you was shaped by one enormous neighbour. To the south rises Medicine Lake Volcano. It does not look like a classic cone. Instead, it is a broad, gentle shield that spreads across the horizon. Measured by volume, it is the largest volcano in the entire Cascade Range. That is bigger than Mount Shasta, the snowy peak you may see to the west. Over hundreds of thousands of years, this shield poured lava north, and much of that lava ended up right here.

Here is the first surprise. Much of the most important landscape is not on the surface at all. It is underneath you. When rivers of molten rock flowed across this ground, their outer skin cooled and hardened. The hot lava inside kept moving. When the flow drained away, it left behind long, hollow tunnels. These are called lava tubes. The monument protects hundreds of these caves, more than almost anywhere else in North America.

Each cave has its own personality. Some are tall enough to walk through upright, with smooth, curved walls. Others force you onto your hands and knees. Look closely at the walls and you may see horizontal lines, like bathtub rings. These mark the levels where the lava once flowed and paused. In some tubes, small drips of molten rock hardened on the ceiling. Cavers call them lavacicles. In others, golden or silver films shine in your flashlight beam. Those are bacteria and minerals, not paint.

Some caves hold another secret — ice. In the deepest, coldest tubes, winter air sinks inside and stays trapped. Water seeps in, freezes, and can remain frozen through the hot summer above. Skull Cave is one well-known example, with a cold floor far below the entrance. It feels strange to stand in the high desert, sweating in the sun, and then walk a few minutes into air cold enough to see your breath.

If you are new to caving, Mushpot Cave near the visitor centre is the gentle place to start. It has lighting and a paved path, so you can see the shapes of a lava tube without crawling. Most other caves are wild. They have no lights, uneven floors, and low ceilings. Rangers ask every visitor to get a free cave permit and answer a short screening first. This helps protect the bats from a fungal disease called white-nose syndrome.

Now lift your eyes to the surface again. The lava here came in two main textures, and both have Hawaiian names. Pahoehoe is smooth and ropy, like folded cake batter. Aa is rough, broken, and painfully sharp. Walking on aa is slow and hard. That simple fact about rock shaped one of the most dramatic chapters in the history of the American West.

For thousands of years, this was the homeland of the Modoc people. They fished and hunted around the lakes and marshes to the north. In the 1860s, white settlers pushed into the region, and the United States government moved the Modoc onto a reservation they shared with the Klamath, a rival people. Conditions there were difficult. A band of Modoc, led by a man named Kintpuash, left and returned to their home country. Settlers called him Captain Jack.

In late 1872, the army tried to force the band back, and fighting broke out. The Modoc retreated into the lava beds. They chose a natural fortress of ridges, trenches, and cracks in the rock. Today it is called Captain Jack's Stronghold. With only a few dozen fighters, they held off a much larger army for months. The soldiers struggled across the sharp lava, while the Modoc knew every hiding place.

Then came the most shocking moment of the war. In April 1873, during peace talks, Modoc leaders killed General Edward Canby. He became the only United States general killed during the wars against Native nations in the West. The army answered with greater force. By that summer, the Modoc band was defeated. Kintpuash and several others were hanged. Many survivors were sent far away to Indian Territory, in what is now Oklahoma. A trail through the Stronghold lets you walk among the trenches today.

Near the northeastern edge of the monument stands another reminder of human life here. Petroglyph Point is a tall cliff of volcanic rock that once rose from the water of an ancient lake. People paddled to its base and carved thousands of symbols into the soft stone. It is one of the largest panels of rock art in California. Their exact meaning is not fully known. Please look, but never touch the carvings. Even gentle hands slowly wear them away.

If you want the big view, climb Schonchin Butte. It is a cinder cone, a steep hill built from loose volcanic fragments. A fire lookout sits on the summit. From the top, you can see the dark lava flows, the shield of Medicine Lake Volcano, and the flat basin of Tule Lake. On clear evenings, this remote place also offers deep, dark skies. With little light pollution, the stars and the Milky Way can be bright.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Feel the rough rock under your feet and the dry wind on your face. Somewhere below you, a tunnel of cold darkness runs quietly through the ground. Above you, hawks may circle over the sagebrush. This place holds volcanic fire, frozen caves, and painful human history, all within a short walk. As you head back to the road, watch the black lava flows in the changing light.

Before you go

Practical tips

Get a cave permit at the visitor centre before entering caves.
Carry at least two light sources for every person.
Start in Mushpot Cave if you are new to caving.
Wear sturdy boots because the aa lava is very sharp.
Stay after dark to enjoy the remote night skies.
