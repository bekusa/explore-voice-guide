The guide

The story

Put your hand on the nearest boulder. The granite is rough and warm, and it holds the heat of the sun long after the light starts to fade. All around you, huge rounded rocks sit on top of each other, some balanced so finely that they look ready to roll. Between them, you will see carved pillars, broken gateways and temple towers. This is Vijayanagara, the City of Victory, and the ruins spread across a wide landscape near the modern town of Hosapete.

Today most people call this place Hampi, after the small village at its heart. The name comes from Pampa, an old name for the Tungabhadra River and for a local goddess linked with it. The river runs along the northern edge of the ruins, wide and green, and it shaped everything here. It gave water, protection and a sacred meaning. In 1986, UNESCO listed the Group of Monuments at Hampi as a World Heritage Site.

The story of the city begins in the fourteenth century. Tradition says two brothers, Harihara and Bukka of the Sangama family, founded the kingdom around 1336. Over the next two hundred years, it grew into one of the largest empires in southern India. It controlled much of the Deccan plateau, the high, dry land in the middle of the peninsula. Its capital became a place of kings, priests, soldiers, traders and thousands of ordinary workers.

Why build a capital here, among these rocks? Look around and the answer becomes clear. The boulders and ridges form natural walls. The river closes the north side. Granite lay everywhere, ready for builders to cut and carve. Even today, you can find stones with lines of small square holes along one edge. Workers cut those holes and drove in wedges to split the rock. Some stones were never finished, and they still show the marks.

Long before the empire, this land already held a sacred story. Local tradition links the area with Kishkindha, the monkey kingdom in the Ramayana, the great Hindu epic about the god Rama. Locals say that Hanuman, the monkey hero devoted to Rama, was born on a hill nearby. This is legend, not documented history. But you will see monkeys everywhere among the temples, and many visitors find it hard not to smile at the connection.

The oldest living heart of Hampi is the Virupaksha Temple, near the village bazaar. It is dedicated to Shiva, one of the main Hindu gods. Unlike most of the ruins, this temple is still in active worship. Its tall entrance tower rises above the village, layered with carved figures. Inside, you may hear bells, smell incense and see a temple elephant blessing visitors with its trunk, depending on the day. Here, the past and present use the same stone floor.

The empire reached its high point under King Krishnadevaraya, who ruled in the early sixteenth century. He was a successful military leader and a patron of poets and temples. Foreign visitors left written accounts of the city in this period. A Portuguese traveller named Domingo Paes described its markets, festivals and wealth. Earlier, in the fifteenth century, a Persian envoy called Abdur Razzaq also recorded his visit. Both writers were clearly impressed by the size of the capital.

Those markets once lined the long streets that you can still trace today. In front of several temples, rows of stone pavilions run in straight lines along wide roads. Historians believe that traders sold goods here, from cloth and grain to horses and precious stones. Now the pavilions stand empty, and goats sometimes rest in the shade. Walk slowly along one of these streets and try to picture crowds, carts and the noise of bargaining.

Now for one of the surprises of Vijayanagara. At the Vittala Temple, near the river, some of the slender stone pillars are known as musical pillars. When struck, they are said to produce different tones, like an instrument. Today visitors are not allowed to tap them, because years of touching caused damage. Nearby stands the famous stone chariot, a small shrine carved to look like a temple cart with wheels. You may already know it — it appears on India's fifty rupee note.

The royal centre feels very different from the sacred centre. Here you find the great stone platform called the Mahanavami Dibba, decorated with carved scenes of horses, hunters, dancers and soldiers. Nearby is a stepped tank, a square water basin with neat steps cut in a geometric pattern. Within the walled area known as the Zenana Enclosure stands the Lotus Mahal, with arched openings that mix Hindu and Islamic styles. A short walk away are the long Elephant Stables, with domed chambers in a row.

The ending of this city came suddenly. In 1565, the army of Vijayanagara lost the Battle of Talikota against an alliance of neighbouring sultanates. After the defeat, the capital was looted and burned over several months. The empire continued in other places, but the great city never recovered. This is the second surprise for many visitors. Most of what you see did not slowly fall apart. Much of it was broken in a short and violent period.

Despite that destruction, a great deal survives, partly because granite is so hard. Look at the giant statue of Narasimha, a form of the god Vishnu who is half man and half lion. It is carved from a single boulder, with large staring eyes. Also look closely at the smaller details everywhere: carved fish, elephants, dancers and small animals on pillars and walls. The builders put care into corners that most people walk past without noticing.

The village of Hampi itself is lively in a quiet way. Small guesthouses, cafes and shops sell tea, bananas and souvenirs. On the river, you may see round boats called coracles, woven from bamboo and covered to keep out the water. Boatmen spin them gently across the current. In the early morning and late afternoon, the light turns the boulders orange and pink, and the whole landscape seems to soften.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Listen to the wind between the rocks, the call of a bird, perhaps a temple bell from the village. Look at the stone under your feet and the carving beside you. Six centuries ago, someone shaped it by hand. As you walk away, notice how the boulders and the ruins blend together. Here, it is often hard to tell where nature ends and the city begins.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at sunrise to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Rent a bicycle or auto-rickshaw to cover distant monument groups.
Carry plenty of water and wear a wide-brimmed hat.
Remove shoes before entering the active Virupaksha Temple.
Keep food hidden, as temple monkeys grab bags quickly.
