The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The stone floor of the courtyard is cool where the shadows fall, and warm where the sun has been sitting on it since morning. Somewhere ahead, a bell rings, then rings again. You can smell camphor smoke, wet flowers, and the faint sweetness of bananas left as offerings. This is Virupaksha Temple, and unlike most of the ruins scattered across Hampi, it is still a living place of worship.

That single fact changes everything about how you experience it. Many of the great stone buildings around you are now quiet shells. Here, priests still perform daily rituals. Families still arrive with coconuts and garlands. Pilgrims still walk barefoot across the same paving that worshippers crossed many centuries ago. Many scholars believe there has been a shrine on this spot since well before the Vijayanagara Empire rose to power in the fourteenth century.

The temple is dedicated to the Hindu god Shiva, worshipped here as Virupaksha. The name is often explained as the one with unusual eyes, a reference to the third eye Shiva carries on his forehead. Shiva does not live here alone. His consort is Pampa, a local goddess closely tied to the Tungabhadra River that flows just behind the temple. In fact, the old name for this whole area, Pampa Kshetra, means the sacred land of Pampa.

Now look back at the tower you walked through to enter. That great eastern gateway is called a gopuram — a tall, tiered entrance tower typical of South Indian temples. It rises in nine stacked levels, each one slightly narrower than the one below. The lower sections are made of stone. The upper tiers are built from brick and plaster. Much of what you see today was restored in the nineteenth century, long after the empire had fallen.

To understand that fall, picture the scale of this city at its height. Vijayanagara, which means City of Victory, was the capital of one of the largest Hindu kingdoms in Indian history. Foreign travellers who visited in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries described markets full of gems, horses, and spices. Then, in 1565, the empire lost a major battle against an alliance of neighbouring sultanates. The capital was sacked over the months that followed. Palaces burned. Temples were damaged.

Yet this temple kept going. Worship here continued through the centuries after the destruction, and that continuity is one reason it feels so different from the empty monuments nearby. When you hear the chanting from the inner sanctum, you are hearing a tradition that outlasted the very empire that once paid for these walls.

Walk deeper into the complex and you will reach a pillared hall known as the Ranga Mantapa. Inscriptions connect this hall to Krishnadevaraya, the most celebrated of the Vijayanagara kings. He is said to have added it around 1510, to mark his coronation. Look closely at the pillars. Many of them are carved with yalis — mythical creatures with the body of a lion, the face of something fiercer, and sometimes a rider balanced on their backs.

Now tilt your head back. This is the detail most visitors walk straight past. The ceiling of the hall is painted. The colours have faded to ochre, rust, and soft greens, but you can still make out figures and scenes from Hindu mythology. Among them, look for images connected to Shiva and his marriage. Paintings from this period are rare survivors, so take a slow moment here before moving on.

Here is a second surprise. Somewhere in the complex, ask an attendant or follow the small crowd toward a dim chamber near the rear. On the wall inside, in the right light, you may see an upside-down shadow image of the gopuram. A small opening in the stone works like a pinhole camera, projecting the tower onto the wall. Nobody can say for certain whether the builders planned this effect. Either way, it delights almost everyone who sees it.

The temple is also a place of celebration. Each year, festivals mark the marriage of Virupaksha and Pampa, and the town fills with pilgrims, music, and decorated temple chariots. Even on an ordinary day, you might see a newly married couple here for a blessing, or a child having a first important ceremony. Watch quietly, keep a respectful distance, and you will see the temple doing what it has done for generations.

Keep an eye on the rooftops and walls, too. Monkeys are permanent residents here, and they are very interested in anything that looks like food. Hold your bags close and keep snacks out of sight. You will also notice small shrines tucked into corners around the courtyards, each with its own deity, its own oil lamp, and often its own small pile of flowers left by a visitor before you.

When you step back out through the eastern gateway, the long, straight street in front of you is Hampi Bazaar. Stone pavilions line both sides. In the empire's day, merchants traded here, and the street served as a grand processional route for the temple chariot. At the far end, a large stone Nandi — the sacred bull who carries Shiva — sits facing back toward the temple, keeping watch down the whole length of the road.

The Virupaksha Temple is part of the Group of Monuments at Hampi, recognised by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site in 1986. Around you, the landscape seems almost unreal. Huge granite boulders sit balanced on hilltops, coloured orange and pink in the late light. Hemakuta Hill rises just to the south, covered in smaller, older temples. Behind you, the river runs on, as it did when the first shrine was built here.

Before you leave, pause in the courtyard one more time. Listen for the bell. Notice the smoke drifting up from the lamps, the coolness of the shaded stone, the quiet movement of people who come here not as tourists, but because this is still their temple. As you walk away down the bazaar street, turn once and look back at the gopuram. It is still doing its first job — showing everyone, from far away, exactly where the sacred ground begins.

Before you go

Practical tips

Remove your shoes at the entrance before entering the complex.
Come early morning to hear rituals before the crowds arrive.
Keep snacks hidden from the resident monkeys.
Dress modestly with shoulders and knees covered.
Walk Hampi Bazaar to the stone Nandi after your visit.
