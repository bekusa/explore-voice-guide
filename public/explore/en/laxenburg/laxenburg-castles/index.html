The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere beyond the trees, water laps against a stone edge. The air smells of cut grass and damp leaves. Ahead, between old trunks, the pale walls of a palace come into view. This is Laxenburg, a small market town just south of Vienna. For centuries it was one of the places where the Habsburg family came to escape the city and breathe a little easier.

The Habsburgs were the dynasty that ruled Austria and much of central Europe for more than six hundred years. Vienna was their stage, full of ceremony and politics. Laxenburg was something else. It was a country retreat, a hunting ground, a summer home. Here the court could ride, row, picnic and play. That spirit of leisure is still in the air today. People walk dogs, push prams and sit on benches facing the water.

The name "Laxenburg castles" is plural for a reason. There is not one palace here, but several, spread across the town and its great park. Each belongs to a different age. Think of the site as a timeline you can walk through. The oldest building is medieval at its core. Another is a baroque residence. The most surprising one is a romantic fantasy of a castle, built on an island in a lake.

Start with the Old Castle, the Altes Schloss. Its roots go back to the Middle Ages, when the Habsburgs acquired the estate and used it as a hunting lodge. The walls are thick and the shape is compact and practical. This was a building for shelter, not for show. Today it has an unexpected role. It houses part of the Austrian Film Archive, so behind these old walls, reels of cinema history are kept safe.

Now turn to the Blue Court, the Blauer Hof. This is the main palace of the town, and it faces the square. Empress Maria Theresa, who ruled in the eighteenth century, loved Laxenburg. She enlarged this residence and spent many spring seasons here with her large family. The building has the calm, even rhythm of baroque architecture, with long rows of tall windows and a wide courtyard. It feels open and orderly.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Blue Court was the birthplace of Crown Prince Rudolf, in 1858. He was the only son of Emperor Franz Joseph and Empress Elisabeth, the empress the world remembers as Sisi. Rudolf's later life ended in tragedy at the hunting lodge of Mayerling. But his story began here, in these quiet rooms beside the park. A few years earlier, in 1854, Franz Joseph and Elisabeth had spent their honeymoon at Laxenburg.

The Blue Court has a second surprise. Since the 1970s, it has been home to the International Institute for Applied Systems Analysis. This is a research centre where scientists from many countries study global problems — energy, climate, population and food. So the palace where an empress once raised her children is now a place where researchers model the future of the planet. It is not a museum. People work here, and you can often see them crossing the courtyard.

Now walk into the park. It is very large, and it was reshaped in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries in the English landscape style. That style was a reaction against the strict geometry of French gardens. Instead of straight lines and clipped hedges, designers created winding paths, open meadows, clusters of trees and irregular lakes. The goal was to look natural, even though almost everything was carefully planned. Watch how the paths curve to hide a view, and then suddenly open one.

Scattered through the park are small buildings and monuments from that romantic period. Some are temples, some are bridges, some are pavilions placed at the end of a view. They were made to be discovered on a walk, like scenes in a story. Many visitors simply follow the water. That is a good instinct. The lakes and canals connect much of the park, and they lead you towards the most famous building of all.

There it is. The Franzensburg rises from a small island in the lake. It looks like a medieval knight's castle, with towers, battlements and red and grey stone. But it is not medieval. It was built in the decades around 1800 for Emperor Franz, who ruled at the time of Napoleon. It is a romantic fantasy — a dream of the Middle Ages, created when people in Europe were falling in love with the idea of knights, ruins and old legends.

The second surprise of Franzensburg is inside. Emperor Franz did not only imitate the past. He collected it. Builders brought old elements from other buildings around the Habsburg lands, such as carved woodwork, panelling, glass and architectural pieces, and set them into the new castle. So the Franzensburg is a kind of patchwork. Some parts are genuinely old, and some are new work made to look old. The castle also became a monument to the Habsburg family, with halls full of portraits of the dynasty.

Getting to the island is part of the experience. In the warmer months, a small ferry carries visitors across the water, guided along a fixed line. There is also a bridge. The crossing is short, but it changes how you see the castle. From the boat, the towers grow taller, and their reflection breaks into ripples. Guided tours lead visitors through the interior, so check on site how to join one.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the lake for a moment. Look at the towers of the Franzensburg against the sky, and the trees that frame them. Everything around you was designed to make people slow down — the curved paths, the still water, the distant roofs. As you walk back towards the town, listen again for the gravel under your feet. It is the same sound emperors, empresses and their children heard on these paths, many summers ago.
