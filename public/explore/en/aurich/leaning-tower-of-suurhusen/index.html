The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look up. The red brick tower in front of you does not stand straight. It tips, clearly and visibly, toward one side, as if it had stopped halfway through a slow bow. Your eyes try to correct it. They cannot. Around you, the flat marshland of East Frisia stretches toward the horizon. The wind comes across the fields with almost nothing to stop it, and it carries the smell of grass and wet earth.

This is the church tower of Suurhusen, a small village in the municipality of Hinte, in the Aurich district of Lower Saxony, in north-western Germany. For a few years in the early twenty-first century, this modest village tower held a surprising title. The Guinness World Records listed it as the most tilted tower in the world. Its lean is a little over five degrees. That is more than the famous bell tower in Pisa, which leans just under four degrees today.

The church and the tower belong to different periods. The church hall is the older part, built in the Middle Ages, around the thirteenth century. The tower was added later, in the fifteenth century, against the western end of the church. Both are made of brick. That matters here. East Frisia has very little natural stone, so builders in this region learned to work with fired clay. The deep red colour you see comes from local clay, baked in kilns.

Now look at the ground beneath the church. The building stands on a slight rise. This is a warft, an artificial mound of earth. People along this coast built such mounds over centuries to keep their homes and churches above floodwater. Before the great dikes, storm tides from the North Sea could flood the low land. A warft was a place of safety. A church on a warft was often the highest and driest point in the village.

And here is the first surprise. The tower does not rest on stone foundations. According to accounts of its construction, it stands on oak tree trunks, driven into the wet, peaty soil. For centuries, this worked well. The waterlogged ground kept the oak wet, and wood that stays wet and away from air can last a very long time. The marsh preserved the foundations that held the tower up.

Then the land changed. In the nineteenth century, people drained the surrounding marshes to create better farmland. The water table dropped. The oak trunks under the tower were no longer fully under water. Air reached the wood, and the wood began to rot. As the foundations weakened, the heavy brick tower began to sink unevenly. So the lean is not a medieval mistake. It is mostly a result of later changes to the land around it.

The tilt did not appear overnight. It grew slowly, over many decades. By the second half of the twentieth century, the tower was considered unsafe. It was closed to visitors in the 1970s. Engineers then worked to stabilise it. They did not try to pull it straight. Instead, they secured it in its leaning position. After this work, the tower opened again in the 1980s. The goal was preservation, not correction.

Here is the second surprise. The top of the tower hangs out beyond its base by roughly two and a half metres. Stand at the foot of the leaning side and look straight up. You will see the upper walls far above you, but not directly above you. The tower is about twenty-seven metres high, about the height of a nine-storey building. It is not huge. But the lean makes it feel much more dramatic.

For a while, Suurhusen held its world record proudly. Then other contenders appeared. A modern skyscraper in Abu Dhabi was designed with a deliberate tilt, and Guinness created a separate category for intentionally leaning buildings. Later, a church tower in another part of Germany was measured as leaning slightly more. Records come and go. What stays is the simple fact that you are standing beside one of the most tilted medieval towers anywhere.

It also helps to remember that this is still a village church. It is not a museum piece. The congregation here belongs to the Reformed Protestant tradition, which has deep roots in East Frisia. Reformed churches are usually plain inside, with few images and simple furnishings. The focus is the spoken word, not decoration. So if you are able to step inside, expect calm and light rather than gold and paint.

Walk slowly around the outside. Notice the brick courses, the horizontal rows of bricks. On the leaning side, they tilt with the tower. Look at the windows and openings, and notice how their frames sit at an angle. Then look at the churchyard around you. Gravestones stand among the grass, and many of them carry local family names. The village life of Suurhusen continues around this tower every day.

The landscape is part of the story too. From the warft, you can see how flat this country is. Church towers like this one were landmarks for people crossing the marsh and for sailors near the coast. They were visible for long distances. In that wide, open land, a tall brick tower showed where a village stood. Today, the lean makes this one easy to recognise from far away.

Before you leave, stand back and look at the whole scene once more. The red tower tips against the sky. The church roof runs straight beside it, which makes the lean even clearer. The wind moves the grass on the mound. Somewhere nearby, you may hear birds over the fields. Five centuries of weather, water and human work all meet in this one leaning tower... and it is still standing.
