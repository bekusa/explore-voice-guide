The guide

The story

Feel the sand shift under your shoes as you climb. It is fine and golden, and it slides back a little with every step, so the hill seems to push you gently down toward the river. Behind you the Nile is a deep blue, broken by grey granite boulders and white feluccas. Ahead, cut into the pale cliff, a row of dark doorways looks out over the water. These are tombs, and they have watched this view for more than four thousand years.

This hill on the west bank of the Nile at Aswan is called Qubbet el-Hawa. In Arabic the name means "the Dome of the Wind." Look up to the very top of the ridge and you will see why. A small domed shrine sits on the summit, the tomb of a local holy man. The wind blows across this high point almost constantly, and on a hot afternoon it is the coolest place you can stand for miles.

The doorways below that dome belong to a much older world. They are the rock-cut tombs of the governors and officials of Elephantine, the island town you can see in the river just across from here. Most of them date to the Old Kingdom and the Middle Kingdom, the long early ages of pharaonic Egypt. The men buried here held one of the most important jobs in the kingdom. They guarded the southern frontier.

To understand that job, picture the map. For the ancient Egyptians, Elephantine marked the edge of Egypt proper. South of the First Cataract — the stretch of rocks and rapids around Aswan — lay Nubia. From Nubia came gold, ebony, incense, animal skins and ivory. The island's ancient name is usually linked to elephants and the ivory trade. The governors here were traders, soldiers and explorers, all at once.

As you walk along the terrace, notice how the tombs line up at almost the same height on the cliff. The builders chose a band of good sandstone and carved straight into it. Some tombs have long ramps or stairways running down toward the river. These were the processional routes. On the day of a funeral, the coffin came across the water by boat and was carried up the slope, with the whole town watching from the far bank.

One of the most famous tombs belongs to a man named Harkhuf. He lived during the Sixth Dynasty, near the end of the Old Kingdom. On the facade around his doorway, carved lines of hieroglyphs tell his life story. He describes long journeys south into Nubia, leading caravans of donkeys loaded with goods. It is one of the earliest travel accounts in human history, written on the front of a grave.

And here is the part most visitors miss. Harkhuf also had a royal letter copied onto his tomb. It came from the king Pepi the Second, who was still a young boy at the time. Harkhuf had sent word that he was bringing back a dancing dwarf from the south. The boy king wrote back with obvious excitement. He told Harkhuf to guard the dwarf day and night, and to make sure he did not fall into the water. Four thousand years later, you can still hear a child's delight in those lines.

A little further along is a double tomb shared by a father and son, Mekhu and Sabni. Their inscriptions tell a darker story. Mekhu died on an expedition in Nubia. His son Sabni gathered men and pack animals, travelled south, and brought his father's body home for proper burial. For an Egyptian, burial in Egyptian soil mattered deeply. Sabni wanted the world to know he had done his duty, so he recorded it in stone.

The tombs of the Middle Kingdom feel different. The finest belongs to a governor named Sarenput the Second. Step inside and your eyes need a moment to adjust. Then a corridor appears, lined with niches. In each niche stands a statue of the owner, carved from the rock and shown in the wrapped form of the god Osiris. At the far end, painted scenes still hold their colour — reds, ochres, soft greens and blues.

Another local official buried here, Pepinakht, also called Heqaib, had an unusual afterlife. After his death, people in Elephantine began to treat him as a kind of local saint. A shrine was built for him on the island, and later governors left statues and offerings there. It is a reminder that this hill was not only a cemetery. For centuries it stayed part of the town's religious life.

Archaeological work here continues. Teams of researchers still excavate on this hillside, and over the years they have found burials, coffins and objects in shafts that earlier visitors walked straight past. So the hill you see is not a finished story. Some of its chambers are open to the public, and many more are closed, sealed or still being studied.

Now turn around and face the river, because the view is half of the experience. Below you, small boats cross between the banks. To the left and right, granite rocks rise from the water like the backs of animals. Across the Nile, Aswan's corniche runs along the shore, and Elephantine Island lies in the middle of the current. The governors chose this spot so they could look forever at the town they once ruled.

Before you start down, stand still for a moment near the edge of the terrace. Listen to the wind moving over the ridge, and to the faint sound of boat engines on the water. Look at the dark doorways once more, and at the dome on the summit above them. As you walk down the sandy slope toward the river, you are following the same path the funeral processions took, only in the other direction — back toward the living town.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take a boat across the Nile from Aswan's corniche.
Climb early in the morning before the sand heats up.
Wear shoes with good grip for the sandy slope.
Carry water, there is little shade on the climb.
Bring a small torch for the darker tomb interiors.
