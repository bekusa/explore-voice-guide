The guide

The story

The ground here is soft and pale underfoot. It is a mix of dust, broken pottery, and crumbled mud brick that cracks quietly under each step. Around you, the plain spreads out toward low brown mountains. On a still day you may hear only the wind, a distant tractor, or sheep moving across the fields. It does not look like a capital city. But you are standing on the remains of one of the great royal centres of the ancient Near East.

This place is called Tal-e Malyan, named after the nearby village of Malyan, in the highlands of Fars Province, northwest of Shiraz. The word "tal" means a mound. That is exactly what you see: a wide, low rise in the land, made almost entirely by people. Over thousands of years, homes, workshops, and walls were built, collapsed, and built again. Each layer lifted the ground a little higher. In ancient texts, this city had another name. It was Anshan.

Here is the first surprise. For a long time, nobody knew where Anshan was. Scholars knew the name well from clay tablets and royal inscriptions in Mesopotamia and Elam. But the city itself was missing from the map. That changed in the early 1970s. Inscribed bricks found at this mound carried the name of Anshan. Researchers studying the inscriptions were able to link this quiet field to the lost city. A name that had floated in the records for centuries finally had a place on the ground.

To understand Anshan, it helps to know about Elam. Elam was an ancient civilisation in what is now southwestern Iran. It lived side by side with the cities of Mesopotamia, such as Ur and Babylon, and it often traded and fought with them. Elam had two great centres. One was Susa, in the lowlands to the west. The other was Anshan, here in the highlands. Elamite kings often called themselves "king of Anshan and Susa", joining the mountains and the plains in a single title.

The story of this mound goes back very far. Archaeologists working here, including a long project led by teams from the University of Pennsylvania, found layers from the late fourth millennium before Christ. From that early period came clay tablets written in a script called Proto-Elamite. It is one of the oldest writing systems in the world — and it has still not been fully deciphered. So the people who lived here were keeping written records at almost the same time as the first scribes of Mesopotamia.

At its peak, the city covered a very large area, much bigger than the mound looks at first glance. It was surrounded by a long defensive wall of mud brick. Today that wall survives only as a faint ridge in the earth. If you walk the edges and look carefully, you may see how the ground rises in a long, gentle line. That line is the ghost of a city boundary that once protected thousands of people.

Anshan was not an isolated mountain town. Mesopotamian sources describe kings who sent armies toward Anshan, and others who tried to win it through diplomacy and royal marriages. Its location mattered. The highlands of Fars connected the lowlands with routes running deeper into the Iranian plateau. Goods such as stone, metals, and timber could move along these paths. Whoever controlled Anshan held an important door between worlds.

The city's fortunes rose and fell over the centuries. There were long periods of growth, and there were times when much of the site seems to have been abandoned or reduced to a small settlement. Excavators found evidence of a later royal building here, with bricks naming an Elamite king of the later second millennium before Christ. Then, gradually, the great city faded. Its memory, however, did not.

And this is the second surprise. The name of Anshan lived on in a famous royal title. Cyrus the Great, the founder of the Persian Empire, called himself "king of Anshan". On the clay object known as the Cyrus Cylinder, he also names his father, grandfather, and great-grandfather with the same title. So when the Persian Empire began, it did not describe itself with a new name. It reached back to this old Elamite city on the plain of Fars.

Historians still discuss exactly what that title meant. Some see it as a sign that the early Persian royal family ruled the region around Anshan. Others see it as a way to borrow the prestige of an ancient kingdom. Either way, it shows how strong the name remained. The ruins of the great Persian capitals, Pasargadae and Persepolis, are not far away in the same province. Anshan is part of their story, even if it is much older and much quieter.

Today, there are no grand columns or carved staircases here. Most excavated areas have been filled in again to protect them, as is common at archaeological sites. What you see is the shape of the land itself. Look down as you walk. You may notice small pieces of pottery, some plain, some with a darker painted surface. Please leave them exactly where they are. Each fragment is part of the record that archaeologists use to read this place.

Take a moment to look at the setting. The plain is fertile, and water from the surrounding hills has fed farming here for thousands of years. The fields you see today grow on the same soil that fed the ancient city. The mountains on the horizon are the same mountains that Elamite farmers, traders, and kings looked at every morning. The light changes their colour through the day, from soft grey in the early hours to warm brown in the late afternoon.

Before you leave, stand still on the highest part of the mound and turn slowly in a full circle. Notice the rise and fall of the ground beneath you, the line of the old wall, the village nearby, the open sky. A city of kings once stood here, then disappeared from memory, then was found again by its own bricks. As you walk back across the dusty earth, listen to the crunch under your feet... and remember that the ground itself is the monument.
