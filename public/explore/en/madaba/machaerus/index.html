The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here the wind is often the loudest sound, a steady push across the bare summit. It carries dust and the dry smell of sun-warmed limestone. Below you the land falls away in long brown folds toward a pale band of haze. That haze is the Dead Sea basin, the lowest land surface on Earth. You are standing on Machaerus, a fortress hilltop above the village of Mukawir. Take a moment to catch your breath before we begin.

The climb you just made is part of the story. For an ancient army, this hill was a natural defence. Steep ravines drop away on several sides, and only narrow approaches lead to the top. The first-century historian Flavius Josephus described the site at length. He wrote that the ravines around it were so deep the eye could hardly follow them down. Look over the edge and you can judge his description for yourself.

The first fortress here was built by the Hasmoneans. They were a Jewish royal dynasty that ruled Judea in the last two centuries before Christ. The builder is usually named as Alexander Jannaeus, a Hasmonean king who fortified his eastern frontier. Machaerus faced east, toward the kingdoms of the desert. From here, signals could also pass across the Dead Sea toward other fortresses on the western side. In the first century before Christ, Roman forces captured the fortress and pulled it down.

The hill did not stay empty for long. Herod the Great, the king remembered for rebuilding the Temple in Jerusalem, raised Machaerus again. He liked palaces on remote, fortified peaks. Masada and Herodium are the best-known examples of this taste. Here he combined a military stronghold with a royal residence. There were courtyards, a bathhouse, and columned halls, all set on a summit with no natural water. That last detail matters, and we will come back to it.

Now look at the two tall columns standing on the summit. They are not survivors left standing for two thousand years. Excavators re-erected them from fragments found on the site. A Hungarian archaeological team, which has worked here since the late 2000s, set them up again. Earlier excavations in the 1970s and 1980s were led by Franciscan archaeologists. Together these teams traced the plan of the palace. The columns help you picture the height of the rooms that once stood here.

Most visitors come because of one name — John the Baptist. The Gospels tell how Herod Antipas, a son of Herod the Great, imprisoned John and later had him beheaded. The Gospels do not name the place. Josephus does. He writes that John was sent in chains to Machaerus and put to death there. Josephus gives a political reason. He says Antipas feared John's influence over the crowds might lead to a revolt.

The Gospel story adds a feast. During a banquet for Antipas, a young woman danced for the guests. She was promised any reward, and she asked for John's head. The Gospels do not give her name. Christian tradition calls her Salome, a name that comes from Josephus, who lists her among the Herodian family. Over the centuries, painters, writers, and composers turned this scene into one of the most retold episodes of the ancient world. And many visitors believe it happened on the ground under their feet.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Remember the missing water? Herod's engineers solved it with cisterns. Rain that fell on the slopes was channelled into large plastered chambers cut into the rock. On your walk up, you may have passed dark openings in the hillside. Some of these are cisterns. Others are caves. Local tradition links certain caves on the slopes with John's imprisonment. That is a tradition, not a proven fact, but it gives the hillside its own quiet weight.

The last chapter of the fortress is a siege. In the year 66, Jews rose in revolt against Rome. Jerusalem fell in the year 70, but a few strongholds held out. Machaerus was one of them. The Roman commander Lucilius Bassus marched here and surrounded the hill. His soldiers began to build a great earthen ramp against the western side. The aim was to bring siege engines close enough to break the walls.

Josephus tells how the siege ended, and the ending is surprising. A young defender named Eleazar was captured outside the walls. The Romans stripped him and prepared to crucify him in full view of the fortress. His family and friends inside could not bear it. They negotiated a surrender in exchange for his life. The ramp was never needed. This may be the only time in the story of Machaerus that a single life decided the fate of the whole hill.

Now turn toward the west and look down. The long sloping ridge of earth below the summit is the remains of that Roman ramp. It is still visible nearly two thousand years later. Look further out across the plateau and you may make out low lines of stone. These are traces of Roman camps and the siege wall that circled the hill. After the surrender, the Romans destroyed the fortress. What you see today is mostly foundations, floors, and fallen stone.

When you return to the village of Mukawir, the story changes from stone to people. The village is home to Bedouin families, many of whom keep strong ties to the land. Mukawir is also known for a weaving project run by local women. They make rugs on traditional looms in deep reds, oranges, and blacks. If you have time, stop and look at their work. It connects this ancient hill to the living community around it today.

Before you go, stand still once more and face west. On a clear day the far shore of the Dead Sea rises as a pale wall of hills. Somewhere over there, out of sight, are Jerusalem and Herod's other fortresses. Feel the wind again, and the hard stone under your shoes. Kings ate and plotted here. A prophet died here, if Josephus is right. A family once gave up a fortress for a son. Now there is only the wind, the light, and you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the steep, stony path up.
Bring water and a sun hat; shade is almost absent.
Visit early morning or late afternoon for softer heat.
Hold onto hats and phones; summit winds can be strong.
Leave time to visit the weaving workshops in Mukawir village.
