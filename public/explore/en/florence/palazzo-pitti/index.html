The story

You cross the river, and the street opens into a wide square. In front of you stands a long wall of rough golden stone. It stretches far to the left and far to the right. The blocks are huge and rugged, left rough on purpose. Carriages once rolled across this open ground toward the doors. This is the Pitti Palace, the largest palace in Florence, and it fills almost your whole view.

The story begins with a banker named Luca Pitti. Around the middle of the fifteenth century, he wanted a house grander than any other in the city. He was a rival of the powerful Medici family. The palace was meant to prove that he could match them, stone for stone. But the project was vast, and in time his fortune ran out before the work was done.

In 1549, the palace passed to the very family Luca Pitti had hoped to outshine. Eleonora di Toledo, the wife of Duke Cosimo de' Medici, bought it. The Medici then made it their main home. The house built to rival them became the center of their court. History has a quiet way of turning such stories around.

For the next three centuries, this palace was the seat of power in Florence. The Medici lived here, and after them the ruling family from Austria, and later still the kings of a newly united Italy. Each family added rooms, paintings, and treasures of its own. The walls slowly grew heavy with the wealth of one age after another.

Look closely at the front of the building. The stone is left rough and rugged, not smooth and polished. Run your eyes along the long rows of heavy blocks. This finish was a careful choice, not an accident. It made the palace look strong, old, and rooted to the earth. Strength itself became the style. The whole front seems to grow up out of the ground like a low stone cliff.

Step inside, and the mood changes completely. The rough outer stone gives way to rich and golden rooms. The greatest of these is the Palatine Gallery. Here the paintings hang close together, frame against frame, covering the walls from low down to high above. This was how rulers once chose to show their art, in a thick and dazzling crowd.

On these walls hang works by some of the greatest painters who ever lived. There are many paintings by Raphael, soft and calm and full of feeling. There are deep, warm portraits by Titian. Some of the painted faces seem to follow you with their eyes. The collection was gathered by the ruling families across many years. To walk slowly through these rooms is to walk through their own personal taste.

Beyond the gallery lie the royal apartments. These were the private rooms of the families who lived here. The ceilings are painted with scenes and figures. The walls are hung with colored silk. Thrones, mirrors, and heavy furniture still fill the spaces. You can almost sense the slow and careful life of a court moving softly through these rooms.

The palace holds far more than paintings. One museum here shows the treasure of the grand dukes, full of carved stone, gold, and jewels. Another shows clothing and fashion across the centuries. Another shows fine porcelain. You could spend a full day here and still not see all of it. The single building works like many museums stacked together, each one with its own quiet world inside.

There is one more secret worth knowing. A long covered passage, built in 1565, connects this palace to the government building across the river. The rulers could walk above the streets, unseen and safe, from their home to their offices. Power here moved quietly, hidden away over the heads of the ordinary people passing below.

Behind the palace rises a great garden on the hill. From the upper windows, the family could look out over their own private green world. Palace and garden were planned together as one. Inside there was art and gold. Outside there were hedges, fountains, and statues. The line between the two almost disappears. Together they formed a complete and closed stage built for rule.

Step back out into the square and turn to face the palace once more. Think of the banker who began it and then lost it. Think of the family who finished it and ruled from it for so long. Think of the kings who came last of all. So much ambition is pressed into this golden stone. Stand a moment, and let the long story settle before you go.

Key facts

Built for banker Luca Pitti in the fifteenth century
The Medici bought the palace in 1549
Largest palace in Florence, on the Arno's south bank
The Vasari Corridor connected it across the river in 1565
Palatine Gallery holds many works by Raphael and Titian

What to look for

The rough, rugged golden stone of the facade
Paintings hung frame against frame, floor to ceiling
Painted ceilings above the private royal apartments
Silk-hung walls and heavy gilded furniture inside

Practical tips

Buy a combined ticket that includes the Boboli Gardens
Allow half a day for all the museums
Visit the royal apartments before the main gallery
Expect many rooms; pace yourself and rest often
