The guide

The story

You stand in a long stone courtyard. On both sides, tall walls rise toward a narrow strip of sky. The stone is the color of honey and ash. Footsteps echo on the pavement around you. Street painters set up their easels along the columns. At the far end, between two pillars, you can see a bright line of water. That is the Arno river. And this long courtyard is the entrance to the Uffizi Gallery, one of the oldest art museums in the world.

The name Uffizi means offices. That is exactly what this building was meant to be. In 1560, Cosimo the First de' Medici, the ruler of Florence, wanted one place to hold the city's law courts and government offices. Until then they were scattered across the town. He asked the painter and architect Giorgio Vasari to bring them all together under one roof. Vasari was already the most trusted artist at the Medici court.

Vasari built two long wings, joined at one end by a short third wing near the river. Seen from above, the shape is like a giant letter U. The open side faces the Arno. Work began in 1560 and went slowly. Vasari died in 1574, before it was done. Other architects, including Bernardo Buontalenti, finished the building around 1581. By then the simple offices had begun to fill with art.

The Medici family ruled Florence for generations. They began as bankers and became dukes. They were among the richest people in Europe, and they spent their money on beauty. They paid artists, bought paintings, and collected ancient statues. Over time, the top floor of the offices became a private gallery, where the family could walk among their treasures and show them to important guests.

Francesco the First, the son of Cosimo, made this gallery real. He turned the upper rooms into a place to display paintings, sculptures, jewels, and curiosities from around the world. At the heart of it he built a special room called the Tribuna. It has eight sides and a domed ceiling lined with shell. The walls are covered in deep red cloth. Here he kept the rarest and finest pieces of all.

Today the gallery holds some of the most famous paintings ever made. The most loved are by Sandro Botticelli. In one bright room you will find The Birth of Venus. The goddess stands on a seashell, nude, her long hair moving in the wind. Beside it hangs Primavera, a garden full of figures from old myths. The colors are soft, and the figures seem to float just above the ground.

There is far more to see. Leonardo da Vinci is here. One early work shows an angel kneeling before Mary, telling her she will bear a child. Michelangelo painted a round picture of the Holy Family in sharp, glowing colors. Raphael, Titian, and Caravaggio each have rooms of their own. You could spend a whole day here and still not see everything.

The collection also tells the story of how painting itself changed. In the first rooms you see flat, golden pictures from the Middle Ages. The figures are stiff and far away, like signs more than people. Then artists such as Giotto began to give their figures weight, shadow, and feeling. As you walk forward through the building, you walk forward through time. You watch art slowly learn to look real.

How did all this stay together for so long? The answer is one woman. Anna Maria Luisa de' Medici was the last of the family line. She had no children. When she died, she gave the entire Medici collection to the city of Florence. The year was 1743. She made one firm rule. Nothing could ever be sold or carried away. The art had to remain in the city forever.

Because of her gift, almost everything you see is still in the place it was made for. This is rare. Many great collections were broken up and scattered across the world. Hers was not. Travelers now come from every country to these same rooms. The lines outside can be long and slow. But inside, the crowds move quietly, room by room, painting by painting.

Look for one more secret before you leave. From inside the gallery, a covered passage runs out of the building. It crosses above the street, then over the river, riding on top of an old bridge called the Ponte Vecchio. Vasari built this passage in 1565. It let the Medici walk from their offices to their home, the Palazzo Pitti, without ever stepping into the public street. It is called the Vasari Corridor.

Find a place by one of the tall windows that face the river. The glass is old and a little wavy. Below you, the Arno moves slowly under the bridges. The water catches the light and carries it along. Think for a moment about what surrounds you. The same light that fell on these paintings five hundred years ago is falling on them now. The clerks and judges who once worked here are long gone. Their offices became a home for beauty. And the beauty remains, waiting quietly for the next person to look up.
