The guide

The story

Stand in the piazza for a moment and feel the ground under your shoes. The paving is broad and flat, wide open to the sky, with tall houses leaning in along the edges. Pigeons lift and settle. Somewhere a bicycle bell rings over the low murmur of voices. At the far end, a white and green marble front rises like a striped cliff. This is the Basilica of Santa Croce, the Church of the Holy Cross, and behind that face lies one of the most crowded rooms of memory in Italy.

Begin with the people who built it. Santa Croce is a Franciscan church. The Franciscans are the religious order that follows Saint Francis of Assisi, who preached poverty and simplicity in the early thirteenth century. His followers settled here when this was marshy ground outside the city walls, close to the river. Work on the present building started in the late thirteenth century. Tradition gives the design to Arnolfo di Cambio, the same master often linked to Florence's cathedral. It is often described as the largest Franciscan church in the world.

Now look again at that façade. It seems medieval, but it is not. For centuries the front of the church was rough, unfinished stone. The marble skin you see now was added in the mid nineteenth century, in a Gothic revival style. That means it copies the pointed arches and patterns of the Middle Ages. Look high up near the top, and you may spot a six-pointed star. It is often said to be a signature of the architect, Niccolò Matas, who was Jewish. It is a quiet detail most people walk past.

To your left, before you enter, stands a tall white statue of a stern, hooded man. That is Dante Alighieri, the poet of the Divine Comedy, and Florence's most famous son. The statue went up in the nineteenth century, when Italy was becoming one nation. Dante was a symbol of a shared language and identity. Here is the first surprise. Dante is not buried in Florence at all. The city exiled him in his lifetime, and he died in Ravenna, which has kept his remains ever since.

Step inside. The first thing most people notice is the space. The nave, the long central hall, stretches far ahead. Slender columns divide it into aisles. Look up, and the roof is not a stone vault but an open wooden ceiling, painted and beamed. The light is soft and falls in long shafts from tall windows. Your footsteps sound different here. The air is cool and carries a faint smell of old stone, wax, and wood.

Now look down. The floor is a patchwork of flat stone slabs, many carved with figures, coats of arms, and inscriptions. These are tombs. For centuries, Florentine families paid to be buried inside the church, as close as possible to the altar and to prayer. Generations of feet have worn many of the faces smooth. Some figures are now only faint shadows of a nose or a folded hand. Step gently, and try to read one before you move on.

Along the walls, the tombs grow grand. On the right side, near the entrance, is the tomb of Michelangelo. It was designed by Giorgio Vasari, a painter and the first great biographer of Italian artists. Three mourning women sit below the bust: Painting, Sculpture, and Architecture. Michelangelo died in Rome, but his body was brought home to Florence. Further along you will find a memorial to Dante, an empty tomb, a cenotaph, waiting for a poet who never came back.

Keep walking and the names keep coming. Niccolò Machiavelli, the political thinker whose name became a word for cunning, rests here. So does the composer Gioachino Rossini, who wrote The Barber of Seville. Across the nave lies Galileo Galilei, the astronomer who was condemned by the Church for supporting the idea that the Earth moves around the Sun. For years he was denied a grand tomb. Only in the eighteenth century was his body moved to the monument you see today. A poet later called this church a temple of Italian glories.

Among all this marble, look for a quieter work. On the right wall is a carved Annunciation by Donatello, the great early Renaissance sculptor. The Annunciation is the moment the angel tells Mary she will bear a child. The stone is gilded, and the two figures seem to hesitate. Mary turns away, almost startled. Nearby, the tomb of the scholar Leonardo Bruni, carved by Bernardo Rossellino, became a model for Renaissance tombs across Italy. He lies on his bier, with a book resting on his chest.

Now walk toward the far end of the church, to the chapels around the main altar. Two of them hold paintings by Giotto, the artist who helped bring weight and human feeling back into Western painting in the early fourteenth century. In one chapel, scenes from the life of Saint Francis fill the walls. The colours have faded and parts are lost. Yet watch the faces at the deathbed of the saint. The grief in those gestures is plain, direct, and very human.

Here is a story worth knowing. In 1966, the Arno river burst its banks and flooded this whole neighbourhood. Santa Croce sits low, and the water rose high inside the church and its buildings. A great painted crucifix by Cimabue, Giotto's teacher, was badly damaged. Volunteers from around the world came to help save art and books from the mud. People called them the angels of the mud. Look for marks around the district that show how high the water climbed.

Outside the main church, through the cloister, stands the Pazzi Chapel. It is linked to Filippo Brunelleschi, the architect of the cathedral dome. Inside, everything is clarity and calm. Grey stone lines trace out squares, circles, and arches against white walls. After the crowded church, it feels like a deep breath. The museum spaces nearby hold the restored Cimabue crucifix. Many visitors rush past the cloisters, but this is where the noise of the city fades away completely.

One more curiosity. The French writer Stendhal visited Santa Croce in the early nineteenth century. He later wrote that the beauty here left him dizzy, with a racing heart. Much later, an Italian psychiatrist used his name for a condition seen in visitors overwhelmed by art. People still call it Stendhal syndrome. Outside, the piazza has its own loud tradition. Each summer it hosts calcio storico, a rough historic game of football played in costume, with sand spread across the square.

As you leave, pause again on the steps and look back across the piazza. Notice the long, plain side walls of the church, and the bell tower rising behind. Listen to the voices, the scooters, the café spoons on saucers. Inside, poets, scientists, and artists lie under one roof. Out here, the city carries on around them. Take a last look at the striped marble front, then walk slowly into the narrow streets beyond.

Key facts

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly; shoulders and knees should be covered inside.
Walk through the cloister to reach the quiet Pazzi Chapel.
Save time for Giotto's chapels near the main altar.
Visit the museum to see the restored Cimabue crucifix.
Return to the piazza at dusk for softer light on marble.
