The guide

The story

You step out of a narrow shaded street and into a wide open square. The sky opens above you, and the noise of the city spreads out. In front of you stands a tall stone building with a tower that climbs into the air. Statues stand all around the open space, pale against the gray stone. This is the Piazza della Signoria, the main square of Florence, and for many people the true heart of the city.

For more than seven hundred years, this square has been the center of power in Florence. When the city had to make a decision, the people gathered here. The square has seen festivals and parades. It has also seen riots, speeches, and public executions. Think of it as an open-air stage, where the long story of Florence has been acted out in front of the crowd.

The large building with the tower is the Palazzo Vecchio. The name means the Old Palace. It was built between 1299 and 1314 to house the city government, and it still serves as the town hall today. The tower rises about ninety-four meters into the sky. From down in the square, you have to lean your head all the way back to see the bell at the top.

At the doorway of the palace stands a famous figure. He is a tall marble man, nude, calm, with a sling resting over one shoulder. This is David, carved by Michelangelo. The statue standing here is a copy. The real one stood on this exact spot from 1504 until 1873. Then it was moved indoors, into a museum, to protect it from the weather. Years later, this faithful copy was set in its place.

To one side of the square stands an open stone porch with high, rounded arches. This is the Loggia dei Lanzi, built in the late fourteenth century. Long ago, city officials stood beneath it during ceremonies, sheltered from sun and rain. Today it works as an open-air museum. You can walk straight in, free of charge, and stand close enough to touch the bases of the statues.

One bronze figure draws every eye. A young man stands holding up the severed head of a monster, while her body lies twisted at his feet. This is Perseus with the head of Medusa, cast by Benvenuto Cellini in the sixteenth century. The dark metal catches the light. Walk slowly around it. Even the back of the figure is finished with great care.

Beside it rises a swirl of white marble, three figures turning upward in a spiral. This is the Rape of the Sabine Women, by the sculptor Giambologna. The whole group was carved from a single block of stone. The bodies wind around one another, so the work looks completely different from each side. There is no single front. You must circle it to understand it.

Out in the open square stands a great white fountain. At its center looms the sea god Neptune, huge and pale, surrounded by bronze figures and horses. The sculptor was Bartolomeo Ammannati. The people of Florence were not impressed when it first appeared. They thought it had wasted a fine block of marble. They nicknamed the big figure Il Biancone, which means, more or less, the big white one. The joke has lasted for centuries.

Nearby, a bronze man rides a bronze horse. This is Cosimo the First de' Medici, the duke who once ruled the city and brought the surrounding region under his control. The statue was made by Giambologna in 1594. Horse and rider are caught in mid-motion, the horse lifting one hoof, as if they have paused for only a single moment before moving on.

But the square holds darker memories too. Look down. Set into the pavement near the fountain is a round plaque. It marks a place of death. Here, in 1498, a preacher named Girolamo Savonarola was hanged and then burned, along with two of his followers. For a few years he had ruled Florence through strict religious fear. The same crowds that once obeyed him gathered to watch his end.

Stand in the middle of the square and turn around slowly. Gods and kings, heroes and monsters surround you, in white marble and dark bronze. Few open squares anywhere in the world hold so much great sculpture out under the sky. And nearly all of it is free to see, by day or by night, for anyone who simply wanders in.

As the day fades, lamps switch on across the piazza. The statues throw long shadows over the pale stone. Street musicians begin to play near the loggia. People sit on the steps, sharing food and talking quietly, in no hurry to leave. The old seat of power has become a meeting place. Where leaders once decided the fate of the city, travelers now rest their feet and watch the evening come. The stones beneath you have seen all of it, the glory and the cruelty alike. They keep their memories, and they say nothing at all.
