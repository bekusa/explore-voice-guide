The guide

The story

Cool air meets you first. Just past the entrance, the noise of the market streets outside falls away, and the floor under your feet turns to smooth, worn stone. This is a crypt. You are standing beneath the family that ran Florence for much of three centuries. Welcome to the Medici Chapels.

The chapels belong to the Basilica of San Lorenzo, the Medici family's parish church. They lived just a few streets away, and for generations they paid for this church, prayed in it, and were buried in it. The chapels sit at the back of the basilica, with their own entrance on a small square. Inside, there are really two worlds. One is loud with colour and power. The other is quiet, grey, and white. Both were made for the dead.

This low vaulted crypt holds the remains of many members of the dynasty, including the grand dukes. These were the rulers of Tuscany after Florence stopped being a republic. Look at the floor slabs and the plaques along the walls. Names repeat again and again... Cosimo, Ferdinando, Francesco. It is a family tree written in stone, and you are walking across it.

Now climb the stairs into the Chapel of the Princes. Look up — slowly. The space is enormous and eight-sided, and a great dome crowns it. Work began in the early 1600s. The grand dukes wanted a mausoleum that would show the world how rich and permanent their line had become. Every wall is covered in polished stone, in deep greens, blacks, reds, and golden browns.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. Almost none of this colour is paint. It is pietra dura, a Florentine craft of cutting hard and semi-precious stones and fitting them together like a puzzle, with no visible gaps. Florence had a workshop dedicated to this technique, set up by the Medici themselves. Lean close to the lower walls. You will see coats of arms of Tuscan towns, each one built from tiny shaped pieces of stone.

The ambition of this room was almost too large. The project ran for generations and was never fully completed in its original form. Look at the large niches above the tombs. The plan called for bronze statues of the grand dukes in all of them, but only two were ever installed. The empty niches tell a quiet story. Even a dynasty this wealthy could not finish everything it started.

Now walk through the passage into the New Sacristy, and notice how your eyes adjust. The colour disappears. In its place you see grey stone and white marble, and soft light falling from above. The grey stone is pietra serena, a local sandstone used all over Florence. This room is the work of Michelangelo. He designed both the architecture and the sculpture, beginning around 1520, for two Medici cousins who both later became popes.

It is called the New Sacristy because an older one already existed on the other side of the church. That Old Sacristy was designed by Brunelleschi a century earlier. Michelangelo was answering him. He kept the same basic idea, a square room under a dome, but he made the walls feel tense and crowded. Look at the windows and doorways. Some seem too tall, too narrow, or squeezed into their frames. That unease is deliberate.

Two great wall tombs face each other. One honours Lorenzo, Duke of Urbino, and the other Giuliano, Duke of Nemours. These were not the famous Lorenzo and Giuliano of the fifteenth century, but younger relatives. Both men sit in niches above their sarcophagi, dressed like Roman commanders. Lorenzo rests his chin on his hand, deep in thought. Florentines have long called him the Thinker. Giuliano sits upright, holding a baton.

According to a well-known story, someone complained that the faces did not look like the real dukes. Michelangelo is said to have answered that in a thousand years, no one would know or care. Whether he said it or not, the point stands. These are not portraits. They are ideas about rule, about action and reflection, carved in marble for whoever stands here.

Now look down at the reclining figures on each tomb. Below Giuliano lie Night and Day. Below Lorenzo lie Dawn and Dusk. They represent passing time, which carries every life toward death. Several are left rough, with chisel marks still clear on the faces and limbs. Michelangelo left Florence for Rome in 1534 and never returned to finish the room. Night was famous even in his lifetime. A poet praised her as so alive she might wake. Michelangelo replied with his own short verse. In it, Night says she would rather sleep and stay as stone while shame and harm continue in the world.

On the entrance wall, a simpler tomb holds two more famous men. Lorenzo the Magnificent and his brother Giuliano lie here. Giuliano was murdered in the cathedral in 1478, during a plot against the family. Above them sits Michelangelo's Madonna and Child, the child twisting toward his mother. Here is the second surprise. In 1975, beneath this sacristy, a small hidden room was found with charcoal drawings on its walls. Many scholars connect them to Michelangelo, perhaps from a time when he may have been hiding.

Before you leave, stand in the centre of the room for a moment. Notice the silence, and the slow movement of light on the white marble. Then remember the colourful hall behind you, full of polished stone and empty niches. Two ideas of death sit side by side here — one about display, one about time. As you step back into the street, listen. The market vendors and the scooters will return. The Medici are still beneath your feet.

Before you go

Practical tips

Use the separate entrance behind San Lorenzo, not the basilica door.
Save time and energy for the quieter New Sacristy.
Book ahead if you hope to see the hidden drawings room.
Stand in the sacristy centre to see both tombs together.
Visit the Old Sacristy inside the basilica for comparison.
