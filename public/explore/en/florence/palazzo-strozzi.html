The guide

The story

Put your palm flat against the wall. The stone is cool, rough, and swollen outward in heavy, rounded blocks, each one bulging like a loaf of bread. This is pietraforte, the brown-grey sandstone of Florence, cut and shaped by hand more than five centuries ago. Above you, the wall rises in three great storeys. It feels less like a house and more like a fortress. You are standing beside Palazzo Strozzi, one of the largest private palaces built in Renaissance Florence.

The man who began it was Filippo Strozzi, a banker. His story starts with a defeat. In 1434, Cosimo de' Medici took control of Florence, and the Strozzi family ended up on the losing side. They were exiled. Filippo spent long years away, building his fortune through trade and banking abroad, mostly in Naples. He finally returned to Florence in 1466, rich, patient, and determined to put his family's name back on the map of the city.

And he chose stone to do it. Construction began in 1489. Filippo had been buying up the houses on this plot for years, one by one, until he held enough land to clear an entire block. The scale was a statement. In a city where the Medici held quiet but firm power, a private family building something this large was a bold, almost provocative act. The palace said, very clearly, that the Strozzi were back.

According to one popular account, Filippo played a careful game with Lorenzo de' Medici, known as Lorenzo the Magnificent. The story goes that Filippo pretended to want only a modest house — and let Lorenzo persuade him to build something grander, for the honour of the city. That way, the ambition looked like Medici approval rather than rivalry. It may be more legend than record. But it fits the man, and it fits the city.

Filippo never saw his palace finished. He died in 1491, only about two years after the first stones were laid. His will asked his heirs to complete the work, and they did — slowly. Building went on for decades. The design is usually credited to Benedetto da Maiano, with later work by Simone del Pollaiolo, a master builder better known by his nickname, Il Cronaca, which roughly means the chronicler.

Now look up, to the very top of the walls. That deep, overhanging stone ledge is the cornice, the work of Il Cronaca. It projects far out over the street, casting a hard shadow line across the façade. Here is a detail many visitors miss — the cornice was never completed. Walk around the building and you can see where it simply stops. The money, the will, or the moment ran out, and the palace has worn its unfinished crown ever since.

Lower your eyes to the level of the windows and the street. The palace carries some remarkable ironwork. There are lantern holders at the corners, torch brackets, and heavy rings set into the stone, once used to tether horses. This metalwork is attributed to a smith called Niccolò Grosso. Florentines gave him the nickname Caparra, which means deposit — because, so the story goes, he refused to start any job until he was paid in advance.

Along the base of the walls, notice the long stone bench. Many Florentine palaces were built with one. It is a small, generous gesture in a building otherwise designed to keep the world out. Passers-by could sit, wait, and rest in the shade of the great family. Today it still does that job. You will often see students, tired walkers, and people checking their phones, sitting exactly where Florentines sat five hundred years ago.

Now step through the main doorway and into the courtyard. The change is immediate. The noise of the street drops away. The fortress outside gives way to calm, ordered arches on slender columns, with light falling from the open sky above. This is the Renaissance idea in miniature — a hard, defensive shell on the outside, and inside, harmony, proportion, and air. Stand in the centre and turn slowly to feel the symmetry around you.

The Strozzi family kept the palace for centuries — until the twentieth century, when it passed into public hands. Its rooms have since held cultural institutions, including a historic library and reading society, and a research institute devoted to the Renaissance. The building that began as one family's bid for status has become a shared space for the whole city, and for anyone who walks in from the street.

Today the palace is best known for its exhibitions. A cultural foundation runs a programme here that moves between old masters and contemporary artists. That mix gives the place a lively, unexpected energy. Over recent years, artists have used the courtyard and even the stone façade itself for large installations — sometimes playful, sometimes unsettling. Here is the second surprise. A building made to project power and permanence now changes its face from season to season.

It is worth thinking about what that means. Filippo Strozzi built to be remembered. He wanted the stone to outlast him, and it has. But what fills these rooms now would probably astonish him. Modern art, video, sound, crowds of visitors from every corner of the world. The walls stay the same. The life inside them keeps changing. In a way, that is exactly how Florence itself survives — by keeping its old stone and letting each generation use it differently.

Before you leave, walk out and stand back across the street. Take in the whole block at once. Notice how the rough blocks grow slightly smoother as the walls rise. Notice the arched windows, the iron rings, the unfinished line of the cornice against the sky. Listen to the footsteps on the paving and the voices drifting from the shops nearby. Then lay your hand on the stone once more — cool, rough, and still standing, just as Filippo Strozzi hoped it would.
