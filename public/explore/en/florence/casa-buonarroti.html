The guide

The story

Listen to Via Ghibellina for a moment. Scooters pass over the paving stones. Footsteps echo between the tall stone façades. The plaster walls are the colour of weak tea, and the shutters are the same quiet green you see all over Florence. Before you stands a house that looks like a hundred others in this neighbourhood. Yet this one carries a name that every visitor to the city already knows. This is Casa Buonarroti, the house of the family of Michelangelo.

The first surprise is that Michelangelo hardly lived here. In 1508 he bought a group of properties on this street. That was an investment, the kind of careful move a successful artist made to protect his family's future. He did spend some time in the area. But much of his working life passed in Rome, carving tombs and painting ceilings for popes. The house you see today belongs more to his descendants than to the man himself.

So who shaped it? The key figure is his grand-nephew, also named Michelangelo, usually called Michelangelo Buonarroti the Younger. He was a writer and a man of letters, active in the early seventeenth century. He decided to turn the family home into a tribute to his famous great-uncle. He hired painters of his own generation and gave them one subject: the life and glory of Michelangelo. In a sense, this building is one of the earliest museums in Europe dedicated to a single artist.

Inside, those rooms survive. The walls and ceilings are covered with painted scenes and allegories. You will see Michelangelo presented to princes, honoured by poets, and surrounded by symbolic figures of virtue. It is flattering, theatrical, and very much of its time. Think of it as a family album painted in oil and fresco. It tells you as much about how Florence remembered its hero as it does about the hero himself.

Now to the treasures most people come for. Two small marble reliefs hang here, and they are among the earliest works by Michelangelo that we know. He carved them as a teenager, in the early 1490s, when he was living in the circle of Lorenzo de' Medici. They are quiet in size. They are not quiet in ambition. Standing before them, you are looking at the moment a young stonecutter became an artist.

The first is the Madonna of the Stairs. Mary sits in profile, holding the child against her body. Behind her rises a flight of steps, where small figures seem to play. The carving is extremely shallow, in places almost flat. This technique comes from Donatello, the great Florentine sculptor of the previous century. The young Michelangelo was studying him closely. Look at how much depth he creates with only a few millimetres of marble.

The second relief is the Battle of the Centaurs. It shows a crowd of struggling bodies, half human and half horse, twisting and pushing against one another. The surface is crowded, and parts of it are left rough and unfinished. Here is the second surprise. Many historians see this unfinished quality as a first sign of a habit that followed Michelangelo all his life. He often left marble partly raw, as if the figures were still climbing out of the stone.

The house also keeps something that never became a building. There is a large wooden model for a façade of the Church of San Lorenzo, the Medici family church across the city. Michelangelo designed a grand marble front for it. The project was cancelled, and the church still stands with a bare, rough brick face today. This model is the closest anyone can come to seeing what he imagined. It is a reminder that even great artists lost commissions.

Look out, too, for a model of a reclining river god. It was made as a study for the Medici tombs, which Michelangelo designed for the New Sacristy at San Lorenzo. The figure is not carved in marble. It was built from lighter, more fragile materials and shaped by hand. Models like this were working tools, meant to be thrown away. That one survived at all is remarkable. You can almost see the pressure of his fingers in the form.

Then there are the drawings. The family collected many sheets by Michelangelo, and the museum holds an important group of them. Because paper and ink fade in light, the drawings cannot stay on the walls for long. They are shown in rotation, a few at a time. So what you see today may be different from what a friend saw last year. Each sheet shows his mind at work: a muscle studied again and again, an architectural idea sketched and then corrected.

How did this private house become public? The family line ended in the nineteenth century. The last Buonarroti left the property and its collections to the city of Florence. Not long after, it opened as a museum. The house had moved from family pride to civic memory. Today, the city cares for it, and the rooms still keep the layout and decoration of the family home.

When you leave, turn your head toward the end of the street. You are only a few minutes' walk from the Basilica of Santa Croce. Michelangelo died in Rome in 1564, but his body was brought back to Florence and buried in that church. His tomb stands inside, near the entrance. So the house and the grave sit in the same neighbourhood, almost within calling distance of each other. The family that bought these properties kept him close in the end.

Stand for one more moment on the pavement. Look at the plain stone doorway, the iron window grilles, the ordinary rhythm of the street. Nothing here announces genius. That may be the best lesson of the house. The boy who carved those reliefs came from streets just like this one. As you walk away toward Santa Croce, listen again to the footsteps on the stone... and imagine one young apprentice walking the same way, five hundred years ago.
