The guide

The story

Listen for the river first. The Arno moves slowly below the Ponte Vecchio, green and brown, and its sound rises between the shops. Above the goldsmiths' windows, a long line of small square openings runs across the bridge. Most people walking here never look up. But behind those windows is a passage. For centuries, only a very small number of people were allowed to walk through it.

This is the Vasari Corridor. It is an enclosed walkway, roughly a kilometre long. It links the Palazzo Vecchio, the old seat of government, to the Palazzo Pitti on the far side of the river. On its way, it passes through the Uffizi, crosses the Ponte Vecchio, and threads between the houses of the Oltrarno. The Oltrarno is the neighbourhood whose name simply means the district beyond the Arno.

The corridor was built in 1565 for Cosimo the First de' Medici, Duke of Florence. The occasion was a wedding. His son Francesco was marrying Joanna of Austria, a Habsburg princess. Cosimo wanted something grand to show the new family. He also wanted something practical. Records say the work was finished in about five months. That is a remarkable speed for a structure this long, cut through a crowded medieval city.

The architect was Giorgio Vasari. Today many people remember him as a writer. He wrote the Lives of the Artists, a collection of biographies of Italian painters, sculptors and architects. Many of the stories we still tell about Leonardo, Michelangelo and Raphael come from his pages. Vasari was also the court architect who designed the Uffizi offices. This corridor, which carries his name, extends that same building out over the water.

Why did a duke need a private passage? The answer is security, and also status. Cosimo had moved his family into the Palazzo Pitti. His government stayed in the Palazzo Vecchio. Between the two lay narrow streets full of crowds, and Florence had a long memory of political violence. The Medici themselves had been driven out of the city more than once. With the corridor, the duke could cross the city without touching the street. He could watch his people, and they would not see him.

Now look back at the bridge itself. When the corridor was built, butchers and tanners worked in the shops of the Ponte Vecchio. They threw scraps into the river, and the smell rose. In 1593, Cosimo's son Ferdinando the First expelled them. He gave the shops to goldsmiths and jewellers instead. That is why the bridge glitters today. The gold you see in the windows is, in a sense, the result of a duke who disliked the smell under his feet.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. At the southern end of the bridge, find the old stone tower on the corner. This is the Mannelli Tower. The Mannelli family owned it, and they refused to let the Medici demolish it. So the corridor does not pass through the tower. It goes around it, carried on heavy stone brackets. Even the most powerful family in Florence had to step around a stubborn neighbour.

Further on, the corridor passes the church of Santa Felicita. Inside the church, high on the wall, there is a balcony with a window. It connects directly to the corridor. From there, the Medici could attend mass without going down among the congregation. They could hear the service and see the altar, and the people below could see only a dark window. It is a small architectural detail, but it shows exactly how the family wished to live.

The twentieth century brought violence close to these walls. In 1944, as German forces retreated from Florence, they blew up the city's bridges over the Arno. The Ponte Vecchio was the only one left standing. The buildings at both ends of it were destroyed instead, to block the approaches. The corridor was damaged, and it was later repaired. Many of the buildings you see at the ends of the bridge are postwar reconstructions.

Another scar is more recent. In 1993, a car bomb exploded in Via dei Georgofili, a small street beside the Uffizi. The attack was carried out by the Sicilian Mafia. Five people were killed. The gallery and the corridor were damaged, and several works of art were lost or harmed. Florentines still remember the night. A memorial stands in that street, close to where the blast happened.

For many decades, the corridor held a famous collection of self-portraits by artists. Painters from many centuries looked out from its walls. Then the corridor closed for a long restoration. When it reopened to visitors in late 2024, the walls had been left largely bare. The self-portraits were moved elsewhere in the Uffizi. The aim was to let people see the passage itself, its windows, and its views of the city. Visits require a reservation, and places are limited.

If you walk inside, the windows become the story. Over the bridge, a few larger openings give wide views up and down the Arno. These larger windows were added in the 1930s, for a state visit by Hitler to Mussolini's Italy. The rest are small, and they frame narrow slices of rooftops, river and hills. The walk ends near the Boboli Gardens, behind the Palazzo Pitti, close to the grotto known as the Buontalenti Grotto.

So stand still for a moment, here on the bridge. Look up again at that row of small windows above the shops. Behind them, a duke once crossed his city in silence, above the noise of trade. Now the river still moves below, and the goldsmiths still open their shutters each morning. As you walk on, watch the line of the corridor. You can follow it with your eyes, over the rooftops, all the way to the hill.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a reservation in advance because places are limited.
Walk the Ponte Vecchio early morning before the crowds arrive.
Visit Santa Felicita church to see the Medici balcony.
Combine your visit with the Boboli Gardens at the exit.
Cross to the riverbank to see the corridor's full line.
