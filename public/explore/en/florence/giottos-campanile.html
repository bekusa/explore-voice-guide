The guide

The story

Stand close to the base of the tower and lay your palm against the stone. It is cool, smooth, and polished by centuries of hands. Now look at the colours. White, green, and a soft rose-red are set into geometric panels. Around you, the Piazza del Duomo fills with footsteps, bicycle bells, and a dozen languages. Above you, a slender square tower rises into the Florentine sky. This is Giotto's Campanile, the bell tower of Florence Cathedral, and it is one of the most carefully dressed buildings in Italy.

Begin with the name. Giotto di Bondone was the most celebrated painter of his age. His frescoes changed how Europeans pictured the human body, with weight, emotion, and real space. In 1334, the city appointed him master of works for the cathedral. That was a surprising choice. Giotto was a painter, not a trained builder. But Florence wanted a famous name, and it wanted beauty. Giotto took the job and began work on the tower that same year.

Here is the first surprise. Giotto barely saw his tower rise. He died in 1337, only a few years after work began. By then, only the lowest level was finished. That is the part closest to your eyes right now. So the tower carries his name, but most of its height came from other hands. It is a little like a book named after an author who wrote only the first chapter.

After Giotto died, the sculptor Andrea Pisano took over. He was already known for the bronze doors of the Baptistery, just across the square. Pisano continued the tower upward and added the level of niches for statues. Then work passed to the architect Francesco Talenti. Talenti completed the upper storeys, and the tower was finished around the middle of the fourteenth century. Three masters, one design... and somehow the result feels like a single voice.

Now look closely at the marble. The white stone comes from Carrara, the famous quarries in the mountains of Tuscany. The green comes from the area around Prato, a town near Florence. The red comes from southern Tuscany. These same three colours cover the cathedral and the Baptistery. Together, the three buildings form one family. The colours were not only decoration. They told visitors that this was one sacred complex, built by one proud city.

Lower your eyes to the first level, just above the ground. You will see a band of six-sided relief panels. Each one shows a scene carved in stone. Together they tell the story of human life and work. There is the creation of Adam and Eve. There are farmers, weavers, builders, musicians, and scholars. This was a medieval idea of progress — humanity rising through skill and labour. For a city of merchants and craftsmen, the message felt personal.

Here is the second surprise. The panels you see today are copies. The originals were moved indoors to protect them from weather and pollution. You can find them in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo, the cathedral museum just behind the apse. Seeing them there, at eye level, is a different experience. You notice the faces, the tools, even the folds of cloth. If you have time, visit after the climb, and compare memory with stone.

Above the hexagonal panels sits a second row of diamond-shaped reliefs. These show the planets, the virtues, the liberal arts, and the sacraments. Above them are the niches, once filled with statues of prophets and sibyls. Some of those figures were carved by Donatello, the great sculptor of the early Renaissance. Locals have long called one of his bald prophets 'Zuccone', which means something like 'big pumpkin head'. That original also waits in the museum.

Now follow the windows upward. This is one of the cleverest tricks of the design. Near the bottom, the openings are small and narrow. Higher up, they grow wider. Each window gains more divisions and more light. At the top, the openings are tall and open. This makes the tower look lighter as it rises. Heavy stone seems to dissolve into air. The eye moves up without effort, just as the builders intended.

The tower stands about eighty-four metres tall. It is a few metres shorter than Brunelleschi's dome next door. And there is no lift. To reach the top, you climb 414 steps. The staircase is narrow, and in places it feels tight. But the climb is broken into stages. On each level you can pause, catch your breath, and look out through the windows. The bells hang inside, and you pass close to them.

The top terrace gives one of the most rewarding views in Florence. Look one way, and the red dome of the cathedral fills the frame. You can see the curve of the brick, the white ribs, and the small figures on the lantern. Look farther, and the terracotta roofs spread toward green hills. You may hear church bells from other parts of the city. Up here, the noise of the square becomes a soft hum below.

One more detail is easy to miss. The flat top of the tower was not part of the original plan. Drawings suggest a pointed spire was once intended to crown it. That spire was never built. Many people think the tower is better without it. The flat roof gives a calm, clean ending. It also lets the dome remain the clear star of the skyline. Florence, in a way, chose balance over height.

As you step back from the tower, take a final look from across the square. Notice how it stands apart from the cathedral, close but separate. Notice how the morning or afternoon light warms the white marble and deepens the green. Listen for the bells. They still mark the hours of the city. And as you walk away, remember the painter who began it — a man who never saw it finished, but whose name the stone still carries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear comfortable shoes for the long, narrow staircase.
Book the climb as part of the cathedral pass.
Climb early to avoid crowds on narrow stairs.
Visit the cathedral museum to see original reliefs.
Use the intermediate levels to rest and look out.
