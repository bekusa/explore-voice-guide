The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the wide lawn and feel the heat rise from the ground. The air smells of cut grass and warm stone. In front of you, a long white facade fills your view. It is plain and bright, almost blinding at midday. A crow calls from the palm trees. Somewhere behind you, a tour bus idles. This is the Se Cathedral of Old Goa, and its size takes a few seconds to register.

The name itself needs a short explanation. The word Se comes from the Portuguese word for a bishop's seat, or see. So this is the main church of the archdiocese, the place where the bishop's chair stood. It is dedicated to Saint Catherine of Alexandria, an early Christian martyr. That choice was not random. It ties this building directly to the moment the Portuguese took the city.

In 1510, Portuguese forces under Afonso de Albuquerque captured Goa from the Sultanate of Bijapur, a powerful kingdom of the Deccan plateau. The decisive victory fell on the feast day of Saint Catherine. The Portuguese read this as a sign. A small chapel to the saint went up first. Decades later, they decided the city needed something far grander — a cathedral worthy of the capital of their eastern empire.

Construction began in the 1560s. It moved slowly, as most great churches do. The main structure took over half a century to complete, and the cathedral was consecrated in 1640. Think about what that means. The workers who laid the foundations did not live to see the finished building. Their children, and perhaps their grandchildren, carried the work forward.

Now look closely at the facade. The style is often described as Portuguese Manueline on the outside, with clean Tuscan lines. Tuscan is the simplest of the classical orders — plain columns, little decoration. Here is the first surprise. The walls are not built of marble or fine cut stone. They are mostly laterite, the rough, reddish local rock, covered in thick lime plaster and whitewash. That white skin protects the porous stone from the monsoon rains.

You may notice something odd about the shape. There is one tower, on one side only, and the facade looks a little unbalanced. That is because the cathedral once had two towers. The second one collapsed in 1776 and was never rebuilt. So the building you see today is, in a sense, incomplete. Once you notice the empty space, it is hard to stop seeing it.

The surviving tower holds the famous Golden Bell. It is said to be the largest bell in Goa, and it is known for its deep, rich tone. The name refers to that sound, not to the metal. Locals say the bell once rang during the public ceremonies of the Goa Inquisition, a dark chapter of religious persecution here. Treat that link as tradition rather than documented record. Either way, if you are lucky enough to hear it ring, the sound carries far across the flat land.

Step inside, and the temperature drops. Your eyes need a moment to adjust from the glare. The interior is long and high, with rows of columns in the more ornate Corinthian style, topped with carved leaves. The contrast is deliberate. Plain and severe outside, richer and more decorated within. The floor carries old stone slabs, and the walls rise in pale, quiet layers.

Walk toward the main altar. It is covered in gilded wood, glowing gold even in low light. Carved and painted panels show scenes from the life of Saint Catherine, including her trial and her martyrdom. Tradition says she was condemned to death on a spiked wheel. Take your time here. The detail is easy to miss if you only glance from the doorway.

Along the sides of the nave, you will find a series of chapels. Each one has its own altar and its own dedication. One of the most visited is the Chapel of the Cross of Miracles. According to a popular local tale, a vision of Christ appeared on a simple cross in the seventeenth century, and the cross was said to grow in size. Believers still come to pray before it. Whatever you believe, the quiet attention of the people around you is real.

Now think about the city around this church. Old Goa was once a crowded colonial capital, sometimes compared to Lisbon itself. Then epidemics swept through it, and over the following centuries people moved away. The capital eventually shifted to Panaji, closer to the sea. Most of the old city vanished. Only the great religious buildings remained, standing among fields and trees.

That is why this cathedral, together with the nearby churches and convents, became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986. Turn around and look across the green. Facing you stands the Basilica of Bom Jesus, with its reddish, unplastered stone. It holds the remains of Saint Francis Xavier, a famous missionary. The two buildings, one white and one red, make a striking pair.

Before you leave, stand once more on the lawn and look up at the lonely tower. Listen to the wind in the palms and the distant traffic. Notice the cracks and patches in the plaster, the stains left by many monsoons. This building has outlasted the city it was built for. As you walk away, glance back once. The white wall will still be there, catching the light, as it has for nearly four centuries.
