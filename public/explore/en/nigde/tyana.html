The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across the open plain, and somewhere a tractor works a field. Under your feet the soil is pale and dusty, and the light here is wide and bright. Ahead of you, a line of stone arches stands in the farmland like a row of open doors. This is Tyana, close to the village of Kemerhisar, a short drive south of Niğde.

It does not look like much at first. There is no great temple and no city wall in sight. But for well over two thousand years, people lived, traded, prayed and fought on this ground. Much of the ancient city still lies under the fields and the modern village. What you see is only the part that stayed above the earth.

Start with the name of the village. In Turkish, kemer means arch, and hisar means fortress or castle. So Kemerhisar is, roughly, the castle of the arches. The arches are the Roman aqueduct, the most visible piece of Tyana today. Generations of villagers named their home after it. That alone tells you how long these stones have shaped daily life here.

The story begins long before Rome. In the Bronze Age, this area appears in Hittite texts under a name close to Tuwanuwa. The Hittites were a powerful Anatolian people who ruled much of central Turkey more than three thousand years ago. Later, in the early first millennium before Christ, a smaller kingdom called Tuwana ruled this plain. Its best-known king, Warpalawas, appears in a famous rock relief at İvriz, south of here in the Taurus foothills.

Why here? Look south toward the mountains. Tyana sat on one of the great routes of the ancient world. Roads ran from central Anatolia down toward the Cilician Gates, a narrow pass through the Taurus Mountains to the Mediterranean coast. Armies, merchants and pilgrims all had to pass this way. A town at the mouth of that route could grow rich, and it could also attract trouble.

Now for a name you may know. In the first century, Tyana was the home of Apollonius of Tyana, a wandering philosopher and teacher. Ancient writers described him as an ascetic who travelled widely and was said to perform wonders. A biography about him was written more than a century after his death, so historians treat many of its stories with caution. Still, his fame carried the name of this small town across the Roman world.

Here is a story that many visitors miss. In the year 272, the Roman emperor Aurelian marched east against Zenobia, the queen of Palmyra, who had taken control of much of the Roman East. Tyana resisted him and closed its gates. According to a later Roman account, Aurelian planned to destroy the city. Then, the tale says, Apollonius appeared to him in a vision and asked him to spare it. Aurelian did spare Tyana. The vision is legend, but the mercy became part of the city's memory.

Walk closer to the aqueduct now. The arches are built from cut stone blocks, and you can see where the mortar has weathered and where stones are missing. The structure carried fresh water from springs in the hills toward the Roman city. Water moved by gravity alone, along a carefully measured slope. Stand at one end and look along the line. You can still follow the direction the water once travelled.

Nearby is another piece of the water story, known locally as the Roman Pool. It is a stone-lined basin fed by natural springs. Even today, clear water collects here, and on warm days the air near it feels cooler. It is a quiet reminder that the springs which gave Tyana life are still running. The Romans built around the water, but the water came first.

In the Byzantine period, Tyana became an important Christian city and the seat of a bishop. Later it served as the capital of a Roman province in southern Cappadocia. Cappadocia is the historic name of this part of central Turkey, better known to travellers for its rock-cut churches farther north. Because Tyana stood on the main road to the frontier, it also became a target when the Byzantine and Arab worlds went to war.

The early ninth century brought the hardest years. Abbasid armies raided this region several times, and the Caliph Harun al-Rashid is recorded as taking Tyana during one campaign. Some years later, his son, the Caliph al-Ma'mun, began to rebuild the town as a forward military base. But al-Ma'mun died on campaign in 833, and the project stopped. After that, Tyana slowly faded from the big stage of history.

Look around at the village around you. You may notice old cut stones built into walls, doorways and garden edges. For centuries, people here reused what the ancient city left behind. This is common across Anatolia, and it means Tyana is not only in the ground. Parts of it are in the houses. Archaeologists continue to excavate here, and each season adds more detail to the picture.

Before you leave, stand still near the arches once more. Look at the shadows between the stones, and at the fields that run right up to them. Listen to the wind, the birds, perhaps a dog barking in the village. Philosophers, emperors, bishops and soldiers all passed this exact spot... and now it belongs to farmers and to you. As you walk away, glance back once. The arches will still be standing in the light.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with the İvriz rock relief to the south.
Come early morning or late afternoon for softer light.
Bring water and a hat; the plain has little shade.
Wear sturdy shoes for dusty, uneven ground near the arches.
Ask locals in Kemerhisar for directions to the Roman Pool.
