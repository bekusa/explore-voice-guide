The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. A dry wind moves through the brush with a soft, papery hiss. Gravel crunches under your boots, coarse and pale. Somewhere nearby, a cactus wren calls with a rough, rattling voice, like an engine that will not quite start. Around you stand hundreds of green columns, ribbed and spined, their arms raised against a wide blue sky. This is Saguaro National Park, on the edge of Tucson in southern Arizona. These giant cactus plants grow naturally in only one place on Earth.

That place is the Sonoran Desert. It stretches across parts of Arizona, California, and northwestern Mexico. The saguaro, pronounced sah-WAH-ro, grows nowhere else in the wild. Its range has firm limits. Long, hard freezes can kill it, so it cannot spread far north or climb too high into the mountains. If you have ever seen a cactus in a cartoon about the American West, with arms raised like a person surrendering, it was almost certainly a saguaro. Here, you are standing among the real thing.

The park is divided into two separate districts, and the city of Tucson sits between them. To the west is the Tucson Mountain District. Its saguaros grow thickly across rocky slopes and low hills. To the east is the Rincon Mountain District. There, the land rises from desert flats into high mountains with oak and pine forests near the top. Many first-time visitors do not realise the two halves are a long drive apart. Each district has its own visitor centre and its own character.

Protection of this landscape began in 1933, when it became a national monument. In 1994, it was upgraded to a national park. People did not come here only for scenery. Early scientists worried that the great stands of saguaros were declining. Cattle grazing, wood cutting, and frost all took a toll. The park was created partly so that this desert could recover, and so that researchers could watch how a saguaro forest lives and changes over many decades.

And decades is the right word. A saguaro grows with great patience. In its first years, a seedling may be only a few centimetres tall. It survives by hiding in the shade of a nurse tree, often a palo verde or a mesquite. The nurse tree shields the young cactus from burning sun and from frost. Here is a surprise most visitors miss. Over time, the cactus often outlives the tree that protected it. Sometimes the old nurse tree dies, and the saguaro stands alone where it once sheltered.

The famous arms come late. Many saguaros do not grow their first arm until they are several decades old. Some never grow arms at all and stay as single green spears. A large, many-armed saguaro you see today may have been alive for well over a century. The tallest ones can rise well over ten metres. Look at the trunk and you will see deep vertical pleats, like the folds of an accordion. Those folds are how the plant stores water.

When rain comes, the saguaro drinks quickly through a wide net of shallow roots. The pleats then expand outward, and the trunk swells with stored water. A fully soaked saguaro becomes extremely heavy. In dry months, the pleats slowly pull back together as the plant uses its supply. Inside the trunk, a ring of woody ribs holds everything upright. When a saguaro dies and its green flesh falls away, those ribs remain, standing like the bones of a small building.

Look closely at the trunks, and you may see round holes high up in the green skin. These are not damage from people. Gila woodpeckers and gilded flickers carve nest cavities into the cactus. The saguaro responds by sealing the inside of the hole with a hard, protective layer. When the cactus later dies, that hardened shell can fall to the ground in one piece. Locals call it a saguaro boot. Old holes later become homes for other birds, including the tiny elf owl.

In late spring and early summer, the saguaro blooms. Waxy white flowers open near the tips of the trunk and arms. Each flower opens at night and stays open into the next day. Bats, birds, and bees all feed on the nectar and carry pollen from plant to plant. Later, the flowers become fleshy red fruits full of tiny black seeds. The saguaro blossom is the official state wildflower of Arizona, so you will see it on signs and emblems across the region.

This fruit has deep meaning for the Tohono O'odham people, whose homeland covers much of this desert. For generations, families have harvested saguaro fruit in early summer. They use long poles, traditionally made from saguaro ribs, to knock the fruit down from the tops of the plants. The fruit is cooked into syrup and other foods. For the Tohono O'odham, the harvest is tied to the coming of the summer rains and the start of a new year. It is a living tradition, not a museum piece.

People lived in this desert long before the Tohono O'odham harvest you just heard about was ever recorded. In the Tucson Mountain District, a short trail at Signal Hill leads up to a pile of dark boulders. Carved into these rocks are petroglyphs, images pecked into the stone surface. Archaeologists link them to the Hohokam, an ancient farming people of this region. You can see spirals and other shapes. Their exact meaning is not known, and that open question is part of their power.

To explore by car, each district has a scenic loop. In the east, the Cactus Forest Loop Drive winds through dense stands of saguaros. In the west, the Bajada Loop Drive is an unpaved road through rugged desert hills. Both have pullouts and short trails. Please treat the heat with respect. Even short walks here can drain your body quickly. Carry more water than you think you need, wear a hat, and keep a careful distance from the spines of every cactus, including the small ones close to the ground.

As the afternoon fades, the light here changes fast. The low sun turns the hills gold, then orange, then soft purple. The saguaros become black outlines against the glowing sky, their arms frozen in mid-gesture. Then the desert cools, and a new set of sounds begins. You may hear insects, the call of a distant owl, or the faint yip of a coyote. Many desert animals wait through the hot day and come out only after dark.

Before you leave, choose one saguaro near you and simply look at it. Notice its pleats, its scars, perhaps a woodpecker hole near the top. This plant may have been a tiny seedling under a palo verde long before anyone alive today was born. It has lived through dry years, cold nights, and summer storms. As you walk back along the gravel, listen again to the wind in the brush. The desert is quiet, but it is far from empty.

What to look for

Round woodpecker holes high in saguaro trunks.
Accordion-like pleats running down each trunk.
Young saguaros sheltering under palo verde trees.
Exposed woody ribs on fallen, dead saguaros.
Spiral petroglyphs on the boulders at Signal Hill.
Before you go

Practical tips

Carry plenty of water, even for very short walks.
Visit early morning or near sunset to avoid peak heat.
Drive the Cactus Forest Loop in the eastern district.
Check which district you want before setting off.
Keep a careful distance from all cactus spines.
