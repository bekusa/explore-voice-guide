The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves through creosote bushes, and the air carries a sharp, resinous smell, a bit like rain on hot pavement. The ground underfoot is coarse gravel, pale and loose, and it crunches with every step. Around you, tall green columns rise from the desert floor in clusters, like bundles of candles pushed into the earth. This is Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, in the far southwest of Arizona, where the Sonoran Desert runs south across the border into Mexico.

The cactus that gives this place its name is easy to spot once you know the shape. A saguaro usually grows as one thick trunk with arms. The organ pipe is different. It sends up many slender stems straight from the ground, all rising together. Early travellers thought the clusters looked like the pipes of a church organ, and the name stayed. Stand back from one, and you can see why. The stems stand side by side, each one ribbed and lined with spines.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. This monument sits at the northern edge of the organ pipe's natural range. Most of these cacti grow in Mexico, in Sonora and along the Gulf of California. In the United States, this corner of Arizona is close to the only place they grow in any number. The plant is sensitive to frost, so it favours warm, south-facing slopes. As you drive or walk, notice how the cacti gather on rocky hillsides that catch the sun.

The land was protected in 1937, when President Franklin Roosevelt set it aside as a national monument. Decades later, in 1976, it was named an international biosphere reserve under a United Nations programme. That title recognises places where people study how a living landscape works over time. Much of the monument is also managed as designated wilderness. In practice, that means there are few roads, few buildings, and long distances of open desert with no development at all.

If you visit in late spring or early summer, there is a second surprise. The organ pipe blooms at night. Its pale flowers open after dark and usually close by the next morning. Bats are among the most important pollinators. They fly up from Mexico and move from flower to flower, feeding on nectar. So the plants you see in daylight depend, in part, on visitors that arrive after sunset and leave before you wake.

After the flowers come the fruit — round, spiny, and red inside when ripe. For the Tohono O'odham people, whose homelands cover much of this desert, cactus fruit harvests have long been an important part of the year. The O'odham knew where water could be found and which plants could feed a family. Their knowledge shaped how people lived here long before any park boundary was drawn. The desert around you was never empty. It was a working, known landscape.

Look at the other plants as well, because the organ pipe has company. Saguaros stand tall and single-trunked across the flats. Chain fruit cholla carries hanging strings of fruit that look like green beads. Ocotillo sends up long, thorny whips that turn green with leaves after rain, then drop them again in dry weather. If you are lucky, you may also find the senita, a rarer columnar cactus whose older stems are covered with grey, hair-like spines.

The Kris Eggle Visitor Center is a good place to begin. It is named for a park ranger who was killed in the line of duty here in 2002. Inside, rangers can tell you about current road and trail conditions, which change with weather and season. Exhibits explain the plants, animals, and people of the area. It is also a reminder that this monument lies right along an international border, and that working here can be demanding.

From the visitor centre area, the Ajo Mountain Drive is the classic way to see the landscape. It is a long, unpaved scenic loop that climbs toward the Ajo Range, where some of the densest stands of organ pipe grow. The road is slow, and that is the point. Pull-outs let you stop, turn off the engine, and listen. Along the way, a natural stone arch sits high on a ridge near the trailhead for Arch Canyon.

On the western side of the monument lies Quitobaquito Springs, a small pond fed by underground water. In this dry country, it is a true oasis. Cottonwood trees grow around the edge, and birds gather in large numbers. The spring is home to a tiny desert fish, the Quitobaquito pupfish, which lives almost nowhere else on earth. People have stopped here for water for a very long time, and travellers on old desert routes knew this place well.

The border is never far away in this monument. In recent years, construction of a tall border barrier along the southern edge brought heavy machinery into the desert. Scientists, tribal members, and conservation groups raised concerns about effects on Quitobaquito's water and on sites important to the O'odham. Whatever your view, it is worth knowing that this quiet landscape sits at the centre of serious national debates about land, water, and security.

The animals here are often shy and active at dawn or dusk. Watch for desert bighorn sheep on the rocky slopes, and for javelina, a pig-like animal that moves in small groups. Coyotes call at night. Gila woodpeckers carve nest holes into saguaros, and those holes later become homes for other birds. On warm days, lizards dart between rocks. Stay alert, too, for rattlesnakes. Give every snake plenty of space and simply walk around it.

Before you leave, stop for a moment and look at the nearest cluster of organ pipe stems. Notice the shallow ribs that let each stem swell with water after rain and shrink during drought. Notice how the light changes the colour of the hills from tan to rose as the sun drops lower. As you walk back to the road, listen once more for the wind in the creosote — the same sound this desert has carried long before anyone came to name it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the Kris Eggle Visitor Center for road conditions.
Carry far more water than you think you need.
Drive Ajo Mountain Drive slowly and use the pull-outs.
Visit in early morning or late afternoon to avoid heat.
Give rattlesnakes plenty of space and walk around them.
