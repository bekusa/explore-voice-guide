The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through dry grass, and somewhere a magpie calls. Around you the ground falls away in bands of grey, tan and rust. The hills are bare, carved into ridges and gullies by water and time. This is the Red Deer River valley in Alberta, Canada. Locals call it the badlands. The museum sits low among these hills, as if it grew out of the slope. Before you go inside, look at the earth. It is the reason this building exists.

Those stripes in the hillside are layers of rock. Each band was once mud, sand, or ash laid down when this land looked very different. Around seventy million years ago, this valley was a warm, swampy coastal plain. A shallow inland sea lay to the east. Rivers carried sediment across the lowlands and buried plants and animals as they died. Over millions of years, that mud turned to stone. Then the last Ice Age ended, and meltwater cut this deep valley. The water opened the rock like the pages of a book.

The museum takes its name from Joseph Burr Tyrrell, a young geologist with the Geological Survey of Canada. In 1884, he was mapping this region and searching for coal. Instead, he found something else. Near the Red Deer River, he uncovered part of the skull of a large meat-eating dinosaur. That animal later became known as Albertosaurus, a smaller cousin of Tyrannosaurus. Tyrrell was looking for fuel. He found a door into the deep past.

His coal was real too. In the early twentieth century, Drumheller became a mining town. Dozens of mines once worked the seams in these hills. Miners dug through the same rock layers that held dinosaur bones. The mines are gone now, but the coal is still here. You can often see thin black bands in the hillsides. They are the remains of ancient swamp forests, pressed into stone.

The museum opened in 1985. A few years later, Queen Elizabeth the Second granted it the title Royal. It is Canada's only museum dedicated entirely to palaeontology, the science of ancient life. That matters. This is not only a gallery. It is a working research institution. Scientists here collect, study, and describe new species. Many of the fossils you will see were found in Alberta, some only a short drive from where you stand.

Inside, the story is told in order, from the oldest life to the most recent. One early gallery focuses on the Burgess Shale, a famous fossil site in the Rocky Mountains of British Columbia. Its creatures lived more than five hundred million years ago, long before the dinosaurs. They are small and strange. Some have five eyes. Some look like nothing alive today. Take your time here. Most visitors hurry past to reach the big skeletons... and miss some of the oddest animals on Earth.

Here is the first surprise. One of the most important fossils in the building is not a skeleton at all. It is an armoured dinosaur called Borealopelta. In 2011, a heavy equipment operator found it in an oil sands mine in northern Alberta. The animal had likely drifted out to sea and sunk, belly up. It was preserved in three dimensions, with its armour plates and skin still in place. Scientists even found traces of its original colouring.

Stand in front of it and look closely. It does not look like bones. It looks like a sleeping animal turned to stone. Researchers studied chemical traces in the skin and concluded it was reddish-brown on top and lighter underneath. That pattern is called countershading. Many modern animals, like deer, use it for camouflage. So even a heavily armoured plant-eater, the size of a small car, still needed to hide from predators.

Then comes Dinosaur Hall, the heart of the museum. The ceiling rises high above you, and full skeletons stand in poses of walking, hunting, and feeding. You will hear footsteps echo, children calling out names, the low hum of the building. Look for the horned dinosaurs and the duck-billed hadrosaurs. Alberta has produced a remarkable number of both. Many of their skeletons were dug out of rock layers you just saw on the drive in.

The second surprise waits among the predators. Look for a Tyrannosaurus rex skeleton known as Black Beauty. Its bones are a deep, glossy black. That colour comes from minerals, especially manganese, that soaked into the bones as they fossilized. The specimen was found in 1980 by two teenagers fishing in southern Alberta. They spotted a bone sticking out of a riverbank. Sometimes the greatest discoveries begin with someone simply paying attention.

Keep an eye out for the preparation lab. Through large windows, you can often watch technicians at work. They use small air tools, fine needles, and patience to free bone from rock. Some fossils take months or even years to prepare. The work is slow and quiet. It is a good reminder that every skeleton in the hall began as a heavy block of stone wrapped in plaster, carried out of a hillside.

Near the end, the Cretaceous Garden offers a change of pace. It is filled with living plants related to those that grew here in the age of dinosaurs. Ferns, cycads, and conifers grow in the warm, damp air. The smell is green and earthy. Close your eyes and you can almost picture the swampy coast that once covered this valley. Then the final galleries carry the story forward, past the extinction of the dinosaurs, toward the rise of mammals and the world we know.

When you step back outside, the air will feel dry again. Look at the hills once more. Now you know what is inside them. Each grey band and each rusty ledge may still hold bones no one has seen. Walk slowly along the paths if you have time. Notice the cactus, the sage, the crumbling clay under your shoes. The badlands are still eroding, a little every year. And somewhere out there, the next discovery is waiting for someone to look down at the right moment.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least half a day for the full galleries.
Walk the outdoor badlands trail before or after your visit.
Bring water and a hat for the exposed badlands paths.
Visit early on summer days to avoid the busiest crowds.
Check ahead for guided fossil programs needing separate booking.
