The guide

The story

Stand close and look at the brick. It is not flat. It is pressed and shaped into tiny ridges and hollows, so the wall catches the sun like woven cloth. Here and there, a flash of turquoise glaze breaks through the warm honey colour of the clay. In the dry Nakhchivan air, the tower rises above you, ten-sided, sharp-edged and quiet. Before we talk about kings or dates, take a moment simply to look. Few buildings anywhere carry their decoration this deeply into their own skin.

This is the Momine Khatun Mausoleum, one of the great surviving works of medieval architecture in the Caucasus. It was built in the late twelfth century. An inscription on the building gives the year in the Islamic calendar, and it corresponds to 1186. That makes this tower more than eight hundred years old. It has stood through earthquakes, invasions and changing empires, and much of its original surface is still here for you to read.

The tower was raised under the Eldiguzids, a dynasty of atabegs. An atabeg was a powerful regent, originally a guardian who governed on behalf of a young prince, and in this region the atabegs became rulers in their own right. Nakhchivan was one of their main cities. The mausoleum honours Momine Khatun, a woman of the ruling family. Sources differ on her exact place in that family, so it is safer to say simply that she was a royal woman important enough to receive one of the finest tombs of her age.

Here is the first surprise. We know the name of the man who designed it. In much of the medieval world, architects vanished from history, and their buildings stand anonymous. But here the builder signed his work. His name was Ajami ibn Abubakr, remembered today as Ajami Nakhchivani. His name appears in the inscriptions, set into the building itself. Imagine the confidence that took — a master craftsman placing his own name on a royal tomb.

Ajami did not build only this tower. An earlier mausoleum in the city, the tomb of Yusif ibn Kuseyir, is also credited to him. That one is eight-sided and plainer. If you have time to see both, you can follow the growth of one architect's ideas over more than twenty years. The later tower is taller, more complex and far richer in ornament. It feels like the work of a man at the height of his skill.

Now walk slowly around the building. Count the faces as you go. There are ten of them, each one framed by a tall, recessed panel that ends in a pointed arch near the top. At first the panels look identical. Look again. The geometric patterns change from face to face. Stars, interlaced lines and polygons fold into one another, built not from paint or plaster but from carefully cut and arranged brick. The whole surface works like one continuous carpet of geometry.

This kind of ornament had deep meaning in the medieval Islamic world. Geometry was seen as a way to suggest order and infinity without showing human or animal figures. A pattern that seems to have no beginning and no end invites the eye to keep moving. Stand still for a moment and let your gaze follow a single line. You may find it leads you around a star, into a neighbouring shape, and back again, without ever breaking.

Near the top, the tower carries a band of writing. The script is Kufic, an early, angular style of Arabic calligraphy with strong vertical strokes. Here the letters are worked into the brick and highlighted with glaze. The inscriptions include religious texts as well as historical information about the building. Even if you cannot read Arabic, you can see how the calligraphy becomes part of the architecture — a crown of words running around the upper walls.

The second surprise is about height. Today the tower is impressive, but it is not complete. Its original upper section, including the roof that once capped it, has been lost over the centuries. Historians believe it was once noticeably taller than it is now. So what you see is a building that has lost its crown, yet still dominates its surroundings. Try to picture it with a pointed or domed top, rising even higher over the medieval city.

A tomb like this usually has two levels. Above ground stands the memorial tower, the part meant to be seen from far away. Below ground lies the crypt, the burial chamber itself. That pattern is shared with other tower tombs across Iran, Azerbaijan and Anatolia. The tower speaks to the living city. The crypt is a quieter, enclosed space for the dead. Whether you can enter the lower chamber depends on current arrangements, so ask the staff on site.

In its own time, this tower did not stand alone. It belonged to a wider complex of royal buildings in the heart of Nakhchivan, the seat of the atabegs. Most of those structures have disappeared. That is part of why this mausoleum matters so much. It is one of the clearest physical links to that court, its wealth and its taste. Today the area around it is laid out as open grounds, which gives you space to see the tower from every side.

The building also has a strong presence in modern Azerbaijani identity. Ajami Nakhchivani is honoured as a national figure, and you may notice a monument to him in the area. The mausoleum appears on the tentative list for UNESCO World Heritage status, which means it has been formally proposed for that recognition. For local people, the tower is not only an old tomb. It is a symbol of the city, and of a period when Nakhchivan sat at the centre of a regional power.

Before you leave, step back to a distance where you can see the whole tower at once. Notice how the light shifts across the ten faces. One side glows, the next falls into shadow, and the patterns sharpen or soften with the angle of the sun. Listen to the city around you, the traffic and voices moving past a building that has watched eight centuries go by. As you walk away, look back once more. The turquoise glaze will still be catching the light.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk a full circle to compare all ten decorated faces.
Visit in morning or late afternoon for sharper brick shadows.
Ask staff whether the lower crypt is open to visitors.
Pair your visit with Ajami's earlier Yusif ibn Kuseyir tomb.
Bring water and sun protection in the dry summer heat.
