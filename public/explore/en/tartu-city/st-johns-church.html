The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look at the wall in front of you. It is brick, deep red and orange, warmed by the sun or darkened by rain. Run your eyes slowly upward, and something strange appears. Faces. Small clay faces, looking back at you from the walls of this church. Some are calm, some frown, and some seem almost ready to speak. You are standing at Saint John's Church in Tartu, known in Estonian as Jaani kirik.

This is a Lutheran church today, but its story begins in the Middle Ages. It is one of the finest examples of Brick Gothic in the Baltic region. Brick Gothic was the style of the medieval towns around the Baltic Sea, where good building stone was scarce but clay was plentiful. Builders learned to shape and fire brick into pointed arches, tall windows and patterned gables. The main body of this church dates largely from the fourteenth century, when Tartu was a prosperous trading town.

That prosperity matters. In the Middle Ages, Tartu was known by its German name, Dorpat. It belonged to the Hanseatic League, a network of merchant towns that traded across northern Europe. Goods moved through here between Russia and the West, including furs, wax, salt and cloth. Wealthy merchants and craftsmen wanted their town to have churches worthy of that wealth. Saint John's was one of the results, and it still stands close to the old heart of the town.

Now, back to those faces, because they are the reason many visitors travel here. The church is decorated with terracotta sculptures, which means figures made of fired clay. Medieval builders placed them in niches on the façade, around the portal and inside the church. The number of these figures is unusual for medieval Europe. Very few churches anywhere used terracotta on this scale. Here is the first surprise: they are not a repeated pattern. Look closely, and you will see that the faces are different from one another.

Some have beards. Some wear caps or crowns. Some look young, others old and tired. Historians have long wondered whether a few of them were modelled on real people from the medieval town. Nobody can say for certain, and it is best to treat that idea as a possibility rather than a fact. But standing here, it is easy to imagine. Perhaps a merchant, a priest or a local craftsman is still looking down from the wall, centuries after his name was forgotten.

The main portal is a good place to pause. Medieval churches often used the entrance as a kind of sermon in stone, or here, in clay. The figures around a doorway prepared people for what they would find inside. In many Gothic churches, these scenes showed saints, prophets and the Last Judgement, the moment when the dead are judged at the end of time. As you stand near the doorway, notice how the figures are arranged in rows and frames, guiding your eye toward the entrance.

The second surprise is how close this building came to disappearing. Tartu has suffered many fires and wars. In the eighteenth century, a great fire swept through much of the town, and the old centre had to be rebuilt. The church survived those centuries, but the twentieth century was harder. During the Second World War, in 1944, fighting reached Tartu. The church was badly damaged. Its roof burned, and the interior was left open to the weather.

For many years after the war, the building stood as a damaged shell. Estonia was then part of the Soviet Union, and a ruined church was not a priority for the authorities. Rain, frost and neglect continued to harm the brick and the fragile clay figures. Some terracotta pieces were lost. Others were removed and kept safe. When you look at the walls today, you are looking at a survivor, not an untouched medieval building.

After Estonia regained independence in 1991, serious restoration began. It was slow, careful work. Restorers had to decide what to rebuild and what to leave as it was. They studied old photographs, fragments and drawings. The church reopened in 2005 after this long effort. If you look carefully at the brickwork, you may notice differences in colour and texture. Some bricks are medieval. Others are modern replacements. Together, the old and new show how the building was repaired, layer by layer.

If the doors are open, step inside. The atmosphere changes at once. The street noise softens, and your footsteps sound different on the floor. The interior is bright and fairly simple, with white surfaces and red brick. This simplicity suits the Lutheran tradition, which generally prefers a plainer space than a medieval Catholic church once had. Let your eyes travel up the pillars toward the vaulting. More terracotta details appear inside, so take your time and look up.

The tower offers a different view of the church and the town. Visitors can often climb to a viewing area, though access may depend on the season and may require a separate ticket. From above, you see the red roofs of Tartu, the green slopes of Toome Hill, and the line of the Emajõgi River. Emajõgi means mother river in Estonian. From here, you can understand how the medieval town was laid out around trade, faith and the water.

Tartu today is best known as a university town. The University of Tartu is the oldest university in Estonia, and students fill the streets around here for much of the year. That gives this church an interesting position. It is a medieval building in a young, lively neighbourhood of cafés, small shops and old wooden and stone houses. Concerts and services still take place here, so the church is not only a monument. It remains a working place of worship and gathering.

Before you leave, walk once more along the outside wall. Choose one face, just one, and look at it for a while. Notice the curve of the nose, the shape of the eyes, the small marks left by weather and time. That face was shaped by a medieval hand, survived fire and war, and has now been looked at by generations of people standing exactly where you are. As you walk away down Jaani Street, you may notice that you are still looking up.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring binoculars to study the high terracotta faces.
Visit on a bright day when the façade figures show clearly.
Check for concerts, which often fill the church with music.
Respect services in progress and keep voices low inside.
Ask about tower access, which may need a separate ticket.
