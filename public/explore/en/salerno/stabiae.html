The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Below the edge of this plateau, the town of Castellammare di Stabia carries on with its day. You hear scooters, a church bell, maybe gulls over the harbour. Up here, the ground underfoot is old volcanic soil, and the air carries the smell of dry grass and warm stone. In front of you, across the water, rises the long grey shape of Vesuvius.

This is ancient Stabiae. You are on the Varano plateau, a natural terrace above the Bay of Naples. Most visitors to this coast head for Pompeii, a short distance away. Far fewer come here. That is a pity, and also a gift. The villas of Stabiae are a different kind of ruin. They are not a crowded town of shops and streets. They are the country houses of the Roman rich.

The story of this place has two endings. The first came in 89 BCE, during the Social War — a revolt by Rome's Italian allies, who wanted the rights of Roman citizens. Stabiae sided against Rome. The Roman general Sulla took the town and destroyed it. After that, Stabiae never really returned as a proper town. Instead, something new grew on this cliff edge.

Wealthy Romans saw what you are seeing now. A high, cool terrace. A wide view of the bay. Fresh springs in the hills behind. Over the following generations, they built villa after villa along the plateau. These were places for leisure, for long dinners and slow afternoons. Terraces and gardens opened toward the sea. Rooms were placed to catch the light and the breeze.

Then came the second ending. In 79 CE, Vesuvius erupted. Ash and pumice fell on Stabiae, just as they fell on Pompeii and Herculaneum. The villas were buried. And here, the eruption touches one of the most famous eyewitness stories from the ancient world.

Pliny the Elder was a Roman admiral and a great writer on nature. He commanded the fleet at Misenum, across the bay. When the eruption began, he sailed toward the danger, partly from curiosity and partly to help people on the shore. He landed at Stabiae, at the house of a friend. He died here on the shore. His nephew, Pliny the Younger, later described these events in letters to the historian Tacitus. Those letters still survive, and they are the reason we can picture that day at all.

The villas then slept under the ground for centuries. In the eighteenth century, the Bourbon kings of Naples began digging here, starting in 1749. Their workers were looking for treasure. They cut tunnels, removed frescoes and statues, and carried them away for the royal collections. Then, surprisingly, they filled the sites back in. For about two hundred years, the villas went quiet again.

The modern story begins in 1950, and it has an unlikely hero. Libero D'Orsi was not a famous archaeologist. He was a local school headmaster. With a small team and very limited money, he began to dig again on this plateau. His work brought the villas back into the light. Today a museum in the hills above town carries his name.

Now look at the two main sites. Villa Arianna takes its name from a painting of Ariadne, the figure from Greek myth. In the story, the hero Theseus leaves her alone on an island after she helps him escape the labyrinth. The villa sits right at the edge of the cliff. Its rooms step down toward the old shoreline, which in Roman times lay much closer than the sea does today.

A short walk away is Villa San Marco. Its name is not Roman at all. It comes from a small chapel that stood nearby in the eighteenth century. This villa is laid out around open courtyards, with a long garden framed by columns and a large pool. As you walk through, notice how the house moves you from shade into sunlight, and from closed rooms into wide views.

Here is something many visitors miss. One of the most loved paintings from all of Roman art came from Villa Arianna. It shows a young woman walking away from the viewer, picking flowers. People often call her Flora, or Spring. That small fresco now hangs in the National Archaeological Museum in Naples. Its soft green background and light, careful brushwork still feel modern.

The walls that remain here also carry colour. Look for deep red panels, black bands, and fine painted borders. Look for small figures floating in the middle of large plain areas of colour. Romans liked this style. It made rooms feel calm and open. Some of the paint has faded, and some of it has been repaired, but the original idea is still clear.

Before you leave, walk back to the edge of the terrace. Look out over the rooftops, the harbour, and the bay. Then look up at Vesuvius. The Romans who lived here looked at the same mountain and saw only a green slope. They did not know it was a volcano. Today, the town below lives in its shadow too.

Take a last breath of this air. Feel the dry ground under your shoes, and listen to the town again. As you walk away, notice how ordinary life presses right up against these walls — houses, gardens, washing on balconies. Stabiae was a place people chose for its view and its quiet. In a small way, you just chose it too.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Pompeii's crowds first, then come here for quiet.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground and steep paths.
Bring water and a hat on summer afternoons.
See the Flora fresco later at the Naples museum.
Walk to the cliff edge for views toward Vesuvius.
