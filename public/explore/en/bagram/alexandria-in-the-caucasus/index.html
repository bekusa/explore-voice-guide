The guide

The story

Start with the wind. On the open plain north of Kabul, it comes down from the mountains carrying dust and the cold of high snow. The ground here is dry and pale. Low mounds rise from the fields, softened by centuries of rain. Look north and the wall of the Hindu Kush fills the horizon, white at the top for much of the year. Somewhere under these mounds lies a city that a young Macedonian king ordered into being, more than two thousand years ago.

The name sounds strange at first. Alexandria in the Caucasus... but the Caucasus is far to the west, between the Black Sea and the Caspian. So why here? The answer is a geographical mistake. When Alexander the Great and his army reached these mountains, his companions called the Hindu Kush the Caucasus. Some ancient writers believed this range joined the Caucasus mountains the Greeks already knew. The name stayed with the city, and it travelled down into the history books.

Alexander arrived here around 329 BC, in the middle of his long campaign to the east. He had crushed the Persian Empire. Now he was chasing a rival claimant to the Persian throne into Bactria, the land on the far side of these peaks. The ancient historian Arrian, who wrote centuries later but used earlier accounts, tells us that Alexander founded a city at the foot of the mountains. He settled local people here, along with some of his own veteran soldiers who were no longer fit to fight.

This was a pattern for Alexander. He planted cities named after himself across his empire — the most famous is in Egypt. Many of these foundations were not built from nothing. Often a Greek garrison and a new name were placed on an older settlement. Scholars think something similar may have happened here. This plain was already a meeting point of routes long before the Macedonians came. The Greeks gave it a new identity, but they did not invent its importance.

Now a word of honesty. Historians do not fully agree on where Alexandria in the Caucasus stood. Many place it at Bagram, near the ancient city of Kapisa. Others suggest nearby sites, such as Charikar or Jabal Saraj, a little further up the valley. No inscription has yet been found that says, clearly, this is Alexander's city. So when you look across this landscape, you are looking at the strongest candidate — not a certainty.

Why this spot? Look at the rivers and the passes. Two rivers, the Ghorband and the Panjshir, come down from the mountains and meet near here. Their valleys lead up toward the high passes that cross the Hindu Kush. Anyone moving between Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent had to pass close by. A city here could watch the roads, collect supplies, and protect an army's line of retreat. Alexander rested here before he crossed the mountains in spring.

He came back a second time, on his way toward India. According to Arrian, he was not pleased with what he found. The governor he had left in charge had ruled badly, and Alexander removed him. He added more settlers to strengthen the city. It is a small story, but it shows how fragile these new foundations were. A Greek name on a map did not make a working city. It needed people, water, food, and loyal leaders.

The centuries after Alexander were busy ones for this plain. Greek kings ruled from Bactria for a time. Later, the Kushans — a dynasty from Central Asia that built a large empire across Afghanistan and northern India — made Kapisa one of their important centres. Under the Kushans, roads from Rome, India, and China all crossed this region. Merchants, monks, and soldiers passed through. And that brings us to the most surprising discovery of all.

In the 1930s, French archaeologists dug into the mounds at Bagram. The team was led by Joseph Hackin, working with his wife, Ria Hackin. In two sealed rooms, they found an extraordinary collection, now called the Begram Hoard. There was painted glass from the Roman world. There were carved ivories from India. There were lacquer pieces from China, plaster medallions with Greek-style faces, and bronze figures. Objects from three great civilisations, gathered in one place.

Nobody knows for certain why these treasures were stored together. Some scholars think it was a royal treasury. Others suggest a merchant's warehouse, full of luxury goods waiting for buyers. Most date the objects roughly to the first or second century after Christ, long after Alexander. But the hoard shows what this crossroads became. The city at the foot of the mountains turned into a place where the whole ancient world met and traded.

The story of the hoard did not end with its discovery. Part of it went to the Musée Guimet in Paris. Part stayed in the National Museum in Kabul. During the years of war in the 1990s, the Kabul museum was badly damaged and looted, and many pieces were lost. Some were later recovered. Surviving objects have since travelled to exhibitions abroad, so people far from Afghanistan could see what came out of this ground.

The modern name Bagram is known worldwide for another reason. Next to the ancient site is a large military airfield. It was used by Soviet forces in the 1980s, and later by American and allied forces for about twenty years. The roar of aircraft once filled this sky. For many visitors, that is the stronger memory of the place. Yet under the runways and fields, older layers remain — Kushan, Greek, and earlier still.

Access to the archaeological area can be restricted. The security situation in Afghanistan changes, and the land near the airfield is sensitive. If you are here, follow local guidance closely. Even from a distance, though, you can read the landscape. Notice how the plain is green where water runs, and brown where it does not. Irrigation channels have shaped life here for thousands of years, long before any Greek arrived.

So take a moment before you leave. Feel the wind again, coming down from those white peaks. Alexander's soldiers felt it too, waiting for spring so they could cross. The mounds around you hold more than one city, layered one on top of another. Much of it has never been excavated. As you walk away, look at the line where the mountains meet the plain — that line is the reason anyone ever built here at all.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current security advice before travelling to Bagram.
Ask local authorities whether the archaeological area is accessible.
Never photograph the airfield or any military installation.
Visit the National Museum in Kabul to see hoard objects.
Bring layers; mountain wind makes the plain cold quickly.
