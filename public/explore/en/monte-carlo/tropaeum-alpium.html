The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here, the traffic noise of Monaco fades into wind, pine needles, and the dry click of insects in the grass. The air smells of warm stone and wild herbs. Below you, the Mediterranean stretches out flat and blue, and the towers of Monte Carlo look small enough to hold in your hand. And behind you rises a great pale ruin of columns and stacked limestone blocks. This is the Tropaeum Alpium, the Trophy of Augustus. It is about two thousand years old.

A quick note on place. The trophy does not stand inside Monaco itself. It stands in the French village of La Turbie, on the ridge just above the principality. The village name comes from the monument. Over the centuries, the Latin word for trophies, tropaea, slowly wore down into Turbie. So the village did not give its name to the monument. The monument gave its name to the village.

Why build something so large on a mountain shoulder? The answer is a war. For generations, the tribes of the Alps controlled the high passes between Italy and Gaul, which is roughly modern France. Roman armies and merchants needed those routes. Under the emperor Augustus, Rome finally fought a long series of campaigns and brought the mountain peoples under its control. This trophy was the Senate's thank-you gift to Augustus, completed in the last years before the Common Era.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. The monument carried a long inscription naming the defeated tribes, one after another. Dozens of them. We know the wording today because a Roman writer, Pliny the Elder, copied it into his great encyclopedia, the Natural History. Thanks to Pliny, scholars could rebuild the text. Look for the reconstructed inscription on the monument wall. It is one of the very few Roman texts that lists the peoples of the Alps by name.

The location was chosen with care. The trophy stood on the Via Julia Augusta, the Roman road that ran along this coast. This ridge was the highest point of that road along this stretch. It also marked a frontier — the line between Italy proper and the Roman province in southern Gaul. A traveller coming over the hill saw this monument before anything else. The message was simple. Rome is here, and the mountains belong to Rome.

Now look at the shape. The design works in layers. A square base forms the bottom. Above it, a round drum is ringed by tall columns. Above that, the structure once narrowed into a stepped cone. At the very top, historians believe, a statue of Augustus himself looked out over land and sea. In its full form the trophy rose roughly fifty metres high. What you see today is shorter, but it still towers over the village rooftops.

Then the story turns. After Rome fell, the trophy did not simply stand quietly. In the Middle Ages, people turned it into a fortress. Its thick walls were useful, and its view was even more useful. Later, during a war in the early eighteenth century, the French king Louis the Fourteenth ordered the fortifications of the region destroyed. The monument was blown apart. For a long time afterwards, it was little more than a broken stump of masonry.

Here is the second surprise. Much of the trophy did not vanish. It was recycled. Local builders treated the ruin as a quarry for good cut stone. Some of those blocks went into the village church of La Turbie, dedicated to Saint Michael. When you walk down into the village later, remember that the walls around you may contain pieces of an emperor's monument. The old Roman stones are still working — just in different buildings.

So how did it come back? In the early twentieth century, a wealthy American, Edward Tuck, paid for a major restoration. The French architect Jules Formigé led the work, studying the fallen blocks and the old descriptions. They did not rebuild everything. Instead, they raised part of the drum and set several columns back in place. Look closely and you can often tell old stone from new. The original blocks tend to be more weathered, softer at the edges.

There is also a literary echo here. In the fourteenth century, the Italian poet Dante mentioned La Turbie in his Divine Comedy. He used it as an example of the wild, rugged coast between two points on the Ligurian shore. For Dante, this ridge meant rough ground and steep climbing. Stand at the edge of the terrace and look down the slopes. You can easily understand why the name stayed in his mind.

Before you leave the grounds, visit the small museum on site. It shows fragments of carved stone recovered from the monument, along with models of the trophy in its complete form. The models help a great deal. Once you have seen the full shape, return outside and look again at the ruin. Your eye will start to fill in the missing levels — the cone, the steps, the statue against the sky.

The gardens are part of the experience too. Paths run between cypress and olive trees, and benches face the sea. On clear days the view runs along the coast, over Monaco's harbour and out towards the Italian border. Morning light is soft and cool on the limestone. In late afternoon the stone turns warm and golden. Wind can be strong up here, so hold on to hats and loose maps.

Now take one more slow look. The columns in front of you were raised to celebrate a victory over mountain peoples whose names survive mostly because they were carved here. Below, the modern city of Monte Carlo hums along, unaware. Rome is gone. The fortress is gone. The king's soldiers are gone. But the stone is still here, and so are you. As you walk back towards the village, notice the old blocks in the walls along the lane.
