The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind you, engines purr past the Casino Square. In front of you, there is gilded stone, warm and pale in the Mediterranean light. And somewhere beyond the building, below the terraces, the sea moves against the rocks. Few opera houses in the world stand this close to open water. This one shares its walls with a casino, and that strange partnership is the key to its whole story.

You are looking at the Opéra de Monte-Carlo, home to a hall known as the Salle Garnier. It is not a separate building in the usual sense. It forms part of the same grand complex as the Casino de Monte-Carlo. Gamblers and opera lovers have walked through shared spaces here for well over a century. In most cities, a temple of music and a house of chance would keep a polite distance. In Monaco, they sit under one roof.

The name Garnier may sound familiar. Charles Garnier was the architect of the Palais Garnier in Paris — the great opera house that inspired The Phantom of the Opera. After that enormous Paris project, he came south to the Riviera. Here he worked on a much smaller scale. The result is often described as a jewel box version of his Paris style. The ornament is just as rich, but everything is compressed into an intimate space.

The hall opened in 1879. At the time, Monaco was reinventing itself. The tiny principality had lost most of its territory earlier in the century, and with it much of its income. The ruling family and the company that ran the casino needed a new future. Gambling brought money. But culture brought prestige, and prestige brought the wealthy visitors who stayed for whole winters. An opera house was part of that plan.

Here is the first surprise. Most visitors only see the side facing the square, the one in front of you. But the building has a second face. On the far side, overlooking the sea, the façade is decorated with its own towers, statues and ornament. Garnier designed it to be seen from the water and from the terraces below. If you walk around to the casino terraces, you can see it rising above the Mediterranean.

Now imagine the interior, because the hall is the heart of the place. The Salle Garnier is decorated in red and gold. There are painted ceilings, carved figures, bronze details and heavy drapery. The room is small compared to the great opera houses of Europe — it seats only several hundred people. That size changes the experience. Singers do not have to fight across a vast distance. Even from the back, you feel close to the stage.

The second surprise is how much new music began here. For a few decades around 1900, this small hall became one of the most adventurous stages in Europe. The director Raoul Gunsbourg led the opera for a remarkably long period. Under his leadership, Monte Carlo hosted many world premieres. A small principality on the Riviera became a place where composers wanted their new works to be heard first.

Some of those premieres are still performed today. Jules Massenet, the French composer, saw several of his operas open here, including Don Quichotte in 1910. Giacomo Puccini's La Rondine premiered here in 1917. Maurice Ravel's L'Enfant et les sortilèges, a short opera about a naughty child whose toys and furniture come to life, had its first performance here in 1925. For a hall this size, it is an extraordinary list.

Dance has a place in this story too. In the early twentieth century, the Ballets Russes, the famous touring company of the Russian impresario Sergei Diaghilev, made Monte Carlo an important base. The company brought together dancers, composers and painters who changed modern ballet. Monaco still keeps that link alive. Today the principality has its own ballet company, Les Ballets de Monte-Carlo, which carries on the tradition of dance here.

The building you see today is also the result of careful care. Salt air, sunshine and time are hard on gilding and painted plaster. In the early twenty-first century, the hall went through a major restoration. Craftspeople worked to bring back the original colours and decoration. When you see how bright the ornament looks, remember that the sea air is always working against it. Keeping this much gold this close to the water takes constant effort.

Look again at the square around you. The Opéra and the Casino set the tone for the whole district. The gardens, the hotels and the cafés were all part of the same vision — a winter resort for Europe's elite. Many travellers arrived by train, which made the journey from Paris and beyond far easier in the late nineteenth century. Music in the evening, cards at night, and sunshine by day. That was the promise, and in many ways it still is.

If you want to hear the hall rather than just see it, the opera season is the best way. Performances take place mainly in the cooler months, following the old rhythm of the winter resort. Concerts by the Monte-Carlo Philharmonic Orchestra and ballet evenings also fill the calendar. Tickets go quickly for popular productions, so it is worth planning ahead. And dress up a little. In this setting, it feels right.

Before you move on, take one more look at the building in front of you. Notice how the stone shifts colour as the light changes — cream in the midday sun, honey and rose toward evening. Then walk around to the terraces, and turn back to see the sea-facing side. Listen for the waves below, and the faint traffic of the square behind. Music, money and the Mediterranean meet in this one spot. Few places hold all three so close together.
