The guide

The story

Look up. Above the rooftops of Vaduz, on a ledge of rock against the green wall of the mountain, a pale castle watches over the town. From the main street you can hear traffic and café chatter. Up there, it looks perfectly still. Its white walls catch the morning sun first, long before the light reaches the valley floor. Behind it, the forest climbs towards the Alps. Below it, the Rhine valley opens wide and flat towards Switzerland.

This is Vaduz Castle. And here is the first thing that surprises many visitors: it is not a museum. It is not a ruin, and it is not a hotel. It is a home. The castle is the residence of the Princely Family of Liechtenstein, one of the last reigning royal families in Europe. When you look up at those windows, you are looking at a family's private rooms. Somewhere inside, people are making coffee, answering letters, and getting on with their day.

That is why the gates stay closed to the public. You cannot buy a ticket and walk through the halls. For most visitors, this feels strange at first. We are used to castles as places we enter. But here, the experience is different. You come to see the castle from outside, to walk around it, and to look out from its terrace over the country it watches. In a way, that keeps it honest. It is still doing the job it was built for.

The oldest parts of the castle go back to the Middle Ages. Historians usually place the beginnings of the fortress around the twelfth century. Look for the tall, heavy keep — the main defensive tower. Its walls are very thick, and its lower windows are small. That tower was the heart of the early castle, a place of last refuge if attackers broke through. Everything else grew around it over the centuries.

This valley was not always peaceful. In 1499, during the Swabian War — a conflict between the Swiss Confederates and the Habsburg-led forces of southern Germany — Swiss troops set the castle on fire. Much of it burned. In the following decades, local lords rebuilt and strengthened it. The castle you see today carries layers from these different periods. Medieval stone, later repairs, and much younger restoration work sit side by side in the same walls.

Now for a twist in the story. The Liechtenstein family did not come from here. Their name comes from a castle near Vienna, in Austria. For centuries they were powerful nobles in the Habsburg lands, with large estates in Austria and Moravia. In 1712, they bought the County of Vaduz. Together with the nearby Lordship of Schellenberg, it became the Principality of Liechtenstein in 1719. So the country actually took its name from the family, not the other way around.

For a very long time, though, the princes did not live here. They ruled from a distance, mostly from Vienna, and the castle in Vaduz was left to decay. Visitors in the nineteenth century described a building in poor condition. Then, in the early twentieth century, serious restoration began. Workers repaired the walls, rebuilt sections, and slowly turned the old fortress into a building fit for a royal family to live in.

The real turning point came in 1938. In that year, Prince Franz Joseph the Second moved into the castle. He became the first reigning prince to make Liechtenstein his permanent home. This was a significant moment. Europe was moving towards war, and the family's ties to Austria were becoming complicated. By settling in Vaduz, the prince tied his family's future to this small Alpine country. His descendants have lived here ever since.

There is one day each year when the mood around the castle changes. On the national holiday in mid-August, the Princely Family invites people onto the castle grounds. Residents gather on the meadow and in the gardens. There are speeches, drinks, and conversation. In the evening, fireworks light up the sky above the valley. It is one of the rare chances to stand this close to the family in their own home, and many locals treat it as a highlight of the summer.

If you want to reach the castle, you can walk. A footpath climbs from the town centre up the hillside. It is steep in places, but the walk is not long, and the view improves with every bend. You pass through trees and open slopes. The noise of the town fades behind you. By the time you reach the top, you hear birds, wind in the leaves, and perhaps a cowbell from somewhere higher up the mountain.

Near the castle, the ground levels out, and the view opens across the whole valley. Below, you see the modern centre of Vaduz — glass, concrete, and the steep roof of the cathedral. Further out, the Rhine runs north between Liechtenstein and Switzerland. On a clear day, the Swiss mountains rise sharply on the far side. From up here, you can see just how small this country is. It covers only about 160 square kilometres.

As you walk around the outside, take a moment with the walls themselves. Notice the round towers and the thick, sloping base of the fortress. Notice how the white surfaces change colour as clouds pass. Look at the small windows high in the old tower, and then at the wider windows of the later living quarters. These differences show how the castle changed — from a place built for defence to a place built for living.

Stand still for a moment before you go. The castle behind you is not a stage set. It is a working home on a rock above a capital city, where history and everyday life continue under the same roof. As you head back down the path, turn around once or twice. Watch the white walls grow smaller against the forest. And notice how, from almost anywhere in Vaduz, you can still find the castle — keeping its quiet watch over the valley.
