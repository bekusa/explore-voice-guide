The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, the loudest sound is often the wind. It moves fine grey sand across the ground in thin, low ripples. It taps against old wooden posts that stand out of the dunes like the ribs of a ship. The light is flat and pale, and the horizon wavers in the heat. Under your feet the ground is a mix of soft sand and hard, cracked silt. This is the Niya site, deep in the Taklamakan Desert, and almost everything you see was once part of a living town.

The Taklamakan is one of the largest sand deserts on Earth. It fills the great basin of southern Xinjiang, ringed by high mountains. Rivers run down from those mountains, and some of them push north into the sand before they vanish. The Niya River is one of these. For centuries its water reached much farther into the desert than it does today. Where the water went, people followed. They built houses, planted orchards, and dug channels. The ruins around you follow the line of that old river.

Most historians link this site to a small oasis state that Chinese records of the Han period call Jingjue. It was one of many small kingdoms along the southern branch of the Silk Road. That route skirted the edge of the desert, moving from oasis to oasis. Caravans carried silk, horses, and other goods between China, Central Asia, and India. A town like this was a resting point. Travellers found water here, fodder for animals, and a market. Later, the area came under the control of a larger kingdom called Shanshan.

For a long time the sand covered the town and the outside world forgot it. Then, in 1901, the Hungarian-born British explorer Aurel Stein arrived. Local people had shown him written wooden tablets from the area. Stein followed the trail out into the dunes. What he found astonished him. Houses still stood with their wooden frames in place. Doorways, posts, and even furniture survived. The extreme dryness of the desert had preserved organic material that would rot away almost anywhere else.

Here is the first surprise. The most important finds at Niya were not gold or jewels. They were documents — hundreds of them, written on wood and on leather. Many are in a script called Kharoshthi. It came from the region of ancient Gandhara, in what is now Pakistan and Afghanistan. The language was a form of Prakrit, an Indian vernacular, used here for administration. So in a desert oasis in what is now western China, officials kept their records in a writing system from the Indian borderlands.

The documents themselves are wonderfully ordinary. They deal with taxes, disputes over land, the sale of a slave, a missing camel, orders about supplies. Some are letters between officials. They show a community that argued, traded, borrowed, and complained, much like any town. Many tablets were made in two fitted halves, like a wooden envelope. The text sat inside. A cord wrapped around the tablet, and a lump of clay sealed it shut. Breaking the seal was the only way to read the message.

And here is the second surprise. When Stein studied the clay seals, he found that some carried images in a Greek or Roman style. He described figures he identified with classical gods, including Athena. Seals with Chinese characters appeared at the same site. Think about that for a moment. On one small wooden letter in the desert, you could find Indian writing, a Chinese-influenced administration, and a classical Mediterranean image pressed into clay. The Silk Road was not one road. It was a meeting of worlds.

In the 1990s, a joint Chinese and Japanese expedition worked at Niya. Among their finds was a piece of brocade, a richly woven silk textile. Woven into it are Chinese characters that are usually translated as a phrase about five stars rising in the east and bringing benefit to China. Today it is regarded as one of the most celebrated textiles ever found in Xinjiang. It came from a burial, a reminder that the people here wore and valued fine silk woven far to the east.

Now look at what remains around you. The ruins are spread over a wide area, not packed into one compact walled town. Clusters of buildings sit along the old riverbed, separated by stretches of open sand. Many houses were built with timber frames. The walls were made of woven reeds or tamarisk branches, plastered with mud. The posts that still stand are mostly poplar wood. Sun and wind have stripped them silver-grey, and deep cracks run along their length.

Near the centre of the site stands the ruin of a stupa. A stupa is a Buddhist monument, usually a solid dome or tower that holds sacred relics. This one is built of sun-dried mud brick. Wind has worn its edges round and soft. It is a clear sign that Buddhism was present here, carried along the same routes as trade. For travellers crossing the desert, a stupa on the horizon would have marked the town long before they reached its houses.

Why did the people leave? Researchers generally connect the abandonment of Niya with the period around the fourth century. The most common explanation is water. As the river shortened, or as its flow changed, the fields and orchards could no longer survive. Political change in the region may also have played a part. What is certain is that the town was left behind. The sand moved in, filled the rooms, and quietly kept everything it covered for over a thousand years.

A practical word. Niya lies far out in the desert, well north of the modern town of Minfeng, which local people also call Niya. The site is protected, and access is tightly controlled. Visits generally need special permission and an organised trip with experienced local support. Conditions are hard. Heat, wind, and blowing sand can change quickly. If you are here, you are among a very small number of people who have stood in this place. Please keep to the paths your guides set, and touch nothing.

Before you leave, stand still again for a moment. Look at the nearest post, its grain split open by sun and time. Someone cut that tree, carried it here, and raised it as part of a home. Look along the low line where the river once ran, now only a band of darker ground. As you walk back toward your vehicle, notice how quickly the sand closes over your footprints... the same way it closed over this whole town, and kept it safe.

Practical tips

Travel only with an experienced, organised local desert team.
