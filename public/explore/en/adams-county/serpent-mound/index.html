The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the oak and hickory at the edge of the clearing. The grass is short under your feet, and the air smells of cut green and damp earth. Ahead of you, a low ridge of soil curves across the plateau. It rises and falls, bends back on itself, and runs on further than your eye expects. This is Serpent Mound, in the hills of Adams County, Ohio. Take a moment before you walk. You are looking at one of the largest earthen effigies of a snake anywhere in the world.

An effigy mound is a mound built in the shape of a living thing. Here, the shape is a serpent. The body stretches for well over four hundred metres if you follow every curve. It makes several wide, smooth bends, like a snake in motion. At one end the tail tightens into a coil. At the other end, the head opens toward an oval of earth. The mound itself is not tall. In most places it stands about the height of your knee or your waist. Its power comes from length and form, not height.

Here is the first surprise. You cannot really see the whole serpent from the ground. Standing beside it, you see a soft ridge and a curve or two. The full shape only appears from above. That raises a question people still ask today. Who was this made for? The builders could not fly. Perhaps they carried the full image in their minds as they worked. Perhaps the shape was meant for eyes that were not human at all. Nobody knows for certain.

Who built it is also an open question, and the honest answer is still debated. For a long time, scholars linked the serpent to the Adena culture. The Adena were people who lived in this region more than two thousand years ago and built burial mounds nearby. Then, in the 1990s, radiocarbon dates from charcoal in the mound pointed to a much later time. Those dates suggested the Fort Ancient culture, a farming people of roughly a thousand years ago. Later studies argued for the older date again.

So the serpent may be about two thousand years old, or about one thousand. Archaeologists continue to test soil and charcoal and to argue the evidence. Some suggest one group built it and a later group repaired or reshaped it. What is clear is that Native American people made it, by hand, without metal tools or draft animals. They carried earth in baskets, shaped it over a base of clay and stone, and created a form that has lasted for many centuries.

Now look at the ground beyond the mound itself. Here is the second surprise, and it comes from geology. This plateau sits on the edge of an unusual disturbed area in the bedrock, several kilometres across. Geologists have studied it for generations. Many now think it was caused by an ancient impact, perhaps a meteorite, hundreds of millions of years ago. Others proposed a deep underground explosion of gas. Either way, the rock layers here are broken and folded in ways that are rare in this part of Ohio.

Did the builders know they were standing on strange ground? They could not have known about meteorites in a modern sense. But they may have noticed the unusual hills, the broken rock, and the steep drop to the creek below. Some writers suggest this is why the site was chosen. That idea is attractive, but it is speculation. It is better to hold it as a possibility, not a fact. Still, it is worth thinking about as you stand here.

Walk toward the head of the serpent. The plateau ends here in a cliff above Ohio Brush Creek. Through the trees you may see the valley below. The oval shape near the head has many readings. Some people see an eye. Some see an egg held in the open jaws. Others see the sun. Researchers have also noted that the head points roughly toward the sunset at the summer solstice, the longest day of the year. Whether that alignment was intended is still debated.

The written record of the mound begins in the nineteenth century. In 1848, two researchers, Ephraim Squier and Edwin Davis, published a large survey of ancient earthworks in the Mississippi Valley. Their book included a drawing of the serpent. It brought the site to wide attention in the United States and abroad. At that time, much of the land around it was farmland. Plows had already flattened many other earthworks across Ohio, and the serpent was at risk too.

In the 1880s, a Harvard archaeologist named Frederic Ward Putnam came to the site. He was connected with the Peabody Museum at Harvard. Putnam excavated parts of the mound and nearby burial mounds. He also worked to protect the land. With money raised largely through supporters in Boston, the site was purchased and set aside as a park. Later, ownership passed to the state of Ohio. This is one of the earliest efforts in America to save an ancient earthwork on purpose.

What you see today is partly the result of that care. Putnam restored sections of the mound after his digging. Over time, other repairs followed. So the curves under your eyes are ancient in design, but some of the soil has been moved and replaced. That is normal for earthworks. Grass holds them together, and people keep the grass short. Look for the gentle edges of the ridge. They are soft because the mound has been tended, not because it is new.

As you walk the path that follows the body, notice the quiet. There are no carvings and no stone walls here. There is only earth, grass, and sky. The site is considered sacred by many Native American people today. Visitors are asked to stay on the path and off the mound itself. Walking with care protects the shape for the next visitors, and it shows respect. Small details matter here. Footprints on soft ground can wear down an earthwork faster than centuries of rain.

Before you go, find a spot where the curves are clear in front of you. Watch the light on the grass. In the morning and late afternoon, low sun throws a thin shadow along one side of the ridge, and the shape becomes easier to read. Listen again to the wind in the trees and the birds at the edge of the field. People stood on this same plateau long ago and saw this same body of earth. As you walk away, look back once. The serpent is still there, still moving across the hill.
