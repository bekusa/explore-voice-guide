The guide

The story

Put your hand flat on the rampart wall. The stone is warm, rough, and slightly damp with salt. Below you, the Indian Ocean breaks against black rocks, and the wind carries the smell of sea spray and frying spices. Somewhere behind you a bicycle bell rings. A crow calls from a frangipani tree. This is Galle Fort, a walled town on the southwest coast of Sri Lanka, and people still live, work, and pray inside its walls.

It helps to picture what this headland meant to sailors. Galle sits on a natural harbour, sheltered from the worst of the monsoon swells. For centuries before any European arrived, Arab, Persian, Indian, and Chinese traders stopped along this coast. They came for cinnamon, gems, and elephants. The harbour was useful long before it was fortified. The walls came later, with a new kind of visitor who wanted to control the sea routes.

The Portuguese came first, in the early sixteenth century. A popular story says their ships were blown off course by a storm and arrived here by accident. Treat that as a good tale rather than settled fact. What is clear is that the Portuguese built a modest fort on this peninsula. It guarded the landward side with earth and stone. Their work was small compared with what came next.

In 1640, the Dutch East India Company captured Galle after a siege. The Dutch company, known by its initials VOC, was one of the most powerful trading organisations of its age. Over the following decades, the Dutch rebuilt and expanded the defences. They enclosed almost the whole peninsula with thick granite and coral walls. Most of the ramparts you walk on today are their work, from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Notice how the walls change as you walk. Some stretches are rough and grey. Others carry patches of pale coral stone, cut from the reefs offshore. The bastions — the angled, arrow-shaped projections — have names like Sun, Moon, and Star. Their angles let defenders fire along the face of the wall, so no attacker could hide at its base. It is a design from European military engineering, transplanted to a tropical shore.

In 1796, the British took control of Galle without a major battle. Under British rule, the town kept its Dutch street plan but gained new buildings. Look for the Old Gate near the harbour side. On its outer face, you can see a British royal coat of arms. On the inner face, there is the older VOC monogram, carved by the Dutch. One gate, two empires, back to back.

Here is something many visitors miss. The streets inside the fort follow a grid, laid out by the Dutch. Beneath them runs an old drainage system. Local accounts say it was designed to flush with the tides, using seawater to clean the channels. You will not see the tunnels, but you can see the grates and gutters along the lanes. The grid itself is easy to feel underfoot as you walk.

Now look up at the lighthouse. Its white tower rises at the southeastern corner of the fort, close to the ocean. The current tower dates from the twentieth century, built by the British after an earlier lighthouse was lost to fire. On a clear evening its light sweeps over the reef. Nearby is Flag Rock, an old signal point. From here, lookouts once warned ships about the dangerous rocks below.

The second surprise is this. When the Indian Ocean tsunami struck in December 2004, it caused terrible destruction along this coast. The new town of Galle, just outside the walls, was badly hit, and many people died there. Inside the fort, damage was much lighter. The old ramparts, built to stop armies, helped hold back the water. People in Galle still speak about that day with great care.

Walk inland a few streets and the town becomes quiet and lived-in. The Dutch Reformed Church, built in the eighteenth century, has old gravestones set into its floor. Some carry skulls and Dutch inscriptions. Not far away, the white Meeran Mosque faces the sea. There is also an Anglican church and a Buddhist temple. Muslim, Christian, and Buddhist families have shared these lanes for generations.

The houses carry their own story. Many have deep verandas with wooden pillars, thick walls, and high ceilings that keep the rooms cool. Clay roof tiles overlap like fish scales. Today, many of these homes are cafes, galleries, and small shops selling lace, gems, and spices. Yet behind some doors, families still live as they always have. In 1988, UNESCO listed the old town and its fortifications as a World Heritage Site.

In the late afternoon, life moves to the ramparts. Families sit on the grass. Couples watch the sun drop into the sea. Schoolchildren in white uniforms play cricket on the open lawns, and just outside the walls is the Galle cricket ground. Near Flag Rock, young local men sometimes leap from the walls into the sea below, hoping for a small tip from watching visitors.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment on the wall. Feel the wind push against you, steady and warm. Listen to the waves, the call to prayer, the scooters in the lanes behind you. These stones have watched Portuguese sails, Dutch soldiers, British ships, and a tsunami. Today they hold up schoolchildren and kite flyers. As you walk away, look back once at the lighthouse — and notice how ordinary life carries on inside these old walls.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full rampart circuit in the late afternoon.
Carry water and a hat for the exposed walls.
Cover shoulders and knees when entering the mosque.
Explore the inner lanes on foot rather than by tuk-tuk.
Stay after sunset to watch the lighthouse beam start.
