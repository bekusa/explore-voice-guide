The guide

The story

Stand in the inner courtyard and look up. The walls rise on every side, pale and plain, rows of small windows climbing toward a steep slate roof. The space feels narrow, almost like the bottom of a well. Sound behaves strangely here. A footstep or a voice comes back from the stone a moment later. During the Second World War, hundreds of Allied officers walked this same cobbled square every day. Most of them were thinking about one thing: how to get out.

Colditz Castle sits on a rocky spur above the Zwickauer Mulde river, in the German state of Saxony. The small town gathers below it, with red roofs, a market square, and lanes that run down toward the water. From the river bank, the castle looks calm, even elegant. It is easy to forget, standing here, that this building became one of the most famous prisons of the twentieth century.

The story of the site is much older than the war. A fortress has stood on this rock since the Middle Ages, guarding the river crossing. Over the centuries it passed through the hands of Saxon rulers. In the sixteenth century, much of it was rebuilt in the Renaissance style, and it served as a residence and hunting lodge. Look for the decorated gables and doorways. They come from that era of comfort and display, long before bars appeared on the windows.

The castle then had a long and darker second life. In the nineteenth century, Saxony turned it into an institution for people with mental illness, and it served that purpose for about a hundred years. Records show that it was one of the larger asylums of its time in Germany. Later, the Nazi regime also used the buildings as a prison. So by 1939, these walls already had a long history of keeping people inside.

When the Second World War began, the German army converted the castle into a prisoner-of-war camp for officers. Its official name was Oflag IV-C. The word Oflag is short for the German term for an officers' camp. The idea was simple. Colditz would hold the so-called incorrigibles, the officers who had already escaped, or tried to escape, from other camps. The Germans believed the cliffs, the river, and the thick walls made the castle almost impossible to leave.

Here is the first surprise. By gathering the most determined escapers in one place, the guards created something they did not intend — a school for escape. Officers from Poland, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Britain and its Commonwealth shared skills here. They forged documents, sewed civilian clothes from blankets and uniforms, and made compasses and maps. Some prisoners built hidden workshops behind false walls. The castle, meant to stop escapes, became a centre of escape knowledge.

The attempts were famous for their creativity. A group of French officers spent many months digging a long tunnel from under the clock tower area. It passed through rock and old foundations, and the Germans discovered it before it was finished. Other prisoners tried disguises, climbing ropes made from bedsheets, and hiding places inside the walls. Dutch officers were especially successful, and several of them reached neutral Switzerland. Getting over the wall was only the first step. After that came hundreds of kilometres of enemy territory.

One British name stands out. Airey Neave, a young officer, became the first British prisoner to escape from Colditz and reach home. He left in early 1942, wearing a home-made copy of a German uniform, with help from a route that started near the prisoners' theatre. Another officer, Pat Reid, escaped the same year and later wrote The Colditz Story. His book, and the films and television series that followed, made the castle known around the world.

Now the second surprise, and perhaps the strangest story of all. Late in the war, British prisoners secretly built a glider in the attic, high under the roof. They made it from bed boards, floor timbers, and blue and white checked sleeping bags, stretched as skin over the wings. Their plan was to launch it from the roof and fly across the river. The war ended before it could be used. Decades later, a replica was built and flown, proving the idea could have worked.

Not every prisoner here was an escaper. The Germans also held a small group they called the Prominente. These were officers with family connections to powerful Allied figures, including a nephew of Winston Churchill. The Germans saw them as possible hostages for bargaining. The pilot Douglas Bader, famous for flying with two artificial legs, was also held at Colditz for a time. Life inside mixed boredom, hunger, music, theatre, sport in the courtyard, and constant planning.

In April 1945, American troops reached the town, and the camp was liberated. After the war, Colditz lay inside the Soviet zone, which later became East Germany. The castle served again as a hospital and as a care home. For decades, few Western visitors could come here. Since German reunification in 1990, restoration work has continued, and today the castle holds a museum about its prison years, as well as a youth hostel and other cultural spaces.

As you move through the museum, notice how small many of the objects are. A forged pass. A tiny hand-drawn map. A hiding place cut into wood. These items were made with very few tools and a great deal of patience. Many visitors also take a guided tour, which can open areas you would not otherwise see. Take time in the courtyard as well. It is the best place to imagine the daily roll calls, when guards counted every man again and again.

Before you leave, stand in the inner courtyard once more and look at the upper windows. Picture someone there in the dark, measuring the drop, timing the guards. Then walk down into the town and turn back toward the castle from the river. From below, it looks peaceful again — a white palace on a rock above the water. Both pictures are true. That is the quiet power of this place.
