The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the gravel and look up. The walls in front of you are covered in pale, rough harl, a lime render that softens every corner. Near the top, the plain tower suddenly bursts into turrets, corbels and little conical roofs. Listen, and you may hear rooks in the old trees and the crunch of other footsteps behind you. This is Crathes Castle, on the edge of Royal Deeside in Aberdeenshire. For more than four centuries, one family called it home.

That family was the Burnetts of Leys, and their story here begins long before the castle itself. In the early fourteenth century, King Robert the Bruce granted lands in this area to Alexander Burnard, an ancestor of the Burnetts. Bruce was the king who won Scottish independence in the wars against England. The grant made Burnard a royal forester, a keeper of the king's hunting land. As a symbol of that office, the family received an ivory horn. It became known as the Horn of Leys.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. That horn did not stay hidden in a strongbox. It became the centre of the family's identity, and it still hangs in the castle today. Look for it inside, in the great hall. It is a creamy, curved piece of ivory, banded with metal. You can also find a horn carved into the family's coat of arms. Once you know to look, you will notice it again and again around the house.

The Burnetts did not start out in this tower. Their earlier home was on a crannog, an artificial island built in a nearby loch called the Loch of Leys. Crannogs were an ancient Scottish and Irish way of living safely on the water. The loch has since been drained, but the move inland marks a shift. By the sixteenth century, the family was ready to build something more permanent and more ambitious on dry, higher ground.

Work on the castle began in the mid sixteenth century and took several decades to finish. That long building period was common in Scotland at the time. Money came and went. Generations changed. What rose here is a tower house, a tall, defensible home where the family lived on many floors stacked above each other. The lower walls are thick and quite plain. The decoration was saved for the top, where it could be seen from far away.

Walk around the outside and look carefully at that skyline. The round turrets sit on bands of carved stone that step outward, almost like upside-down staircases. These are corbels. In earlier centuries they supported real defences. By the time Crathes was built, they were mostly about display. This style is often called Scottish baronial, and it influenced later architecture across the country. Here, though, you are looking at the genuine article rather than a Victorian imitation.

Now for the second surprise. Some of the most important treasures at Crathes are above your head. Several rooms keep their original painted ceilings from around 1600. The wooden beams and boards are covered in bright figures, patterns and lettering. For centuries, ceilings like these were often plastered over or simply lost. At Crathes, they survived. They give a rare picture of how a wealthy Scottish home looked and felt around the time of the Reformation and after.

One room is called the Room of the Nine Nobles. Its ceiling shows nine great heroes from history and legend. They were a popular theme across medieval Europe and include figures such as King David, Alexander the Great and King Arthur. Another room, the Chamber of the Muses, shows the nine Muses, the Greek goddesses of the arts. Take your time in these rooms. Look at the painted verses and mottoes. They were meant to be read slowly, like a conversation with the past.

Then there is the Green Lady's Room, and its story is folklore rather than recorded history. Locals say a ghostly woman dressed in green has been seen here, sometimes crossing the room toward the fireplace. According to the most popular version of the tale, the bones of a young child were later found beneath the hearthstone. No one can say for certain what happened. But the room has a quiet weight to it, and many visitors pause there a little longer.

By the twentieth century, keeping a house like this was becoming difficult for any single family. In the middle of the century, the Burnetts gave Crathes to the National Trust for Scotland, the charity that cares for many of the country's historic places. That is why so much of the interior still feels lived in. Furniture, portraits and family belongings stayed together, instead of being scattered at auction. The house shows the layers of many generations, not just one moment.

When you step back outside, head toward the gardens. Thick yew hedges frame the walled garden. Some of these hedges date back to the early eighteenth century, so they are roughly three hundred years old. Over that time they have been clipped into dense, sculpted walls of dark green. Run your hand lightly along one and feel how solid it is. Inside, the space is divided into a series of separate garden rooms, each with its own colours and mood.

Much of the planting you see today was shaped in the twentieth century by Sir James Burnett and his wife, Lady Sybil. He collected unusual trees and shrubs. She designed much of the colour and structure of the borders. Look for the different themed areas, where single colours or particular plant types fill a whole section. In summer, the borders are full of colour and scent. In autumn, the light falls low across the hedges and the leaves turn copper and gold.

Beyond the house and gardens, the wider estate opens into woodland and walking trails. Take a few minutes to walk away from the castle and then turn back to look at it. From a distance, you see how the pale tower stands above the trees, exactly as its builders intended. Notice the turrets catching the light. Notice the horn of the Burnetts, still part of the story. And notice how quiet it becomes, just a short walk from the door.

Practical tips

Visit the house first, then save time for the gardens.
Wear sturdy shoes for the woodland trails around the estate.
Come in early summer for the fullest garden borders.
Walk back from the castle for the best whole view.
Allow extra time to read the painted ceiling inscriptions.
