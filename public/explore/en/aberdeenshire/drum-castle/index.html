The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through old oak branches, and somewhere a rook calls across the grass. In front of you, a square tower of grey granite rises above a softer, warmer house. The stone is rough and patched with lichen. On a damp day it darkens almost to slate. On a bright one it holds pale flecks that catch the light. Welcome to Drum Castle, in the green country of Royal Deeside, a short way west of Aberdeen.

Start with that tower, because it came first. The great square keep dates to the late thirteenth century. That makes it one of the oldest tower houses still standing in Scotland. A tower house is a tall stone home built for defence first and comfort second. The walls are enormously thick at the base. The windows are small. Everything about it says one thing — this was built by people who expected trouble.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. The original way into the tower was not at ground level. The entrance sat higher up, on the first floor, reached by a wooden stair or ladder. In danger, the family could pull that access away. The ground floor below was a dark, vaulted storeroom with no easy way in. An attacker faced a blank wall of granite and very little else to try.

Now the story that gives this place its name and its family. In 1323, King Robert the Bruce granted the lands of Drum to William de Irwyn. William had served the king as his armour-bearer and clerk during the long wars for Scottish independence. The gift was a reward for loyalty. It also began one of the longest family stories in Scotland. The Irvines of Drum would hold this estate for around 650 years.

As you walk, watch for holly. The holly leaf is the emblem of the Irvine family, and it appears in their coat of arms. Locals say the badge goes back to the Bruce himself. According to one popular tale, the king and his followers once sheltered under a holly tree, and he later gave its leaves to William as a symbol. It is a lovely story. Treat it as legend rather than record.

The Irvines did not live quietly. In 1411, a laird of Drum rode out to the Battle of Harlaw, north of Aberdeen. It was a brutal clash between the forces of the north-east and a Highland army. The laird was killed there. Legend has it that he fought a famous Highland chief in single combat, and that both men died of their wounds. The ballads tell it that way. The details belong to song as much as to history.

By the early seventeenth century, life in a cold stone keep no longer suited a family of rank. So in 1619, a later laird built a new house against the old tower. This is the long range you can see beside it — the Jacobean mansion. Jacobean simply means the style of the time of King James the Sixth of Scotland and First of England. The new wing brought larger windows, more rooms, and a softer, more domestic face.

Stand back and compare the two halves. The tower is plain, sheer, and almost windowless. The mansion has rows of windows, gables, and a calmer rhythm. Together they show a shift in Scottish life, from fortress to family home. Few places let you see that change so clearly in a single view. Later generations made further changes in the Victorian era, adding their own comforts and their own taste for the romantic past.

The seventeenth and eighteenth centuries tested the family again. The Irvines supported the Stuart kings, and the house suffered for it during the civil wars of the 1640s, when it was raided and plundered. A century later, the family backed the Jacobite cause — the movement to restore the exiled Stuart line. Locals tell of a laird who came home after the final Jacobite defeat and hid inside the house from government soldiers. Some versions say a secret room kept him safe.

Inside, one room surprises almost everyone. The old great hall of the medieval tower, once a space for feasting and guards, became a library in the nineteenth century. Picture it — a room inside walls built for war, now lined with books, portraits, and quiet chairs. The vaulted stone above stays from the original tower. It is a gentle joke of history. The most defensive part of the castle became its most peaceful room.

If you can, climb to the top of the tower. The battlements carry a wall walk, and the view opens wide across Deeside. You will see farmland, woodland, and the low hills that follow the River Dee towards the sea. On a clear day the landscape feels enormous and calm. Medieval watchmen stood exactly here, looking for riders on the roads below. Today the only movement may be sheep, clouds, and the shadows of passing birds.

The Irvine story at Drum ended in the twentieth century. The last laird passed the castle, its contents, and its grounds to the National Trust for Scotland in the 1970s. That is why so much survives together — the furniture, the portraits, the family papers. Outside, look for the Old Wood of Drum, an ancient oak woodland that is a remnant of a royal forest. Nearby, a walled garden holds a collection of historic roses, arranged to show how roses changed across the centuries.

Before you go, look once more at the meeting point between tower and mansion. One wall was built for siege. The other was built for family life. Between them stand seven centuries of one family, its wars, its loyalties, and its slow move towards peace. As you walk away under the oaks, listen again for the wind and the rooks. Notice the granite, the holly, and the light on the stone. Drum Castle has watched this valley for a very long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the tower for wide views across Deeside.
Wear sturdy shoes for the paths through the Old Wood.
Visit the walled rose garden in early summer for blooms.
Bring a waterproof layer, as Deeside weather changes quickly.
Allow extra time to walk the estate after the house.
