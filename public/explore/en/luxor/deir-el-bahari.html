The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The gravel crunches under your shoes, and then the sound simply stops against the cliff. Ahead of you, a wall of pale limestone rises straight out of the desert, golden where the sun touches it and grey-blue in the shadows. At its foot, three long terraces climb toward the rock. The whole temple sits so neatly against the mountain that it looks carved from it. This is Deir el-Bahari, on the west bank of the Nile, facing the old city of Thebes across the water.

The name itself is a clue to a much later chapter. Deir el-Bahari is Arabic, and it means roughly the Monastery of the North. Many centuries after the pharaohs, Coptic Christians, the native Christians of Egypt, built a monastery among these ruins. Local people kept the name long after the monks were gone. The ancient Egyptians called the main temple something else entirely. They called it Djeser-Djeseru, which translates as the Holiest of Holy Places.

That temple belonged to one of the most remarkable rulers Egypt ever had. Hatshepsut was a woman who took the full title of pharaoh in the fifteenth century before Christ. She was first a queen and then a regent for her young stepson, Thutmose the Third. Then she went further. She crowned herself king and ruled for around two decades. Her reign was largely peaceful and prosperous, and this temple was one of her grandest statements. It was built as a mortuary temple, a place where her memory would be honoured and offerings made after her death.

But here is the first surprise. Hatshepsut was not the first ruler to choose this spot. Look to your left, toward the south side of the bay in the cliff. You will see low, ruined terraces there. Those are the remains of an older temple, built roughly five hundred years earlier by the pharaoh Mentuhotep the Second. He reunited Egypt after a long period of division. Hatshepsut's architects clearly studied his design. Her terraces echo his, but on a larger and more confident scale.

The man most often linked with this building is Senenmut. He was Hatshepsut's chief steward and one of the most powerful officials at her court. Scholars widely credit him with a leading role in the temple's design. He seems to have known his own importance. Inside some of the upper chapels, small portraits of Senenmut were carved where the open shrine doors would hide them. It was a quiet way to place himself inside a sacred space, out of sight but always present.

Now look at the temple's shape. It is built in three levels, joined by long, gently sloping ramps that run straight up the centre. Each level is fronted by a colonnade, a row of square pillars that throws stripes of shadow across the walls behind. There are almost no curves here. The lines are clean and horizontal, repeating the layers in the cliff above. Many visitors say it feels surprisingly modern. That is no accident... the simplicity is the point.

As you climb the first ramp, imagine the forecourt green. Hatshepsut's inscriptions describe trees planted here, and archaeologists have found pits cut into the ground where trees once stood. Some of those trees were said to come from a faraway land the Egyptians called Punt. Nobody knows its exact location for certain, though most scholars place it somewhere along the southern Red Sea coast or the Horn of Africa. Punt was famous for incense, especially myrrh, the fragrant resin burned in temple rituals.

The expedition to Punt is carved on the middle terrace, and it is the second great surprise of this place. The reliefs show ships with tall sails and long oars. They show houses raised on stilts, fish swimming under the boats, and baskets of goods being carried aboard. One figure always draws attention: the ruler of Punt's wife, shown with a heavy, rounded body. She is drawn with unusual personal detail. It reads almost like a travel report in stone, written three and a half thousand years ago.

Nearby, another series of carvings tells a very different story. These scenes present Hatshepsut's divine birth. They show the god Amun as her true father, and they show her being shaped on a potter's wheel by the ram-headed god Khnum. For a woman claiming a king's throne, this was powerful political messaging. It said her right to rule came straight from the gods. Notice too how she is often shown in the traditional form of a male king, sometimes even with the ceremonial false beard.

Walk along the walls and you will see something unsettling. In many places, Hatshepsut's figure and her name have been cut away. Smooth, empty shapes remain where a ruler once stood. Much of this damage happened after her death, during the later reign of Thutmose the Third. Historians still debate his motives. Was it personal anger, or a careful effort to restore a neat line of male kings? The empty outlines are a reminder that history was edited, even here.

On either side of the middle terrace are two smaller chapels. One is dedicated to Anubis, the jackal-headed god of the dead and of embalming. The other is dedicated to Hathor, a goddess of love, music and joy, often linked with this western mountain. Look for her columns, topped with a woman's face and cow's ears, her sacred animal. In some areas inside, painted colour still survives. Deep blues, ochre yellows and reds appear in the shade, protected from the direct sun.

This bay in the cliff kept one more secret. In 1881, in a hidden shaft in the rocks close to the temple, officials uncovered a cache of royal mummies. Ancient priests had gathered them here to protect them from tomb robbers. Among them were some of the greatest names in Egyptian history, including Seti the First and Ramesses the Second. Today those kings rest in a museum in Cairo. Much of what you see now was also rebuilt over many decades, especially by a Polish and Egyptian restoration team that began work in the 1960s.

It also helps to know what lies just beyond these cliffs. On the other side of the mountain is the Valley of the Kings, where pharaohs were buried in tombs cut deep into the rock. A short distance away is Deir el-Medina, the village where the craftsmen who dug and decorated those tombs lived with their families. Old footpaths still cross the hills between these places. The workers who walked them every day would have known this temple as a familiar landmark on their route.

Before you go, turn your back to the cliff and look out across the valley. The ramps lead your eye straight down toward the green fields and the river beyond. That line was deliberate. It linked this temple with the great temple of Amun at Karnak, on the far bank. Now turn around once more. Notice how the terraces and the cliff seem to merge into one shape. As you walk back down, listen again for that silence against the rock. It has not changed in thirty-five centuries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early morning for cooler air and softer light.
Bring water, sunglasses and a hat for the exposed terraces.
Ride the small electric train from the visitor centre.
Walk the central ramp slowly to see all three levels.
Combine your visit with nearby Deir el-Medina and the Valley of the Kings.
