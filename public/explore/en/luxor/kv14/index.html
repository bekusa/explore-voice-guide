The guide

The story

Step out of the sun and into the doorway. The air changes almost at once. It is still warm, but it is still... and it smells faintly of dust and old stone. Underfoot, a modern walkway carries you down a long, gentle slope. Ahead of you, a corridor runs deep into the limestone of the Valley of the Kings. You are entering the tomb that archaeologists call KV14. The letters stand for King's Valley, and the number is simply its place in a long list.

This tomb has an unusual story. It was cut for one ruler and finished for another. The first owner was Twosret, a woman who ruled Egypt as pharaoh at the end of the Nineteenth Dynasty. The second owner was Setnakhte, the man who founded the Twentieth Dynasty after her. So as you walk, you are moving through two reigns, and through a quiet struggle over memory that is still visible on the walls.

First, a word about Twosret. She began her public life as a royal wife, the chief queen of the pharaoh Seti the Second. After his death, a young king named Siptah took the throne, and Twosret acted as his regent — a guardian who governs for a ruler too young or too weak to rule alone. When Siptah died, she did something rare. She took the full titles of a king for herself. Only a handful of women in three thousand years of Egyptian history did the same.

That change in status is written into this tomb. Work here seems to have begun while Twosret was still a queen. Royal wives were not usually buried in this valley, so even that first stage was a mark of high favour. Later, when she became pharaoh, the plan grew. The corridors were pushed deeper, and the decoration was expanded to suit a king rather than a queen. Look closely as you go, and you may see places where the walls were worked more than once.

Now to the surprise that most visitors miss. Twosret's reign was short, and the years around it were unsettled. When Setnakhte came to power, he needed a tomb quickly. Egyptologists believe he first started a tomb of his own nearby. But his workmen broke through into an older tomb by accident, and that project was abandoned. Instead of starting again, Setnakhte took over Twosret's tomb. A royal burial place, one of the most personal objects in Egyptian belief, was simply reassigned.

You can still see how he did it. In many scenes, the figure and names of Twosret were covered with plaster and painted over with Setnakhte's own names and image. Over the centuries, some of that plaster has fallen away. In places, the older layer shows through underneath. It is worth slowing down here. You are looking at an edit made more than three thousand years ago — one ruler written on top of another.

Let your eyes move along the walls. The colours are the colours of the New Kingdom tombs — deep reds, yellows, blues, and a great deal of black on a pale ground. The texts and images come from the sacred books that guided a dead king through the night. These books describe the twelve hours of darkness, when the sun god travels through the underworld before he rises again at dawn. The dead ruler hoped to join that journey and be reborn each morning with the sun.

This is why the tomb is shaped as it is. It is a long, straight descent, corridor after corridor, with small chambers along the way. Think of it less as a building and more as a road into the night. Each section of wall shows a stage of the voyage: gates guarded by serpents, gods in boats, rows of figures who are judged, protected, or punished. The visitor walking downward is following the same route the king was meant to take.

KV14 is also one of the longest tombs in the valley, and it has a rare feature. It contains two burial chambers. The first was prepared for Twosret. When Setnakhte took over, the tomb was extended further into the rock, and a second burial chamber was cut beyond the first. As you walk between them, notice how far you have come from the entrance. The light from outside has long since disappeared, and the sounds of the valley are gone.

The burial halls are wide spaces with pillars cut from the living rock. The ceilings in this part of the tomb were painted with astronomical images, part of the same vision of the night sky through which the sun travels. Stand still for a moment and look up. Then look at the walls around you, where gods receive the king and offer him life. These rooms were never meant to be seen by the living once the tomb was sealed.

What happened to the two rulers themselves is less certain. Like almost every tomb in this valley, KV14 was entered and robbed in ancient times. The original burial goods are gone. The mummy of Twosret has never been identified with confidence. Some objects and fragments connected to these burials have been found over the years, but much of the story of what was laid here, and when, remains open. Honest uncertainty is part of this place.

It helps to picture the valley as the workmen knew it. The tombs were cut by skilled crews who lived in a village on the other side of the hills. They worked by lamplight, chiselling the limestone, smoothing the walls, and then plastering and painting them. Above the valley rises a peak shaped roughly like a natural pyramid. Many scholars think that shape was one reason the kings chose this lonely place for their burials.

As you climb back toward the entrance, the corridor slowly brightens. The air grows warmer, and the noise of other visitors returns. Before you step out, glance once more at the walls and their layers of paint and plaster. Two rulers asked this tomb to carry their names into eternity. One name was covered by the other... and yet both are still here, side by side in the stone.

When you are back in the sunlight, turn around and look at the doorway. It is a plain opening in a pale cliff, much like the others around you. Nothing on the outside tells you about the long road inside, or the queen who became a king. Take that thought with you as you walk on through the valley. Every one of these dark doorways holds a story, and this one holds two.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check at the entrance which tombs are open today.
Come early morning before the valley heats up.
Bring water, since shade outside is very limited.
Ask staff about current photography rules before shooting.
Wear sturdy shoes for the long sloping walkway.
