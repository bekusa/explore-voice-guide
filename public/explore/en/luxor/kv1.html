The guide

The story

Feel the change as you step out of the sun. The air drops a few degrees, and the sharp glare of the valley turns to a soft, amber dimness. Underfoot, a gentle ramp leads down into the hill. Around you, the limestone has been cut smooth and painted. The voices of other visitors go quiet in here, as if the rock itself asks for a lower tone. Welcome to KV1, the tomb of Ramesses the Seventh.

That name, KV1, is worth a moment. KV stands for Kings' Valley. The number one does not mean this is the oldest tomb here, or the most important. It comes from a simple practical fact. In the 1820s, the British traveller John Gardner Wilkinson painted numbers above the tomb entrances to catalogue them. This tomb sits close to the valley entrance, so it was the first one he numbered.

That location also explains something else. Because it sits near the mouth of the valley and has stood open since ancient times, this tomb has had visitors for a very long time. It was not sealed and forgotten like the tomb of Tutankhamun. People have walked into this corridor for more than two thousand years. Some of them left their mark, and you can still find it on the walls.

So who was Ramesses the Seventh? He ruled Egypt during the Twentieth Dynasty, in the twelfth century before the Common Era. This was the late New Kingdom, the last great age of the pharaohs, when royal power was slowly weakening. He was most likely a son of Ramesses the Sixth. His reign was short — only a few years. History records little about him. But here, in his tomb, his name and image survive in bright colour.

That short reign shaped the space around you. Royal tombs in this valley were usually long, deep corridors, cut further and further into the rock as a king's reign continued. Workers dug and decorated at the same time. When a king died early, the work simply stopped. Here, the builders seem to have adapted a space that was planned as a corridor or hall, and turned it into the burial chamber. That is why this tomb feels compact.

The workers who cut it lived nearby, in the village now called Deir el-Medina, just over the hill. They were skilled craftsmen — stonecutters, plasterers, scribes and painters — and they passed their trades from father to son. Think of them coming over the ridge each morning with copper chisels and baskets. The smooth walls here, the clean outlines, the careful hieroglyphs... all of it came from their hands, working by the light of oil lamps.

Now look at the walls of the corridor as you walk. The paintings are not decorations in the modern sense. They are religious texts and images, meant to guide the king through the underworld. Egyptians believed the sun travelled through the darkness each night and was reborn each dawn. The king hoped to make the same journey. The scenes around you are a kind of map, showing the gates, the dangers and the gods along the way.

Notice the king himself. In several scenes, Ramesses the Seventh stands before the gods, offering to them or receiving their protection. Look for his cartouches — the oval rings that enclose a royal name. Inside them, the hieroglyphs spell out his names. For a ruler whom history barely remembers, this was his promise of permanence. The Egyptians believed that a written name helped a person to live on.

Here is a detail many visitors walk past. Look closely at the lower parts of the walls, near the entrance. You may see scratched letters that are not hieroglyphs at all. These are graffiti left by Greek and Roman travellers, and later by Coptic Christians — Egyptian Christians who sometimes lived in these tombs. Ancient tourists stood exactly where you stand. Some of them wrote their names and their opinions in the stone.

Continue down to the burial chamber. This is the heart of the tomb. In the floor, the builders set the king's stone sarcophagus — the outer coffin — directly into the rock. It is a quiet, heavy presence in the centre of the room. The king's mummy has never been found here. Like many royal tombs, this one was emptied long ago, and much of what it once held is lost.

Now look up. The ceiling of the burial chamber carries images of the sky and the heavens. For the Egyptians, the sky was a goddess, Nut, who arched her body over the earth and swallowed the sun each evening. Lying beneath her, the king would travel through her body in the night and rise again with the sun. Many visitors keep their eyes on the walls and never notice the ceiling at all.

Take a moment to appreciate the colour. Yellow ochre, deep red, black, white and touches of blue and green still hold to the plaster after more than three thousand years. The dry desert air has protected them. Today, conservation work and visitor rules help to keep them safe. Breath, humidity and touch all cause damage over time, so this small tomb is a fragile survivor, not only a monument.

That is the quiet surprise of KV1. It is small, unfinished and made for a king few people can name. Yet because of its size, you can see the whole tomb in one walk. You can follow the full idea of a royal burial — the descent, the guiding texts, the sky above the coffin — without getting lost in long corridors. In a way, this modest tomb is one of the clearest introductions to the valley.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in the burial chamber. Listen to the hush. Look once more at the king's painted face, at the faded stars, at the scratched names of travellers long gone. Then walk back up the ramp toward the light. Feel the heat return on your skin. Outside, the cliffs of the valley rise in pale gold. Hidden inside them are many more kings, each waiting in the dark.
