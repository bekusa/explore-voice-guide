The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the heat on your shoulders. The light here is hard and white. It bounces off pale limestone, off the low walls of mud brick, off the cliffs that close in around this small valley. There is very little shade. If you came in the morning, the sun has already climbed above the eastern hills across the Nile. That sun is where our story begins.

The name on your map is Aten. To the ancient Egyptians, the Aten was the disc of the sun itself, the visible circle of light in the sky. For most of Egyptian history it was one sacred image among many. Then, for a short and unusual period, one king placed it above all the other gods. To understand why that name echoes in a place like this, it helps to first understand where you are standing.

This is Deir el-Medina, on the west bank of the Nile, opposite modern Luxor. The Arabic name means roughly the monastery of the town, because Christian monks later used the old temple here. But in pharaonic times, the village had another identity. It was home to the craftsmen who cut and decorated the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings, just over the ridge behind you.

These were not slaves. They were skilled, paid specialists. Stonecutters, plasterers, draughtsmen, painters, sculptors. Their official title is often translated as Servants in the Place of Truth. The Place of Truth was their name for the royal necropolis, the great city of the dead where kings were buried. The workers lived here with their families, generation after generation, during the New Kingdom, the era of Egypt's greatest wealth and power.

Now, back to the sun. In the fourteenth century BCE, a king named Amenhotep the Fourth changed his name to Akhenaten, which means something like effective for the Aten. He promoted the sun disc as the central focus of worship. He built a new capital far to the north, at the site we now call Amarna. He turned the attention of the state away from Amun, the great god of Thebes, the very city whose dead lie in these hills.

Here is a detail many visitors never hear. At Amarna, the royal tomb builders also needed a home. Archaeologists found a walled workmen's village there too, laid out much like this one. So the story of the Aten and the story of Deir el-Medina run in parallel. Wherever a king chose to be buried, a community of craftsmen followed. After Akhenaten's death, the old gods returned, and royal burials came back to Thebes.

Look now at the outline of the village itself. You can still trace a single main street running along the valley floor. Small houses press tightly on both sides. Most homes had a series of rooms in a row, from the street toward the back. Archaeologists found niches for household shrines, low benches, and traces of kitchens with ovens at the rear. It was crowded, practical, and very human.

The second surprise lies in the rubbish. Near the village, excavators found enormous quantities of ostraca. An ostracon is a flake of limestone or a broken piece of pottery used as cheap writing paper. Papyrus was expensive. Stone chips were everywhere. So the villagers wrote on them. Letters, shopping lists, laundry records, love poems, school exercises, and quick sketches. Many of the workers, it seems, could read and write.

Some of these records are wonderfully ordinary. Scribes kept attendance lists for the tomb crews, with reasons for absence written beside the names. A man stayed home because he was ill. Another was stung by a scorpion. Another was brewing beer. Reading them, the distance of more than three thousand years seems to shrink. These are the worries of neighbours, colleagues, and families.

The village also holds one of the earliest recorded labour protests in history. In the twelfth century BCE, under King Ramesses the Third, the workers' grain rations arrived late again and again. The men put down their tools. They marched and sat down near the temples, demanding to be paid. A papyrus account describes the events. It is a reminder that this sacred work rested on very earthly needs: bread, beer, and fair wages.

Above the village, on the western slope, the craftsmen cut tombs for themselves. After a working life spent decorating the burial chambers of kings, they used the same skills for their own families. Small mud brick pyramids once stood above many of these tombs. Inside some chambers, the painted scenes remain bright. They show harvests, offerings, and the hope of a fertile afterlife. Notice how often the sun appears there, in its daily journey across the sky.

At the northern end of the site stands a stone temple from a much later period, the age of the Greek-speaking Ptolemaic kings. It is dedicated mainly to Hathor, a goddess of love, music, and joy, and to Maat, the principle of truth and cosmic order. Its walls are well preserved. Its presence shows that people still treated this valley as sacred long after the tomb builders had gone.

So what do we make of the name Aten here? Perhaps the simplest answer is above your head. Every person who lived in this village woke each day as the sun rose over the eastern hills. They walked to work in its light. They painted its journey on tomb walls. Whatever king ruled and whatever god he favoured, the sun disc was always present in their lives.

Before you walk on, look along the line of the main street once more. Picture doors opening, smoke rising from ovens, children calling out, and the steady tap of chisels in the distance. Then turn toward the ridge, where the path climbs toward the valley of kings. The same sun is on your face now. Take a slow breath of the dry air, and let the quiet of this place stay with you for a moment longer.

Practical tips

Visit early morning before the valley heats up.
Carry water and a hat; shade here is scarce.
Walk the main street lengthwise to sense the village layout.
Check which painted workers' tombs are open on your day.
Combine with the Valley of the Kings across the ridge.
