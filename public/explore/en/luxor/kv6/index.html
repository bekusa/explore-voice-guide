The guide

The story

Feel the change in air as you leave the sun behind. Outside, the light in the Valley of the Kings is white and hard, and the limestone throws heat back at your face. Step through the doorway of this tomb and the temperature drops. The sound changes too. Voices soften, footsteps echo, and the corridor slopes gently down ahead of you, lit by soft electric light on painted walls.

You are standing in KV6, the tomb of Ramesses IX. The letters KV simply mean Kings' Valley, and the number six comes from the order in which early explorers listed the tombs. This king ruled Egypt in the Twentieth Dynasty, near the end of the New Kingdom, during the twelfth century before Christ. He held the throne for close to two decades. That was a long reign for a troubled age.

Notice where this tomb sits. It lies close to the centre of the valley, near the main path, a short walk from the tomb of Ramesses the Sixth. Earlier kings often hid their tombs in remote side valleys, cut high into the cliffs. By this period, builders chose more open positions on the valley floor. The entrance is wide and easy to reach. Secrecy, it seems, mattered less than scale.

Here is the first surprise. This tomb has stood open since ancient times. Long before modern archaeology, Greek and Roman travellers walked down this same corridor, holding lamps. Some of them scratched their names and short messages into the walls. As you walk, keep an eye on the lower surfaces. Those faint scratches are a kind of ancient guest book, left by tourists about two thousand years ago.

Now look at the walls themselves. The Egyptians did not decorate a royal tomb for beauty alone. The images and texts were practical tools. They guided the dead king through the dangers of the underworld, so he could join the sun god and rise again each morning. The scenes near the entrance show the king standing before the gods, offering to them and receiving their protection.

As you move deeper, you meet long columns of hieroglyphs and rows of strange figures. Some come from the Litany of Ra, a hymn that names the many forms of the sun god. Others come from texts known as underworld books, with names such as the Book of Gates and the Book of Caverns. They describe the sun's journey through twelve hours of night, past gates, guardians, serpents, and fire.

Pause for a moment and look at the colours. Yellow ochre forms the background in many places. Figures are outlined in red and black, with touches of blue and green. The paint sits on a layer of plaster over the limestone. In some areas the plaster has cracked or fallen away, and you can see the pale stone underneath. That stone shows the work of chisels, cut by hand more than three thousand years ago.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a dramatic one. The reign of Ramesses IX is famous for a crime wave. Ancient papyri record official investigations into the robbery of royal tombs in western Thebes during his rule. Thieves were questioned and their confessions were written down. While workers cut this tomb for the king, others nearby were breaking into the tombs of earlier rulers.

Those trial records are among the most vivid documents from ancient Egypt. They name officials, accuse rivals, and describe stolen gold. They also show a state under pressure, with an economy in difficulty and a weakening central authority. So as you admire the painted gods around you, remember the tense world outside. The men who decorated these walls may have known some of the robbers personally.

Continue down the corridor and the tomb opens into a pillared hall. Look closely here. The finish is rougher, and the work seems less complete than at the entrance. Scholars generally believe the tomb was not finished as planned. When the king died, the builders likely worked quickly to make the burial chamber ready. Plans changed, and some spaces were left plain or only partly decorated.

Then you reach the burial chamber, the heart of the tomb. Raise your eyes to the ceiling. Here the decoration turns to the sky. Dark blue fields hold figures connected to the night and the heavens. The walls carry scenes from texts about the earth and the sun's rebirth. This room was meant to be the place where the king's body would begin its eternal journey toward the dawn.

And yet, the king is no longer here. Because of the tomb robberies of this age, priests later moved many royal mummies to safer hiding places. The mummy of Ramesses IX was found among a group of royal bodies in a hidden cache near Deir el-Bahari, in the late nineteenth century. Hidden together, those kings survived when their own tombs could not protect them.

Think about that for a moment. The texts on these walls promised the king a safe passage forever. In practice, his protection came from the quiet work of priests who carried him away in secret. The tomb you see now is an empty house, but a beautifully painted one. Its emptiness tells a story about faith, fear, and the practical choices of a worried kingdom.

As you turn back toward the entrance, walk slowly. Watch the rectangle of daylight grow brighter at the top of the slope. Feel the warm air return to your skin. Ancient priests, Roman visitors, and modern travellers have all made this same climb toward the light. Before you step outside, take one last look at the gods on the walls, still guarding a king who left long ago.
