The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The wind moves across pale limestone, and small stones shift under your feet. The cliffs behind you glow honey-gold in the sun. This dry, quiet valley on the west bank of the Nile was once full of chisels, gossip, arguments and children. You are standing in Deir el-Medina, the village of the people who built the royal tombs of ancient Egypt.

The Theban Necropolis is the great city of the dead that runs along the western side of the river, opposite modern Luxor. For the ancient Egyptians, the west was the land of the setting sun, and so the land of the dead. Royal tombs, noble tombs and memorial temples spread across these hills for many centuries. This small village is different, though. It was not built for the dead. It was built for the living people who served them.

The ancient name of this place was Set Maat, often translated as the Place of Truth. The workers called themselves Servants in the Place of Truth. They were stonecutters, plasterers, draughtsmen and painters. These were the skilled teams who cut and decorated the tombs in the Valley of the Kings. The village seems to have been founded early in the New Kingdom, around the fifteenth century before Christ. It stayed in use for roughly four hundred years.

Look at the low stone walls around you. These are the foundations of houses, packed tightly along one narrow main street. The village held around seventy homes inside its enclosure wall. Each house usually had a front room, a main living room with a raised platform, smaller rooms behind, and a kitchen at the back. Stairs often led up to the roof. In this heat, families probably slept and worked up there on summer nights.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. Ordinary people in ancient Egypt rarely left written records. But this village is different, because so many of its people could read and write. Archaeologists found thousands of ostraca here. An ostracon is a flake of limestone or a piece of broken pottery used as cheap writing material. Many of them came from one deep pit just north of the village. Together, they form one of the best records of daily life in the ancient world.

These stone notes are wonderfully human. There are laundry lists, letters, love poems, legal disputes and sketches. There are also attendance registers for the work crews. In these records, men are marked absent for very ordinary reasons — illness, a scorpion sting, a family funeral, and yes, brewing beer. The workers seem to have followed a ten-day week. They usually worked eight days in the royal valley, then rested for two days at home.

Now the second surprise. During the reign of Ramesses the Third, in the twelfth century before Christ, the grain rations for these workers arrived late, again and again. The men put down their tools and refused to work. They sat outside the temples and demanded payment. A papyrus now kept in Turin records these events. Historians often describe this as the earliest documented labour strike in history. It happened right here, among these families.

Turn toward the hillside to the west. Those cuts in the rock are the workers' own tombs. These men spent their days decorating graves for kings, and then they used the same skills for themselves. Many tombs had a small chapel above ground, often topped with a little pyramid of mudbrick. Below the chapel, a shaft led down into painted burial chambers. Some of these small pyramids have been rebuilt, so you can picture the original skyline.

The tomb of Sennedjem is one of the most famous here. He was a craftsman in the Nineteenth Dynasty, and his family tomb was found intact in 1886. Inside, the colours are still bright — warm yellows, deep reds and dark outlines. One scene shows Sennedjem and his wife ploughing and harvesting in the fields of the afterlife. The images show a craftsman's wish for paradise: rich crops, flowing water and peaceful work.

At the northern end of the site, you will see a temple with sandstone walls. It is much younger than the village. It was built in the Ptolemaic period, when Greek-speaking kings ruled Egypt after Alexander the Great. The temple honours Hathor, a goddess of love, music and protection, and Maat, the goddess of truth and order. Later, Christians used it as a monastery. The Arabic name Deir el-Medina means monastery of the town, and comes from that time.

Now lift your eyes above the cliffs. A peak shaped like a natural pyramid rises over the Valley of the Kings. Today it is called el-Qurn, the Horn. The villagers worshipped a cobra goddess linked to this mountain. Her name was Meretseger, which means She Who Loves Silence. People believed she watched over the tombs and punished wrongdoers. Some prayers found here ask her to forgive mistakes. Ancient people felt that this hill was alive with power.

The workers did not travel to the royal valley by road. They walked over the ridge on a mountain path. You can still see stretches of this track in the hills. Along the way, the crews had a small rest station of stone huts where they could sleep during the working week. Picture the men at dawn, climbing with water jars and tools. Then picture them coming down again at sunset, toward their homes and their families.

In the twentieth century, a French team led by Bernard Bruyère excavated much of the village over several decades. Their careful work explains why so much is known about these families today. Even so, scholars still study the ostraca and find new names, new disputes and new jokes. The village is not a finished story. It is still being read.

Before you leave, stand at the end of the main street and look along the line of walls. The stones are small and plain, but people lived real lives inside them. They argued about donkeys, waited for late wages and worried about their children. As you walk back toward the river, notice the cliffs behind you. The workers saw this same view every evening, on their way home.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning before the west bank heat builds.
Carry plenty of water for the dry, shadeless site.
Check whether Sennedjem's tomb needs a separate ticket.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone and steep tomb steps.
Respect the no-photography rules inside the painted tombs.
