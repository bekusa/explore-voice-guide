The guide

The story

Listen first. On most days this corner of the West Bank is quiet. You may hear a donkey cart on the road, a dog barking somewhere in the village, or the dry scrape of sand under your shoes. The light here is hard and white by midday. In the early morning, though, it turns the sandstone columns ahead of you a soft honey colour. This is the Mortuary Temple of Seti I, at the northern edge of the old Theban necropolis.

Most visitors drive straight past this place. They are on their way to the Valley of the Kings, just up the road into the hills. That is one of the first surprises here. The temple sits almost at the mouth of the route to the royal tombs. For a king who would be buried in those hills, the location made sense. This was where his memory would be fed, honoured and kept alive.

So who was Seti? He was a pharaoh of the Nineteenth Dynasty, ruling in the early thirteenth century before Christ. His father, Ramesses the First, reigned only briefly. Seti spent his years rebuilding Egypt's confidence. He led military campaigns abroad and sponsored building projects at home. Today he is often overshadowed by his son, Ramesses the Second. Yet many historians consider the art of Seti's reign among the finest Egypt ever produced.

A temple like this was not a tomb. The king's body lay elsewhere, in the hills behind you. Egyptians called these buildings Mansions of Millions of Years. Priests performed rituals here, made offerings, and spoke the king's name long after his death. The aim was simple — to keep his spirit sustained forever. This temple was also dedicated to Amun-Ra, the great god of Thebes, so the king and the god were honoured side by side.

Now look at what stands in front of you. The temple was once much larger. Great gateways, called pylons, and open courtyards stretched out ahead of the main building. Most of them have gone. In places you can still see low lines of mud brick and scattered stone. What survives best is the inner temple, fronted by a row of tall columns. Those columns are shaped like bundles of papyrus stems, tied together at the top.

Here is a detail many people miss. Seti did not live to see his temple finished. His son Ramesses the Second completed much of the decoration. As you walk inside, you will find both kings in the carved scenes. Look at the depth of the carving. Some reliefs are raised gently from the surface. Others are cut sharply down into the stone. Changes in technique like this often mark where one reign ended and another began.

Walk slowly through the central hall. On the walls, the king stands before the gods, offering incense, flowers and food. The figures are slender and calm. The faces have a quiet, almost gentle quality. Lean close and you may see faint traces of paint in protected corners. Once these walls would have been bright with colour. Now the stone shows the soft brown of age, but the fine lines remain.

The temple also carries a family story. On one side, there is a chapel connected with Seti's father, Ramesses the First. Because that king reigned so briefly, he had little time to build a monument of his own. Seti made space for him here. Think of it as a son making sure his father would not be forgotten. Ramesses the Second, in turn, placed himself in the same temple. Three generations share these walls.

The temple played a part in the religious calendar of Thebes. Each year, during a celebration known as the Beautiful Festival of the Valley, the image of Amun crossed the river from the great temples on the East Bank. The god visited the royal temples of the West Bank. Families also visited the tombs of their own dead, sharing food and music. Seti's temple, close to the river road, was part of this sacred landscape.

As for the king himself, his tomb lies in the Valley of the Kings. It is one of the longest and most richly decorated in the valley. The explorer Giovanni Belzoni entered it in 1817. Seti's mummy, however, was not there. In ancient times, priests moved many royal bodies to protect them from robbers. His mummy was found in 1881, hidden in a cache near Deir el-Bahari, with other kings of Egypt.

Not far south of here lies Deir el-Medina, the village of the craftsmen. These were the men who cut and painted the royal tombs. They lived with their families in small stone houses, worked in teams, and left behind a great many written notes on pottery and stone. The skills you see on these walls came from communities like theirs. Somewhere in the hands that shaped this relief was a family waiting for supper.

The temple has had a hard time over the centuries. Stone was taken for other buildings. Sand and rain worked on the walls. In the 1990s a flash flood swept through the area and caused damage. Archaeologists, including long-running German teams, have studied and restored parts of the site. The modern village of Qurna once crowded close around the hills here. Much of it has since been cleared, which changes the view around you.

Before you leave, stand for a moment in the shade of the columns. Look up at the carved ceiling blocks, then back towards the open ground where the courtyards once stood. Imagine priests walking that path with offerings, three thousand years ago. Then listen again — the cart, the wind, the quiet. As you walk away, turn once and see the columns against the hills. Seti's name is still here, carved in stone.
