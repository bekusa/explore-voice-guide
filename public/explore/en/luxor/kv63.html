The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the valley floor. The limestone around you is pale and bright, and the ground under your shoes is a mix of loose chips and fine white dust. Heat comes up from the path as well as down from the sky. Above the cliffs, a peak shaped like a natural pyramid rises over the valley. Somewhere very close to where you stand, under this packed rubble, a small chamber stayed sealed for more than three thousand years.

This is the Valley of the Kings, on the west bank of the Nile at Luxor. For about five centuries, Egypt's New Kingdom pharaohs cut their tombs into these hills. Archaeologists number each tomb with the letters KV, for Kings' Valley, followed by a number. Tutankhamun's tomb is KV62. For most of the twentieth century, people believed it would be the last great discovery here. That belief lasted until 2005.

That year, an American team led by the archaeologist Otto Schaden was working near the tomb of the pharaoh Amenmesse. They were clearing ancient debris, not hunting for treasure. Then they found something unexpected. It was the top of a vertical shaft, cut into the bedrock and filled with stone chips. Ancient workmen's huts had been built over it. The shaft had been hidden in plain sight, only a short walk from the most famous tomb in the world.

The team announced the discovery in early 2006. The new number was KV63, and it was the first new tomb found in the valley since Howard Carter opened Tutankhamun's tomb in 1922. Think about that gap. More than eighty years of excavation, cameras, and crowds... and yet this shaft had stayed sealed the whole time. Many visitors walk past this spot today without knowing the story at all.

The shaft dropped a few metres down to a single rough chamber. When the excavators looked inside, they did not see golden shrines or painted walls. They saw wooden coffins, standing and lying in the dark. Around them stood large clay storage jars, many of them still sealed. The room had no decoration. It looked less like a royal burial and more like a storeroom closed in a hurry.

Here is the first surprise. When the team opened the coffins, they found no mummies. Instead, the coffins and jars held the materials of mummification. There was natron, the natural salt that Egyptian embalmers used to dry a body. There were linen strips, pottery, and broken seals. Some jars held small pillows stuffed with plant material. For archaeologists, this was not a disappointment. It was a rare look at the working tools of an ancient funeral.

Termites had damaged several of the wooden coffins badly, so conservators worked slowly and carefully. One coffin still showed a painted face with dark eyes. Another was covered in black resin. Among the finds was also a small gilded coffin, made in the shape of a person but sized for a child. It too held no body. Its exact purpose is still discussed, and scholars have proposed different explanations.

So who was this chamber for? The style of the coffins and pottery points to the late Eighteenth Dynasty. That is the royal family line that includes Akhenaten and Tutankhamun. Some clay seal impressions carry a fragment of a name with the word Aten in it. The Aten was the sun disc that Akhenaten made the centre of state religion. A few researchers linked the fragment to Tutankhamun's queen, whose early name also included Aten. That link is a possibility, not a proven fact.

The second surprise is a debate that is still open. Was KV63 first cut as a tomb for a person, and later reused as a cache of embalming material? Or was it always intended as a storage pit? Embalmers treated their leftover materials as sacred, because those materials had touched the body of the dead. They could not simply throw them away. A sealed chamber close to a royal tomb would be a fitting place to keep them.

Now look at the ground around you again. Tutankhamun's tomb lies only a short distance away on this same valley floor. Two of the most important discoveries in Egyptology sit almost side by side. Both were covered by flood debris and later building rubble. Rainstorms are rare here, but when they come, water rushes down these slopes and carries stone with it. Over centuries, those flows buried entrances and helped keep secrets.

KV63 is not open to visitors. The shaft has been secured, and the objects were moved for conservation and study. So there is no chamber to enter here. What you can do is understand the shape of the valley. Notice how low the valley floor sits compared with the cliffs. Notice how the tomb entrances cluster where the rock meets the rubble. Ancient builders chose these spots carefully, and later debris filled the gaps between them.

The story of KV63 also changes how many people see this valley. For decades, the idea of an undiscovered tomb sounded like a fantasy. Then a careful team, clearing old huts and stone chips, found one. Since then, survey work in the valley has continued, using new tools and fresh questions. Archaeologists still argue about what else may lie under these slopes. Nobody can promise more finds... but nobody can rule them out.

Before you move on, listen to the valley. You may hear footsteps on gravel, guides calling to their groups, and the hum of the electric trams near the entrance. Look at the white dust on your shoes. It is the same kind of limestone rubble that once covered this shaft. As you walk toward Tutankhamun's tomb, remember that the ground beside the path was not empty. For more than three thousand years, it held a quiet room full of sealed jars, waiting.
