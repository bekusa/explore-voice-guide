The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel shifts under your shoes, and the sound carries off the pale limestone walls around you. The air is dry and still. Somewhere nearby, a guide calls out a tomb number, and a line of visitors moves toward a dark doorway. You are standing in the Valley of the Kings, on the west bank of the Nile at Luxor. And somewhere close to where you are now lies a small, plain opening that almost nobody stops to look at. It is called KV56. People who know its story call it the Gold Tomb.

First, a word about the name. Every tomb in this valley has a number, and the letters KV simply mean Kings' Valley. The numbers follow the order in which tombs were recorded by explorers, not the order in which they were built. So KV56 tells you that this was the fifty-sixth tomb on the list. It says nothing about who was buried here. And with KV56, that question is still not fully answered.

The tomb came to light in 1908. The excavator was Edward Ayrton, a young British archaeologist. He was working for Theodore Davis, a wealthy American lawyer who paid for years of digging in this valley. Davis held the permit, and Ayrton did much of the work in the field. Together, their team found several tombs in the early twentieth century. Some were grand. This one was not — at least, not from the outside.

KV56 is a simple shaft tomb. A vertical pit drops into the bedrock and opens into a single chamber cut from the stone. There are no long painted corridors here, no pillared halls, no great stone sarcophagus. Compare that with the royal tombs you may have already walked through today, with their walls of hieroglyphs and their deep descending passages. This is a modest space. Ancient Egyptians often cut shafts like this for family members, officials, or for storing objects.

When Ayrton's team opened the chamber, they found it choked with debris. Over the centuries, rare but violent rainstorms had swept through the valley. Floodwater carried mud and stone into low-lying tombs, and then the water dried and the mud hardened. That hardened layer filled much of KV56. The team had to dig through it slowly. And inside that dried mud, they began to find gold.

This is the first surprise. In a valley famous for tombs stripped bare by ancient robbers, a small pit gave up a collection of fine jewellery. The finds included gold earrings, rings, bracelets, and other small ornaments. There were pieces in silver as well. They were not in neat boxes. They were scattered through the dried mud, as if a flood had broken open their containers long ago and moved them around. That is how the place earned its nickname.

Then came the clues. Several of the objects carried royal names. The names belonged to Seti the Second, a king of the Nineteenth Dynasty, and to Tausret, a royal woman of the same period. Tausret was his queen. Later, she went further. She ruled Egypt herself as pharaoh for a short time near the end of that dynasty. Only a handful of women ever held that title, which makes her name on these objects especially interesting.

So who was buried in KV56? Here we must be careful, because the evidence is thin. Some scholars have suggested the tomb held a child of Seti the Second and Tausret. The small size of some jewellery seemed to support that idea. Others think the chamber may have been a cache — a store for valuable objects rather than a burial. No body was identified with certainty. The honest answer is that the question is still open.

Here is a second detail many visitors miss. Davis published his account of this tomb together with two others found in the same period. The book was titled The Tomb of Siphtah, the Monkey Tomb and the Gold Tomb. That title shows how informal the early nicknames were. Excavators named tombs after what struck them most. One tomb got its name from animal burials. This one got its name from what glittered in the mud.

The jewellery did not stay in the valley. Like many finds from this period, the objects went to the Egyptian Museum in Cairo. If you travel north later in your trip, you may be able to see Tausret's world up close there. Here in Luxor, the chamber itself is not part of the regular visitor route. Many tombs in the valley are closed to protect them, and KV56 is one of the many you will not enter.

That is not a loss. Look around you instead. The valley floor is made of broken limestone and packed sand. Above it rise steep cliffs of the same pale rock, layered and cracked by heat. If you look up toward the highest point, you will see a peak shaped like a natural pyramid. It is called el-Qurn, which means the Horn. Many historians believe its shape helped draw the kings of the New Kingdom to bury their dead beneath it.

Now think about that flood once more. This valley looks like a place where water never falls. Yet the mud that sealed KV56 is direct proof that storms did reach here. Those floods damaged many tombs and filled corridors with debris. They also protected some objects by burying them. In this case, the same water that ruined the chamber kept its gold hidden from robbers for more than three thousand years.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Feel the heat coming up from the stone under your feet. Look at the small, unmarked openings along the base of the cliffs. Each one is a number on a list, and each one has its own story. Some held kings. Some held almost nothing. And one small pit, easy to walk past, held earrings and rings that carry the names of a king and a queen who became pharaoh. As you walk on toward the next doorway, keep that in mind.

Before you go

Practical tips

Do not expect to enter; KV56 is closed to visitors.
Visit early morning before valley heat reflects off limestone.
Bring water and a hat for the open valley floor.
Plan a Cairo museum visit to see the jewellery.
Ask your ticket office which tombs are open today.
