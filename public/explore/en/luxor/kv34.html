The guide

The story

The air changes first. Outside, the Valley of the Kings is bright and dry, with pale limestone cliffs and the crunch of gravel underfoot. Then you climb. A steep metal staircase rises into a narrow cleft in the rock, and the sun drops away behind you. The steps ring a little under each shoe. By the time you reach the doorway, the noise of the valley floor has faded, and the cool, still air of the mountain closes around you.

This is KV34, the tomb of Thutmose the Third. The letters KV simply mean King's Valley, and the number records the order in which tombs here were catalogued. Thutmose the Third ruled Egypt in the fifteenth century BCE, during the Eighteenth Dynasty. That was the period when Egypt reached the height of its power. Many historians see him as one of the greatest military rulers the country ever produced, a king who led campaign after campaign deep into the Near East.

His path to power was not simple. He came to the throne as a young boy. For many years his stepmother and aunt, Hatshepsut, ruled beside him and then as pharaoh in her own right. Only after her death did Thutmose rule alone. Later in his reign, many of her images and names were removed from monuments. Scholars still debate why this happened, and when. It is one of the quiet puzzles that hang over this king.

Now look at where the tomb is placed. Most visitors do not think about this, but it matters. The builders chose a high, hidden crack in the cliff, far above the valley floor. The idea was protection. Water from rare desert storms, and robbers looking for gold, were both real dangers. A tomb set high in a cleft was harder to find and harder to flood. The steep climb you just made was part of the plan.

Inside, a sloping corridor carries you downward. Soon you reach a deep shaft cut into the floor, now crossed by a modern walkway. Egyptologists have several ideas about these shafts. One is practical: the shaft could catch floodwater and slow down thieves. Another is religious: it may have marked a symbolic entrance to the underworld. Both ideas can be true at once. Ancient Egyptian builders often joined the useful and the sacred in a single feature.

Beyond the shaft comes a pillared hall, and here the first surprise appears. The walls are covered with rows of small figures, almost like a catalogue. These are gods and spirits of the underworld, listed one by one with their names. Egyptologists count several hundred of them, often given as more than seven hundred. Think of it as a register of every power the king might meet on his journey after death. Nothing was left to chance.

Then you step down into the burial chamber, and its shape is the second surprise. The room is not a simple rectangle. Its corners are rounded into a long oval. This shape echoes a cartouche, the oval ring that Egyptians drew around a king's name to protect it. In other words, the whole room works like a giant name ring. The king's body was placed inside his own protected name.

Look at the walls of this chamber. They look less like carved stone and more like a huge sheet of papyrus, unrolled and pinned to the rock. The background is a soft cream colour. The figures are drawn in simple black and red lines, many of them as thin stick figures. The text is written in a cursive script. Scholars believe the painters were copying a papyrus scroll, perhaps deliberately keeping its plain, hand-drawn style.

The text on these walls is called the Amduat, an ancient Egyptian word usually translated as That Which Is in the Underworld. It describes the sun god's journey through the twelve hours of night. Each night the sun sails through darkness, faces dangers, and is reborn at dawn. The king hoped to travel with the sun and rise again with it. This tomb holds one of the earliest complete versions of the Amduat known in any royal tomb.

Look up. The ceiling is painted deep blue and scattered with yellow stars. For the Egyptians, the night sky and the underworld were closely connected. Then look for the pillars in the chamber. On one of them, the king is shown being nursed by a goddess in the form of a tree. This image is very rare. Nearby are figures of women from the king's family, a small personal note in a room full of gods.

At the far end of the chamber stands the sarcophagus, carved from a hard reddish-yellow stone called quartzite. Its shape also follows the oval of a cartouche, repeating the design of the room around it. But the king is not here. Like many royal tombs, this one was robbed in ancient times. The mummy of Thutmose the Third was later found far away, hidden by priests with other royal bodies in a secret cache near Deir el-Bahari.

The modern discovery came in 1898, when the French Egyptologist Victor Loret entered the tomb. By then, it had already been emptied of most of its treasures. What remained was the architecture, the sarcophagus, and these painted walls. In a way, that is a gift. Without gold to distract the eye, you can see clearly what the builders cared about most: the words, the gods, and the long journey through the night.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the oval walls. Notice the warm cream of the painted surface, the thin lines of the figures, the hum of other visitors' voices in the narrow space. Then walk back up the corridor toward the light. As you climb down the metal stairs into the open valley, look back at the cleft in the cliff. It is easy to miss. That was the whole idea.
