The guide

The story

The air changes as you leave the sunlight behind. Outside, the Valley of the Kings is bleached limestone, dust, and glare. A few steps down the entrance corridor, the light softens and the temperature drops. Then the colour arrives... deep blues, warm ochres, crisp whites, and black outlines so sharp they look freshly painted. You are walking into the longest tomb in this valley, cut deep into the rock of Luxor's west bank.

This is the resting place of Seti the First, a pharaoh of Egypt's Nineteenth Dynasty. He ruled more than three thousand years ago, in the thirteenth century before Christ. History often remembers him as the father of Ramesses the Second, the great builder-king. But Seti was a powerful ruler in his own right. He led military campaigns, restored temples across Egypt, and commissioned some of the finest relief carving the country ever produced. This tomb shows that ambition on every wall.

Egyptologists know the tomb by a simple code — KV17. The letters stand for "Kings' Valley", and the number marks its place in the order of discovery. Many visitors also hear an older nickname: Belzoni's Tomb. That name honours the man who found it in October 1817. Giovanni Battista Belzoni was an Italian adventurer, a former circus strongman, and a remarkably skilled excavator. His story is part of this tomb's story.

Belzoni noticed signs of a hidden entrance at the foot of the cliff. When his workers opened the passage, they found something astonishing. The painted walls were almost untouched by time. Robbers had entered in antiquity and the king's body was gone. But the decoration itself had survived in extraordinary condition. Belzoni named one of the chambers the Hall of Beauties, and as you walk through, it is easy to understand why.

Look closely at the walls as you descend. Much of the decoration is carved in raised relief, then painted. The figures stand slightly proud of the surface, so they catch the light. In some areas the work was never finished. There, you can see the artists' preliminary drawings in red and black ink, with corrections still visible. It is one of the rare places where you can watch an ancient workshop at different stages of the same job.

The images are not simple decoration. They come from sacred texts that guided the king through the night. Ancient Egyptians believed the sun travelled through a dark underworld every night before rising again at dawn. The dead king hoped to join that journey. Texts such as the Litany of Ra, the Amduat, and the Book of Gates map that journey hour by hour. The Amduat means, roughly, "what is in the underworld".

As you go deeper, watch for the serpents. Huge snakes curl along the walls, guarding gates and doorways. Strange gods with animal heads stand in rows. Boats carry the sun god through dark waters. Small figures of the condemned appear too. To the original artists, none of this was fantasy. It was a practical guide, written in images, to help the king survive the most dangerous journey he would ever make.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The tomb does not run in one straight line. It slopes down through a series of corridors and halls, with steps, ramps, and a shaft along the way. That shaft had a practical role. It may have caught rainwater from rare flash floods, and it may also have slowed down thieves. It also carried a religious meaning, linked to the waters of the underworld.

Now raise your eyes in the burial chamber. The vaulted ceiling is painted dark blue and filled with figures. These are the constellations as the Egyptians understood them — stars shown as gods, animals, and human forms. It is one of the earliest surviving astronomical ceilings of its kind. The king's body once lay beneath this painted night sky, surrounded by the stars he hoped to join.

The king's coffin is no longer here. Belzoni removed a magnificent sarcophagus carved from translucent white calcite, a stone often called Egyptian alabaster. Its surfaces are covered with fine carved figures and texts. Today it stands in a small museum in London, the Sir John Soane's Museum. Belzoni also made casts and drawings of these walls. In 1821 he showed a full reconstruction of the tomb in London, and crowds came to see it.

And the king himself? His mummy was not lost forever. In 1881, Seti's body was found among a hidden cache of royal mummies at Deir el-Bahari, not far from here. Ancient priests had moved many kings to that secret place to protect them from robbers. Seti's mummy is famous for its calm, well-preserved face. It now rests in Cairo, at the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization.

Here is a second surprise. Beyond the burial chamber, a long tunnel runs further into the mountain. Its purpose is still debated. Excavations in the twentieth century and again in the early twenty-first century followed it far into the rock, but it was never finished. Some scholars think it was meant to reach groundwater, a symbol of the primeval waters of creation. Others are less sure. The mystery remains open.

The tomb's beauty has also made it fragile. In the nineteenth century, scholars cut panels from the walls and took them to museums in Europe. Visitors' breath, humidity, and earlier flooding caused further damage over the decades. For long periods the tomb was closed entirely. Today, protection matters as much as access. Modern teams have scanned the walls in high detail, so that faithful copies can be studied and shared without harming the original.

Before you climb back toward the light, pause for a moment. Listen to how quiet it is down here. Look again at a single figure — the line of a shoulder, the curve of a hand. A painter stood exactly where you stand, working by lamplight, more than three thousand years ago. When you step out into the valley again, notice the brightness of the sun on the cliffs. It is the same sun this king hoped to follow forever.

Before you go

Practical tips

Buy the separate tomb ticket before entering the valley.
Arrive early to share the corridors with fewer people.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep ramps and steps.
Bring water, since the descent and climb are tiring.
Never touch the walls, as skin oils damage the paint.
