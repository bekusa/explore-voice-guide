The guide

The story

You hear the river before you reach the bridge. The Reuss runs fast and clear, a pale green under the daylight. Wooden planks shift and creak beneath your shoes. A steep roof of dark timber rises overhead and runs the whole length of the crossing. In summer, flowers hang in boxes from the outer rails. This is the Chapel Bridge, the old covered walkway of Lucerne.

The bridge crosses the water at a slight angle and joins the two halves of the old town. People have passed this way for hundreds of years. Merchants carried their goods across. Guards kept watch here. Pilgrims and travelers walked through on their way out of the city. The timber is smooth and dark, polished by countless hands and feet. In places the boards dip a little, worn low by the weight of years.

The first bridge was built here in the fourteenth century. It was not made for beauty. It was part of the town wall. Lucerne was a fortified city then, closed behind stone and water. The covered bridge linked the defenses along the river. It guarded the town on the side that faced the lake. Towers and walls once ringed the whole of Lucerne, and a few of them still stand today.

The bridge takes its name from a small chapel on the bank nearby. The bridge once reached farther across the water than it does today. Part of it was removed long ago, when the riverbank was built up. Even so, it remains one of the longest wooden bridges of its kind in Europe.

Look up as you walk. Under the ridge of the roof, paintings are set into triangular frames. They follow you, panel by panel, the whole way across. There were once more than one hundred of them. They were added in the seventeenth century, long after the bridge itself was built.

The paintings were made to be understood by everyone, even those who could not read. They show the story of Lucerne and the lives of its patron saints. Battles, processions, and miracles pass above your head. Some panels still glow with color. Others have gone dark with age and smoke. Move slowly, and let your eyes travel from one scene to the next.

Near the middle of the river, the bridge meets a tall stone tower. It stands on an eight-sided base, rooted in the current. This is the Water Tower, and it is older than the bridge beside it. Over the centuries it has been a prison, a treasury, and a store for the town's records.

Together, the bridge and the tower became the picture of Lucerne. They appear on postcards and paintings around the world. Yet both began as plain, useful things. A wall to keep the city safe. A tower to guard and to hold what mattered. Their beauty was never planned. It grew slowly, with time.

On a night in 1993, fire broke out on the bridge. The old timber was dry, and the flames ran fast beneath the covered roof. Most of the bridge burned. Many paintings were destroyed, and others were badly scorched. The people of Lucerne gathered by the water and watched part of their city burn.

The city chose to rebuild at once. Within a single year, the bridge stood again across the Reuss. Carpenters followed the old plans and the old methods. Some paintings had survived, and these were cleaned and repaired. Others were replaced with copies. A few frames were left empty on purpose, a quiet mark of what was lost.

So the bridge beneath your feet is both ancient and new. Its first builders worked here almost seven hundred years ago. Some of its planks are younger than the travelers walking beside you. The bridge carries its long past and its recent wound at the same time.

Pause for a while at the rail. Look down the river toward the open lake. The tower stands firm in the moving water. Behind the rooftops, the mountains rise pale and high. Swans turn slowly below. At night, soft lamps light the walkway, and the roof glows above its own reflection. It is a fine hour to cross alone, when the day has emptied out.

The Chapel Bridge is not tall or grand. It is low, wooden, and simple. Yet it has carried a city's ordinary days and survived its hardest night. As you step off the far end, the planks fall quiet behind you. The river moves on toward the lake, the way it always has.
