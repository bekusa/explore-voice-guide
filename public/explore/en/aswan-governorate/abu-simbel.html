The guide

The story

Before you look up, notice the light. Out here in the far south of Egypt, the sun comes off the water of Lake Nasser hard and white. The sand under your feet is pale and fine, and the air is dry enough to feel on your lips. Then the stone face of the mountain comes into view... and four enormous seated kings look straight past you, toward the east, as they have for more than three thousand years.

This is Abu Simbel. Two temples were cut into the living rock here in the thirteenth century before Christ, under the pharaoh Ramesses the Second. He ruled for decades and built across all of Egypt. But this place was different. It stood deep in Nubia, the land to the south that Egypt controlled and traded with. Anyone sailing north on the Nile would have seen these giants first. The message was simple. This is Egyptian power, and it starts here.

Look at the four colossi on the facade of the Great Temple. Each one shows Ramesses seated on a throne, hands on his knees, wearing the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt. Each figure is roughly twenty metres tall. That is about the height of a six-storey building. They were not assembled from blocks. Workers carved them directly out of the sandstone cliff, removing everything that was not the king.

Now look at the second statue from the left. Its head and upper body are missing. They lie on the ground at its feet. An earthquake damaged the statue not long after the temple was finished, in ancient times. The Egyptians repaired other parts of the facade, but the fallen head stayed where it fell. Modern engineers made the same choice. They left the broken colossus exactly as the centuries left it.

Here is a detail many visitors walk past. Low down, around the legs of the colossi, stand much smaller figures. These are members of the royal family, including the king's mother, his principal wife Nefertari, and some of his children. They reach only to his knees. Above the doorway, in a niche, stands the falcon-headed sun god Ra-Horakhty. Along the very top of the facade runs a line of carved baboons, arms raised. In Egyptian thought, baboons greeted the rising sun each morning.

And now the first surprise. On the leg of one of the southern colossi, you can find scratched writing. It is not hieroglyphs. It is ancient Greek. Mercenary soldiers who marched south with an Egyptian army in the sixth century before Christ carved their names and a short account of their campaign into the stone. In other words, this temple was already an old monument when these men passed by. They behaved like tourists, and left their mark.

Step inside the Great Temple, and the temperature drops. The first hall is lined with eight pillars, each fronted by a standing statue of Ramesses in the form of Osiris, the god of the afterlife. The walls are covered with reliefs. The most famous show the Battle of Kadesh, fought against the Hittites in what is now Syria. Ramesses presented it as a great personal victory. Historians today read it as closer to a draw.

Walk deeper, through smaller rooms, and the ceiling gets lower. The temple runs straight back into the mountain toward its innermost sanctuary. There, four seated gods sit side by side. They are Ptah, Amun-Ra, Ra-Horakhty, and Ramesses himself, shown as a god among gods. This is where the builders placed their most remarkable idea... one that depends entirely on the sun.

Twice a year, around late February and late October, the rising sun shines straight through the temple entrance. The beam travels the full length of the halls and reaches the sanctuary. It lights up the statues of Amun-Ra, Ra-Horakhty, and the king. But the fourth figure, Ptah, stays in shadow. Ptah was linked with the underworld, so this may have been intentional. If you are here in these weeks, you may see crowds gathering before dawn for exactly this moment.

The second surprise is what you are standing on. This is not the original site. In the 1960s, Egypt built the Aswan High Dam, and the rising waters of Lake Nasser threatened to drown both temples. An international rescue campaign, led by UNESCO, took on the job. Between 1964 and 1968, engineers cut the temples into more than a thousand large blocks, lifted them, and rebuilt them on higher ground, about sixty-five metres above the original level.

The mountain behind the facade is also new. The engineers built an artificial hill over a huge concrete dome, then covered it with rock and sand so it looks natural. They also aligned the temple carefully to keep the solar event working. It still happens, though experts generally say the dates shifted slightly after the move. Look at the facade closely, and you may spot faint lines where the blocks meet. They are the seams of a twentieth-century rescue.

Next door stands the Small Temple, dedicated to the goddess Hathor and to Nefertari. Its facade has six standing statues. Four show Ramesses, and two show his queen. Here is the detail that matters. Nefertari is carved at the same scale as the king. In Egyptian royal art, queens were usually shown much smaller. Inside, scenes show her making offerings and being crowned by goddesses. Few women in ancient Egypt received an honour like this in stone.

Before Europeans wrote about it, sand had covered much of the Great Temple. The Swiss traveller Johann Ludwig Burckhardt saw the upper parts of the colossi in 1813. A few years later, in 1817, the Italian explorer Giovanni Belzoni cleared enough sand to enter. Local people in Nubia had long known the place. The name Abu Simbel itself has several folk explanations. Locals tell different stories about it, so treat any single version as legend.

Before you leave, turn around and face the lake. This is the view the colossi have now — a wide sheet of blue water where a river valley once was. Somewhere beneath that surface lies the original site. Then look back at the four kings one more time. Notice the broken one, still waiting beside its fallen head. As you walk away, listen to the quiet. It is the same desert silence these stones have heard since Ramesses first ordered them cut.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to see the facade before the heat builds.
Bring a hat, sunscreen and plenty of water.
Visit both temples; the smaller one rewards slow viewing.
Stand near the lake shore for the full facade view.
Book well ahead if visiting during the solar alignment.
