The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, water is moving. It is the Tagus, Spain's longest river, sliding past the palace walls on its way west toward Portugal. In front of you, the façade alternates warm red brick with pale, cream-white stone. On a sunny day the two colours seem to glow against each other. Under your feet is gravel, and it crunches softly. This is the Royal Palace of Aranjuez, and for centuries the Spanish court came here to breathe.

Why here, on this flat plain south of Madrid? The answer is water. The Tagus and the Jarama rivers meet near this spot. That gave the Spanish kings something rare in the dry centre of the country: shade, green meadows, orchards, and cool air in the hot months. Long before the palace stood here, the land belonged to a powerful medieval military order, the Order of Santiago. Later the Crown took it over as a royal estate for hunting and leisure.

The palace you see began in the sixteenth century under King Philip II. He is the same king who built the vast monastery of El Escorial in the mountains northwest of Madrid. Philip gave the work to two architects, Juan Bautista de Toledo and Juan de Herrera. Herrera's style is sober, geometric, and disciplined. Look at the steady rhythm of the windows and the clean lines of the corners. That calm order is his signature, and it was copied across Spain for generations.

Here is the first surprise. Philip II never saw this building finished. Construction ran on and off for nearly two centuries. Money ran short, priorities changed, and fire damaged the work more than once. The palace was completed in the eighteenth century under the Bourbon kings, a French-born dynasty that took the Spanish throne after 1700. Ferdinand the Sixth pushed the main body to completion. Then Charles the Third added the two long wings that reach forward and frame the great courtyard in front of you.

The court did not live here all year. Aranjuez was a seasonal residence. The kings and queens usually arrived in spring and stayed into early summer, when the gardens were at their best. These stays were called jornadas, which simply means royal journeys or seasons. During those weeks the whole court moved here: ministers, servants, musicians, and diplomats. So this small town became, for a short time each year, the political heart of Spain.

Inside, the rooms follow one another in a long line of state apartments. Many visitors remember one room above all others: the Porcelain Room. It was made for Charles the Third by the royal porcelain factory of Buen Retiro in Madrid. The walls are covered with porcelain panels, not painted plaster. You will see climbing vines, birds, and figures in Chinese-inspired dress. This European fantasy of East Asia was called chinoiserie, and in the eighteenth century it was the height of fashion.

The second surprise waits in a smaller room. Queen Isabella the Second, in the nineteenth century, created a smoking room in the style of the Alhambra. The Alhambra is the great Moorish palace in Granada, built by the Muslim rulers of southern Spain. Here, its carved arches and dense geometric patterns were copied in a royal palace far to the north. It is a reminder that Spain's Islamic past still shaped royal taste centuries after the last Muslim kingdom fell.

Aranjuez is not only a place of leisure. In March 1808, it became the stage for a political crisis. Crowds rose against Manuel Godoy, the powerful chief minister of King Charles the Fourth. The event is remembered as the Mutiny of Aranjuez. Within days, Charles abdicated in favour of his son, Ferdinand the Seventh. Soon after, Napoleon's armies took control of Spain. A quiet spring residence had helped open one of the most turbulent chapters in the country's history.

Now turn toward the gardens, because the palace was always half of the story. Right beside the building lies the Parterre, a formal garden of low hedges and flower beds laid out in neat patterns. Just across a water channel is the Garden of the Island. It sits on land almost surrounded by the river and an artificial canal. Its long avenues pass between tall trees and old fountains, and the sound of water follows you nearly everywhere.

Further east stretches the Prince's Garden, a much larger park created for the future Charles the Fourth. Hidden inside it is the Casa del Labrador, which means the Farmer's House. The name is a royal joke. From outside it looks modest. Inside it is lavishly decorated with silk, marble, and fine clocks. In 2001, UNESCO listed the whole Aranjuez Cultural Landscape as a World Heritage Site. It recognised the palace, gardens, orchards, and planned town as one connected whole.

Many people around the world know the name Aranjuez without knowing the place. In 1939 the Spanish composer Joaquín Rodrigo wrote the Concierto de Aranjuez for guitar and orchestra. Rodrigo had been blind since early childhood. The music is not a literal description of these gardens. It is more like a memory of them. Its slow, aching middle movement has been played and recorded countless times, by classical guitarists and jazz musicians alike.

The land here still feeds people. The fertile river plain has long been known for strawberries and asparagus, and you may find both sold in town when they are in season. Locals take real pride in this produce. A seasonal heritage train, nicknamed the Strawberry Train, runs from Madrid on certain days. It recalls one of Spain's earliest railway lines, which reached Aranjuez in the mid-nineteenth century so that the court could travel here more easily.

Before you walk on, stand still for a moment in the great courtyard. Look at the line where red brick meets white stone, and at the steady windows Herrera's style made famous. Behind you, the Tagus keeps moving past, as it did when kings arrived each spring. As you head toward the gardens, listen for the change in sound. The gravel grows quieter, the trees close in, and the water becomes the loudest voice around you.
