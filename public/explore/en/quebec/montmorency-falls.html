The guide

The story

The sound reaches you before the sight. A deep, steady roar fills the air. Then you see the water. It falls in a wide white sheet down a tall cliff. Mist rises from the bottom and drifts across your face. Look up, and the top of the cliff is far above your head. You are standing beside Montmorency Falls.

These falls are very tall. The water drops about 83 metres, which is roughly 272 feet. That makes them higher than Niagara Falls, though they are far less wide. The Montmorency River ends its journey here. It pours over the edge of the cliff and joins the much larger Saint Lawrence River below. From a distance, the white line of water stands out against the dark rock.

The falls sit about twelve kilometres east of central Quebec City. The land around them is a public park, open through every season. Paths, lookouts, and stairs let you see the water from many angles. You can stand at the top, walk down to the bottom, and pause at points along the side. Benches along the way let you rest and take in the view.

The French explorer Samuel de Champlain gave the falls their name in 1613. He named them after the Montmorency family. This was a powerful noble house in France at the time. The name has stayed for more than four centuries. Few names in this region reach back that far.

At the top of the cliff stands a large house called Manoir Montmorency. In the 1790s, a British prince lived near this spot. He was Prince Edward, the Duke of Kent, the father of the future Queen Victoria. The house you see today is a reconstruction. The earlier building was lost to fire in 1993 and then rebuilt. From its terrace, you look out over the falls and the river beyond.

A footbridge crosses the river right at the top of the falls. When you walk across it, the water rushes beneath your feet and then drops away over the edge. From the middle of the bridge, you can look straight down the full height of the fall. Hold the railing. The view pulls at your eyes. The bridge sways just a little in the wind.

There are two easy ways to move between the top and the bottom. A cable car carries visitors up and down the cliff in a few minutes. A long staircase also runs along the side of the falls. The climb has many steps, but it gives you close views of the falling water the whole way. Take the stairs slowly, and stop often to look.

This place also has a military past. In the summer of 1759, French and British armies fought near here. This was part of a larger war for control of Canada. The British commander, James Wolfe, tried to attack the French position by the falls. The attack failed. A few weeks later, the fighting moved to the Plains of Abraham inside the city.

The falls change with the seasons. In deep winter, the spray freezes as it lands. It slowly builds a tall cone of ice and snow at the base of the cliff. Local people call this cone the sugarloaf. The white mound can grow many metres high before the spring melts it away. In summer, the same spot is green and loud with rushing water.

Walk to the bottom of the falls if you can. From there you look up at the whole wall of water. The wind off the falls is strong and cool, even on a warm day. Fine drops settle on your skin and your clothes. On bright days, the mist often holds a small rainbow. Stand still, and you can feel the ground shake very faintly.

The park offers more than walking. In the warm months, some visitors cross the cliff face on a fixed climbing route. Others ride a zip line across the gorge from one side to the other. You do not need to do these things to enjoy the falls. A slow walk along the paths is more than enough.

Getting here is simple. The falls lie a short drive from the old city, and buses also reach the park. In the warm months, a riverside path connects the area to nearby neighbourhoods. Many people make the falls a half-day trip and pair them with a visit to the island just across the water.

Stand for a moment and just watch the water. It has fallen over this same cliff for thousands of years, long before anyone gave it a name. It will keep falling long after you leave. The sound stays with you as you walk away, soft and steady, like distant thunder that never ends.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the cable car up, then walk down the staircase
Wear a waterproof layer; you will get wet at the base
Cross the footbridge over the crest for a downward view
Come in winter to walk near the frozen sugarloaf cone
