The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes off the river and moves across the grass. Your shoes crunch on gravel. Somewhere behind the walls, a flag snaps against its pole. You are standing on Cap Diamant, the highest point of the rock that holds Quebec City. Far below, the Saint Lawrence River narrows and turns. That narrowing is the reason this fortress exists at all.

The Citadelle of Quebec is a star-shaped fortress. From above, its walls form sharp, angled points called bastions. This was the standard military design of its age. The angles let defenders cover every stretch of wall with crossfire, so no attacker could hide in a blind spot at its base. From the ground, you mostly see low, heavy walls and deep ditches. The real shape shows itself only on a map or from the air.

The British built most of what you see in the first half of the nineteenth century. Work began around 1820 and continued for decades. The design came from Lieutenant-Colonel Elias Walker Durnford of the Royal Engineers. The motive was fear. After the War of 1812, Britain worried that the United States might try again to take its colonies. Quebec controlled the river, and the river was the highway into the heart of the continent.

Here is the first surprise. This enormous fortress never faced the attack it was built to stop. No enemy army ever marched against these walls. In a sense, that was the point. A fortress strong enough to look impossible to take can win without firing a shot. Today the Citadelle is often described as the largest British fortress built in North America, and its quiet history is part of its success.

But the ground under your feet is older than the British walls. Before the British, this was New France, the French colony in North America. French engineers had already fortified Cap Diamant. Inside the Citadelle, a few French structures survive and were folded into the later design, including a redoubt from the late seventeenth century. A redoubt is a small, self-contained stronghold. So when you walk here, you cross two empires in a few steps.

The moment that changed everything happened just beyond these walls. In 1759, British and French forces fought on the Plains of Abraham, the open fields to the southwest of the fortress. The battle was short. Both commanding generals, James Wolfe and the Marquis de Montcalm, were fatally wounded. The British victory led to the end of French rule in Canada. Much of what came after, including the Citadelle, follows from that morning.

Now the second surprise. This is not a museum frozen in place. It is an active military installation. Since the 1920s, the Citadelle has been the home of the Royal 22nd Regiment, a French-speaking infantry regiment of the Canadian Army. English speakers often call them the Van Doos, a playful version of the French word for twenty-two. The soldiers you may see here are real, serving members of the regiment.

In summer, the regiment performs the Changing of the Guard on the parade square. The soldiers wear scarlet tunics and tall black bearskin hats, in the British ceremonial tradition. The drums carry across the stone. And there is a member of the parade most visitors do not expect: a goat. The regiment keeps a goat as its mascot, traditionally named Batisse. He marches with the soldiers, and he has his own handler.

The Citadelle has a second identity, too. Within the walls stands an official residence of the Governor General of Canada, the representative of the Crown in the country. This makes the fortress one of two official homes for that office, the other being in Ottawa. Parts of the residence are sometimes open to visitors. It is unusual to find a working barracks and a vice-regal home sharing the same walls.

During the Second World War, the fortress played host to history once more. In 1943 and again in 1944, Quebec City held two major Allied conferences. Winston Churchill of Britain and Franklin Roosevelt of the United States met here, hosted by Canada's prime minister, William Lyon Mackenzie King. Their discussions helped shape plans for the later stages of the war. Photographs from those days show the leaders seated on the terrace, with the river behind them.

Inside the walls, the Museum of the Royal 22nd Regiment tells the regiment's story. It covers its service in the two world wars and in later missions overseas. The displays include uniforms, weapons, medals, and personal objects carried by soldiers. These small things often say more than the big cannons outside. A worn letter or a dented helmet makes a long war feel human and close.

The Citadelle also anchors something larger. It forms part of the fortifications that still circle Old Quebec. These are often described as the only remaining fortified city walls north of Mexico. Old Quebec is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognised for this survival. From here, you can follow the walls on foot, or walk down the Governors' Promenade along the cliff toward Dufferin Terrace and the Château Frontenac hotel.

As you walk the outer edges, look at the stone itself. The walls are thick, grey, and patched in places, showing generations of repair. Notice how low the outer walls sit in the landscape. This was deliberate. Low, earth-backed walls absorbed cannon fire far better than tall ones. And look for the long ditch that runs outside. It kept attackers exposed and away from the base of the walls.

Before you leave, turn toward the river one more time. Feel how high you are. Watch a ship move slowly through the narrows, where every invader would once have had to pass. The cannons are quiet now. The soldiers march for ceremony, not for war. Take a breath of that cold river air... and notice how the city below still arranges itself around this rock.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in summer to see the Changing of the Guard.
Expect much of the interior to be visited by guided tour.
Bring a windproof layer; the cliff top gets cold.
Walk the Governors' Promenade down toward Dufferin Terrace afterward.
Allow time for the Royal 22nd Regiment Museum inside.
