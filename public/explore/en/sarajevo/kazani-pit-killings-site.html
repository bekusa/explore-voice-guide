The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Above Sarajevo, on the wooded slopes of Mount Trebević, the city sounds fade into birdsong and the creak of branches. The air is cooler here, and the ground underfoot is soft with leaves and loose earth. Through gaps in the trees, you may see rooftops in the valley below. It is a quiet place. For many years, that quiet hid something the city found very hard to speak about.

This is Kazani, a deep natural pit in the hillside. During the war of the 1990s, it became the scene of one of the darkest crimes inside besieged Sarajevo. The victims were mostly Serb civilians who lived in the city. They were taken from their homes, brought up this mountain, and killed. Their bodies were thrown into this pit. This guide will tell that story plainly, because the people who died here deserve plain truth.

To understand Kazani, you first need to understand the siege. From April 1992 until early 1996, Bosnian Serb forces surrounded Sarajevo. They held the hills around the city, including much of this mountain. From those positions, they shelled the streets and fired on civilians below. Food, water, and electricity were scarce. More than ten thousand people died during the siege. Inside the city, life became a daily struggle to survive.

The defenders of Sarajevo were the Army of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina. In the chaotic first years of the war, that army was partly built from local armed groups. Some were led by men from the criminal underworld. They had weapons and fighters when the new state had very little. These men defended front lines, but some also took control of whole neighbourhoods. They operated with little oversight, and their power inside the city grew.

One of these men was Mušan Topalović, known to everyone by his nickname, Caco. Before the war, he had been a musician. During the siege, he commanded the Tenth Mountain Brigade, a unit that held positions on these slopes above the old town. To many locals, Caco was at first a defender. But his unit also became known for intimidation, theft, and forced labour. Civilians were seized on the streets and sent to dig trenches at the front.

Some of those taken never came back. Members of the brigade abducted people from the city, many of them Serbs, and brought them to this area. Here they were beaten, tortured, and killed. The bodies were dropped into the pit at Kazani. Families in the city knew that relatives had disappeared, but they often did not know where. Fear kept many people silent. Speaking out against an armed commander in a besieged city was dangerous.

The exact number of victims is still debated. Remains were later exhumed from the pit, and some victims were identified. Other estimates of the dead are higher. It is honest to say that the full count may never be known. What is clear is that these were civilians, not soldiers. They were neighbours, pensioners, workers, people who had stayed in their city. Their only crime, in the eyes of their killers, was who they were.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. The crimes at Kazani were not stopped by the besieging army. They were stopped by the Bosnian government itself. In late October 1993, the authorities launched a police and military action against the rogue commanders inside the city. It became known as Operation Trebević, named after this mountain. There was fighting in the streets of Sarajevo, and several people died, including police officers.

Caco was arrested during that operation. He was killed while in custody. The official account said he was shot trying to escape, but many people doubted that version. His death meant he never stood trial for what happened here. A few members of his unit were later tried and convicted. Many families felt the sentences were far too light. For them, justice remained incomplete, and the pain of that remains today.

The second surprise is harder. After the war, Caco was reburied in Sarajevo, and a large crowd attended his funeral. For some residents, he was still remembered as a defender of the city in its worst days. That contrast sits at the centre of Kazani. The same man could be seen as a protector by some and a killer by others. Coming to terms with that has taken Sarajevo a very long time.

For years, Kazani was rarely discussed in public. The siege had brought so much suffering to the city that some people found it hard to accept that crimes had also been committed by its own defenders. But silence has slowly given way. In more recent years, local leaders have come to this hillside to lay flowers and to acknowledge publicly what happened here. Relatives of victims, journalists, and activists have pushed to keep the memory alive.

That effort matters well beyond this pit. Sarajevo has long described itself as a city where Muslims, Orthodox Christians, Catholics, and Jews lived side by side. Kazani is a reminder that the war tested that idea from every direction. Remembering the victims here does not lessen the suffering of the siege. It adds to the truth of it. A city that can name crimes committed in its own name is a city that is trying to heal.

As you stand here, look again at the trees, the slope, the light coming through the leaves. This is an ordinary forest, and that is part of what makes the place so heavy. There is no grand monument to fill the silence. There is only the hillside, the pit, and whatever flowers someone has left. You may want to spend a few moments here in quiet, thinking of the people who were brought up this path.

When you walk back down toward the city, listen for the sounds returning. The traffic, the call to prayer, church bells, voices from cafés. These are the sounds of the same Sarajevo that the victims of Kazani once knew. Their names are part of this city too. Carry them with you as you go, and let the quiet of this forest stay with you a little longer.

Before you go

Practical tips

Keep voices low; this is a place of mourning.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, leafy forest ground.
Visit in daylight, as the wooded paths are steep.
Bring a flower if you wish to pay respects.
Ask before photographing any relatives visiting the site.
