The guide

The story

Lower your head as you step inside. The air turns cool and damp, and it smells of wet earth and old timber. Your shoulders brush the walls. Above you, wooden beams and metal frames hold back the soil. The floor is narrow, and in places you can see the rails where small carts once rolled. This short passage is all that remains open of a lifeline. For a few years in the 1990s, an entire city breathed through a hole like this one.

You are standing in Butmir, on the edge of Sarajevo, beside a modest family house. Locals call this place the Tunnel of Hope. In Bosnian, it is often called the Tunnel of Rescue. It does not look like a monument. There is no grand gate and no marble. There is a yard, a house with damaged walls, and a doorway into the ground. That plainness is part of the story. Ordinary people built this, with ordinary tools, under extraordinary pressure.

To understand why it exists, picture the city around you during the war. Bosnia and Herzegovina declared independence in 1992, and Sarajevo was soon surrounded. Bosnian Serb forces held the hills that ring the valley. From those positions, they fired shells and sniper rounds into the streets below. The siege lasted from 1992 to 1996. It is widely described as the longest siege of a capital city in modern warfare. Food, fuel, medicine and water all became desperately scarce.

Now look at the geography, because it explains everything. Between the besieged city and the free territory around Butmir and Mount Igman lay one flat, open space — the airport. During the war, the airport was under United Nations control. The runway formed a gap in the ring around Sarajevo. But crossing it on foot meant running across open ground, exposed to gunfire. Many people tried. Many did not make it. The answer, people decided, was to go underneath.

So the digging began, in early 1993. Work started from two ends at once. One team dug from the Dobrinja neighbourhood, inside the city. The other team dug from here, in Butmir. They worked largely by hand, with shovels, pickaxes and wheelbarrows. They worked in shifts, day and night. Soil came out in buckets and carts. The two teams had to meet in the middle, deep beneath an active runway — and they did, by the summer of 1993.

The finished tunnel ran for roughly eight hundred metres. That is the length of several city blocks, all of it underground. Yet it was tiny inside. Most adults had to stoop the whole way. It was barely wide enough for one person carrying a load. Groundwater was a constant problem. Walkers often moved through water and mud, sometimes up to their knees. Pumps had to run to keep the passage usable. Imagine making that walk in the dark, carrying a heavy sack.

And people carried everything. Food and medicine came into the city. Weapons and ammunition came in for the defenders. Wounded people and civilians went out. Soldiers moved in both directions. Over time, the tunnel improved. Rails were laid so carts could carry heavier loads. Later, a fuel pipeline and electricity cables ran through it as well. A single passage under a runway kept hospitals, homes and the city's defence supplied with the basics.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Butmir entrance was not hidden in a military bunker. It began inside a private family home, the house you see beside you. The family that lived here allowed their house and yard to become the start of the tunnel. That house was in the line of fire for the rest of the war. Look closely at its walls as you walk around it. The damage from shelling has been left in place on purpose.

Inside the house and the small museum, you will find objects from those years. There are tools of the kind used to dig. There are carts, stretchers, and uniforms. There are photographs of people bent low in the passage, faces lit by weak lamps. There is often a short film showing life during the siege. The exhibits are simple, and the rooms are small. Give them time. The power of this place comes from close looking, not from size.

Only a short stretch of the original tunnel is open to visitors today. The rest has collapsed, been filled in, or lies under private land and the airport. Still, that short walk is enough to feel the scale of the effort. Notice how quickly the light fades behind you. Notice how your back starts to ache after just a few steps. Then multiply that walk many times over, with a sack of flour on your shoulders, and the airport's lights above.

A second surprise is how close everything still is. Step back outside and listen. You may hear aircraft engines. The runway that once divided the city is still a working airport, very near where you stand. Planes take off over the same ground that people crossed in fear. Beyond the fields, the slopes of Mount Igman rise to the south. During the war, routes over that mountain linked the tunnel to the rest of free territory.

For many Sarajevans, this site is personal, not historical. Some people who visit today crossed the tunnel themselves, or had parents who did. Local guides often share their own family memories of the siege. You may notice that visitors here are quieter than at other sights. People speak in low voices. Children ask simple questions, and adults take a long time to answer. It is a museum, but it is also a place of memory for a city that survived.

Before you leave, stand for a moment in the yard. Look at the scarred walls of the house, and the low dark entrance in the ground. Listen to the next plane rising over the runway. Somewhere under the fields between here and Dobrinja, the rest of the tunnel still lies in the earth. As you walk back toward the city, look up at the hills around the valley. For four years, this small passage was the way through them.

Practical tips
