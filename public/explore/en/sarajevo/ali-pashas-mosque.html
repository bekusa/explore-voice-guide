The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, trams rattle along the main boulevard, and car horns rise and fall. Then you pass through the gate, and the sound drops away. Gravel shifts underfoot. Old trees throw shade across the grass. A single grey dome sits in front of you, low and calm, with a slender stone minaret rising beside it. This is Ali Pasha's Mosque, one of the quietest corners in central Sarajevo, only a few steps from the traffic.

Take in the position first. The mosque stands on Marshal Tito Street, the long avenue that runs through the heart of the modern city. To one side lies the old Ottoman market quarter, Baščaršija, with its copper workshops and cobbled lanes. To the other side rise the heavy facades of the Austro-Hungarian period and, further on, the towers of the twentieth century. The mosque sits right on that seam. It is a small Ottoman building surrounded by centuries of later city.

Now, the name. Ali Pasha was an Ottoman governor of Bosnia in the mid-sixteenth century. At that time, Bosnia was a province of the Ottoman Empire, and Sarajevo was growing fast as its trading centre. Wealthy officials often left money for a mosque, a school, or a fountain. This kind of charitable endowment is called a vakuf in Bosnia. It funded public buildings for generations. Ali Pasha did the same, and the mosque was built around 1560 and 1561, from the endowment he left after his death.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. Ali Pasha did not leave the site. He is buried here, in the courtyard, under a small domed tomb. In Ottoman tradition, a tomb like this is called a turbe. Look for it among the trees. It is modest compared with the mosque, but it tells you something important. This was not only a place of prayer. It was also a founder's memorial, built so that his name would stay in the city.

Look at the shape of the main building. It follows a classical Ottoman plan. A square prayer hall carries one large dome. The weight of the dome passes down through the corners into thick walls. There are no towers of decoration, no bright paint on the outside. The beauty comes from proportion. The dome, the cube beneath it, and the tall minaret are balanced against each other. Some writers connect the design to the circle of Mimar Sinan, the great imperial architect, but that link is a matter of discussion, not a documented fact.

Walk toward the entrance. In front of the door, a covered porch shelters the approach. Its small domes rest on stone columns. In Ottoman mosques, this porch served as an overflow space. When the hall was full on a Friday, latecomers prayed here, under the open arches. It was also a place to pause, to take off your shoes, and to leave the street behind. Stand under it for a moment and notice how the light changes.

Now raise your eyes to the minaret. It is a thin stone shaft with a pointed cap, and a small balcony rings it near the top. For centuries, the muezzin climbed inside to that balcony and called the faithful to prayer. Today, loudspeakers often carry the call. Still, five times a day, the voice rises over the boulevard. Locals hear it mixed with traffic, church bells from nearby, and the clang of trams. That mix of sounds is very typical of Sarajevo.

Here is a second surprise. This building has lived through hard times. During the siege of Sarajevo, from 1992 to 1995, the city came under constant shelling. Many mosques, churches, and libraries were hit. Ali Pasha's Mosque was damaged too. After the war, it was repaired and restored. If you look closely at the stonework, you may notice that some surfaces look newer than others. That difference shows the long work of putting the city back together.

The garden itself deserves a slow walk. Around the mosque, old gravestones stand in the grass. Ottoman Muslim gravestones in Bosnia are often tall and narrow. Many carry a carved turban or a fez on top for a man. Women's stones are usually simpler, sometimes with a floral shape. The carving on each stone once told passers-by about rank and status. Even if you cannot read the old script, the shapes still tell part of the story.

If the door is open and visitors are welcome, step inside quietly. Inside, the space feels larger than you expect. That is the effect of the single dome. There are no columns to break the view. Look for the prayer niche in the wall facing Mecca. It is called the mihrab. Beside it, a tall stepped pulpit, called the minber, is used for the Friday sermon. Carpets cover the floor, and the light comes in through rows of windows.

Think about what this place meant to the city in the sixteenth century. Sarajevo then was smaller and much greener. Mosques like this one often became the centre of a neighbourhood. Houses gathered around them, and the neighbourhood often took the mosque's name. A fountain or a school might stand nearby. The mosque was a meeting point, a clock, and a landmark. Today, the houses have changed, but the building still marks this corner of the city.

It is worth comparing this mosque with the larger Gazi Husrev-beg Mosque in the old market quarter. That one is busier and more famous. Ali Pasha's Mosque is smaller and calmer, and fewer tour groups stop here. That makes it a good place to see a classical Ottoman mosque without a crowd. You can stand in the garden, look at the dome, and think, with only birds and distant traffic for company.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the garden and look back. Notice the grey dome against the sky. Notice the minaret beside the newer buildings behind it. Notice the trees and the old stones in the grass. Then walk back through the gate. The trams and car horns return. But now you know that, a few steps from the boulevard, a governor from the sixteenth century still rests in his quiet garden.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly, covering shoulders and knees, before entering.
Remove your shoes at the entrance to the prayer hall.
Avoid visiting inside during the Friday midday prayer.
Combine with a walk east into Baščaršija's old lanes.
Visit early morning when the garden is at its quietest.
