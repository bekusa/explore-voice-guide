The guide

The story

Put your palm flat against the stone. It is cool, slightly rough, and pitted like the skin of an orange. Rain and frost have worked on this surface for hundreds of years. Along the edges, the carving has gone soft and rounded. A spiral that was once sharp now looks like it was drawn with a wet finger. This is a stećak, a medieval tombstone from the hills of Bosnia. You are standing beside one of the oldest made objects in Sarajevo.

The word stećak, with stećci as the plural, names a whole family of monuments. They were carved and placed over graves across this region in the Middle Ages, most of them in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Some are older, and some are a little younger. The name is often linked to a local word meaning to stand. That fits. These stones were made to stand in the open landscape, in meadows and on slopes, where travellers could see them.

In Sarajevo, the best place to meet these stones is the garden of the National Museum of Bosnia and Herzegovina. Here, stećci brought from different parts of the country rest among trees and lawns. It is a quiet place. You hear birds, the crunch of gravel, and the low hum of traffic beyond the walls. The stones sit in rows, a little like a congregation. Each one came from somewhere else, and each one once marked a real person.

Look at the shapes first, before the decoration. Some are flat slabs, laid on the ground like a heavy lid. Others are chests, solid blocks that rise to about knee or waist height. The most striking ones have a pitched top, like the roof of a small house. Scholars often call these gabled stones. A few are upright pillars or crosses. The house shape is worth a moment. For many visitors, it suggests a home for the dead.

Most stećci were cut from local limestone. That pale stone is soft enough to carve but strong enough to last. The workers who made them had no cranes, so imagine the effort. A single block could weigh several tonnes. It had to be quarried, shaped, and moved, sometimes over rough ground. Communities put real labour and real money into these graves. That tells you something about the people buried beneath them. Many were landowners, local nobles, or respected families.

Now move closer and look for the carvings. Not every stone is decorated. Many are plain, and their beauty is in the mass and the proportion. But on others you will find spirals, rosettes, crescent moons, stars, vine scrolls, and twisted rope borders. There are swords and shields. There are bows and arrows. Some stones show hunting scenes, with riders, dogs, and deer. Others show people holding hands in a line. Many interpret that line as the kolo, a circle dance still performed in the Balkans.

Here is one detail many visitors miss. On some stones a human figure stands with one hand raised, palm open, and the hand is drawn oversized. The best-known example stands at Radimlja, a necropolis near the southern town of Stolac. Nobody knows exactly what the gesture means. Some see a greeting, some a blessing, some a sign of strength or an oath. The honest answer is that the meaning is still debated. That uncertainty is part of the pull of these stones.

A smaller number of stećci carry inscriptions. They are cut in a regional form of Cyrillic script that scholars call Bosnian Cyrillic, or bosančica. Some name the dead person. Some name the carver. A few speak directly to the person reading, with short lines about death, memory, and the land. If you find letters on a stone here, follow them with your eyes from left to right. You are reading words that someone chose for a grave around six hundred years ago.

For a long time, people linked the stećci to a single religious group. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, a popular idea tied them to the Bogomils, a dualist Christian movement. Today, most historians see a wider picture. Members of the Catholic and Orthodox churches used these stones, and so did followers of the medieval Bosnian Church. The stećci belong to a shared regional culture. They are not the property of one faith or one modern nation.

That shared character was recognised in 2016. In that year, UNESCO added the stećci to its World Heritage List. The listing is unusual because it crosses borders. It covers selected graveyards in four countries: Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Montenegro, and Serbia. Most of the listed sites are in Bosnia and Herzegovina. Across the wider region, researchers estimate that tens of thousands of these stones survive, many of them still in fields and forests far from any museum.

The stones also live on in literature. In 1966, the Bosnian poet Mak Dizdar published a collection called Stone Sleeper. He drew on the images and inscriptions of the stećci and turned them into modern poems. The book became one of the best-loved works of poetry in Bosnia and Herzegovina. For many people here, the stećci are not only archaeology. They are a symbol of a deep local past, older than the Ottoman and Austro-Hungarian layers you see across Sarajevo.

As you walk among them, notice how the light changes the stone. In flat midday light, the carvings almost disappear. When the sun is low, shadows fill every groove, and a faint spiral or rider suddenly appears. After rain, the limestone darkens and the lichen glows green and orange. You may notice small repairs, chipped corners, or worn faces. Each mark records time, weather, and the long journey from a hillside grave to this garden.

Before you leave, choose one stone and stay with it for a minute. Look at its shape, its height, the way it sits on the ground. Somebody stood where you stand now, at the side of a fresh grave, and watched this stone placed. As you walk back toward the street, listen for the city again, the trams and the voices. Then remember the quiet rows behind you, still standing, as they were made to do.

Look closer

What to look for

House-shaped stones with a pitched, roof-like top.
Spiral and rosette patterns along the stone edges.
A human figure raising one oversized open hand.
A line of figures holding hands in a dance.
Lichen patches in green and orange on the limestone.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in late afternoon when low sun sharpens the carvings.
Walk slowly along each row; plain stones hide faint details.
Bring a camera with a zoom to frame small motifs.
Combine the garden visit with the National Museum galleries.
Plan a day trip to Radimlja to see stones in situ.
