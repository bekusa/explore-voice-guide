The guide

The story

Listen first. Beside you, the Miljacka River runs shallow over its stones, a low steady hush under the traffic. Across the water, the old city climbs the hillsides in red roofs and minarets. Then you turn, and here it is. This is a pale building with patterned bands and arched windows, and it looks as if it came from Andalusia rather than the Balkans. This is the Sarajevo Synagogue. For more than a century, it has stood quietly on this riverbank.

The building is often called the Ashkenazi Synagogue. That name tells part of its story. The Ashkenazim are Jews whose families came from Central and Eastern Europe. Many of them arrived in Sarajevo after 1878, when Austria-Hungary took control of Bosnia. They came as officials, doctors, engineers and merchants. They spoke German, Yiddish and Hungarian. Within a generation, this growing community wanted a house of worship of its own.

The synagogue was completed around 1902. Its designer was Karel Pařík, an architect from Czech lands. Pařík shaped much of Sarajevo under Austro-Hungarian rule. He designed public buildings, churches and grand façades across the city. If you walk the riverside after your visit, you will probably pass more of his work. That is a small surprise for many visitors: one architect shaped buildings for several faiths in this one city.

Now look at the style. This is Moorish Revival, a nineteenth-century fashion that copied the art of medieval Islamic Spain. Across Central Europe, Jewish communities chose it for their synagogues. It looked grand, and it was clearly different from the Gothic churches nearby. It also pointed back to Spain, where Jewish culture once flourished under Muslim rule. Notice the horseshoe arches, the striped and patterned surfaces, and the small domed turrets that rise above the roofline.

That Spanish connection matters in Sarajevo more than almost anywhere else. Jews were living in this city long before the Ashkenazim arrived. They were Sephardim, descendants of the Jews expelled from Spain in 1492. Many travelled east into the Ottoman Empire. Some reached Sarajevo in the sixteenth century. They brought their own language, Ladino, a form of old Spanish. Families here spoke it at home for hundreds of years.

The Sephardic community had its own synagogue in the old Ottoman market district. People call it the Old Temple, and today it houses the city's Jewish Museum. So Sarajevo once had two Jewish worlds side by side. One was old, Spanish-speaking and rooted in the Ottoman city. The other was newer, Central European and tied to Vienna and Budapest. This building belongs to the second world. Today, it serves both.

That change came from tragedy. Before the Second World War, Sarajevo had a large and active Jewish population. During the war, most of the city's Jews were murdered in the Holocaust. The synagogue was looted and damaged, but the building itself survived. After the war, only a small community remained. Sephardim and Ashkenazim no longer had the numbers to keep separate congregations. So they began to pray here together.

This is one of the quiet facts that many visitors miss. Of the city's historic synagogues, this is the one that still works as a synagogue. It is not only a monument. It holds services and community life. The other historic synagogues became museums, galleries or empty shells. This building on the riverbank is the one where Jewish worship in Sarajevo continued.

The second surprise comes from the 1990s. During the long siege of Sarajevo, the city was cut off. It was shelled, and people lacked food, medicine and heat. The Jewish community, through its humanitarian society La Benevolencija, ran aid operations from this building. They distributed medicine, food and messages, and they did not ask whether people were Muslim, Orthodox, Catholic or Jewish. For many families during those years, this synagogue was a place of practical help as well as faith.

That history fits a pattern people in Sarajevo often describe with pride. Within a short walk of here, you can find a mosque, an Orthodox cathedral, a Catholic cathedral and this synagogue. Locals sometimes call the city a small Jerusalem. That phrase is more an affectionate image than a precise description. Still, it reflects something real. For centuries, different faiths lived side by side in these narrow valleys, even through hard and violent times.

If the doors are open and visitors are welcome, step inside respectfully. Dress modestly, keep your voice low, and follow any instructions from the people at the entrance. Security checks are normal at synagogues across Europe, so please be patient with them. Inside, the decoration continues the Moorish theme. You will see painted patterns, arches and warm colours. Notice how the light from the arched windows falls across the surfaces.

Outside again, step back toward the river for the best view. From the far bank or from one of the nearby bridges, you can see the whole façade. Then the scale makes sense. The building is not huge. It is detailed and carefully balanced, with a strong central section and towers on each side. In the late afternoon, the sun warms the pale walls, and the patterned bands become easier to read.

Before you move on, stay by the railing for a moment. The river is still running over its stones. Trams still rattle along the embankment. Across the water, the call to prayer may drift down from a minaret. Behind you stands a building that survived empire, war and siege. It still serves the people it was built for. As you walk away, look back once. Notice those arched windows facing the river, as they have for more than a hundred years.
