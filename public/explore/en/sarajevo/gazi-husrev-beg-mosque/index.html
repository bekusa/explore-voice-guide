The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water runs into a stone basin somewhere ahead of you, steady and soft. Beyond the courtyard wall, Sarajevo carries on — the clink of coffee cups, the rattle of copper trays, pigeons lifting off warm paving stones. Inside this wall, the noise drops away. The air smells of damp stone and old trees. You are standing at the spiritual centre of the old Ottoman city, in the courtyard of the Gazi Husrev-beg Mosque.

The mosque carries the name of the man who paid for it. Gazi Husrev-beg was the Ottoman governor of Bosnia in the early sixteenth century. Through his mother, he was a grandson of Sultan Bayezid the Second, so he came from the edge of the imperial family itself. The title Gazi means a warrior for the faith. He earned it on campaign. But in Sarajevo, people remember him less as a soldier and more as a builder.

The mosque was completed in the early 1530s. Most sources name its architect as Ajem Esir Ali, a Persian master who worked as chief architect at the Ottoman court in Istanbul. That matters. It means this building was not a provincial copy. It was designed by one of the leading builders of the empire, in the generation just before the great Sinan. Many historians count it among the finest Ottoman mosques anywhere in the Balkans.

Look at the shape of the building. A large central dome sits over the prayer hall, with smaller domes stepping down in front of it, over the covered porch. Behind them, a single slim minaret rises above the rooftops. This is the classical Ottoman pattern: weight gathered in the middle, lightness at the edges. The walls are cut stone. The domes are covered in grey lead, which dulls over time and turns almost silver after rain.

Here is something most visitors miss. The mosque was only one part of a much larger plan. Gazi Husrev-beg used a religious endowment, called a vakuf, to fund an entire neighbourhood. Around this courtyard he paid for a school, a bathhouse, a covered market, an inn for travelling merchants, and a library. The rents from the shops and the inn kept the mosque and the school running. In a real sense, he built the economic engine of old Sarajevo.

Some of that engine still works today. Across the narrow street stands the Kuršumli Madrasa, a religious school named for its lead-covered roofs. Kuršum is the local word for lead. The library he founded also survives, and it is one of the oldest continuously working libraries in this part of Europe. Its collection holds thousands of manuscripts in Arabic, Turkish and Persian. Students still walk these lanes with books under their arms, nearly five centuries after he planned them.

Now turn toward the fountain in the middle of the courtyard. This is the shadirvan, a covered ablution fountain. Before prayer, worshippers wash their hands, face and feet here, as Islamic practice requires. The wooden roof shades the water, and the trees around it keep the courtyard cool even in high summer. Watch for a while and you will see people stop, roll up their sleeves, and wash in a quiet, practiced rhythm. Then they cross the stones to the doors.

Near the mosque, behind an iron grille, stands a small domed tomb. This is the turbe, or mausoleum, of Gazi Husrev-beg himself. He died on campaign in 1541, and his body was brought back to the city he had shaped. A second tomb nearby is traditionally linked to one of his close companions. Locals often pause at the grille for a short prayer. It is a reminder that the founder still rests in the middle of his own creation.

Here is a second surprise. Late in the nineteenth century, this mosque received electric lighting. It is often described as one of the first mosques in the world to be lit by electricity. At that time Bosnia was under Austro-Hungarian rule, and Sarajevo was being modernised fast — trams, gas lamps, new boulevards. So the old Ottoman prayer hall glowed with electric light while many European capitals still burned candles and oil.

The building has also survived serious damage. In 1697, an Austrian army under Prince Eugene of Savoy raided Sarajevo and set much of the city on fire. The mosque burned with it and had to be repaired. Three centuries later, during the siege of Sarajevo in the 1990s, artillery fire struck the mosque again. After the war, a long restoration brought it back. When you look closely at the stonework, you can sometimes see the line between older and newer stone.

If you go inside, remember that this is a working mosque, not a museum. Five times a day, the call to prayer rises from the minaret and the hall fills with worshippers. Visitors remove their shoes at the door. Women are usually asked to cover their hair, and everyone should cover shoulders and knees. Inside, the floor is soft with carpets. Look up into the dome, then toward the mihrab, the niche that shows the direction of Mecca.

Before you leave, glance toward the tall stone clock tower just beside the mosque complex. Its face shows a time that may not match your phone. The tower keeps lunar time, an old Ottoman system in which the day begins at sunset. So the clock is adjusted regularly, by hand, as the seasons change. It is one of very few clocks in the world still kept this way, and it once told the city when to pray.

When you step back out through the gate, notice how quickly the old bazaar returns. Copper workers tap at their trays. Coffee is poured into small cups. The domes of the mosque disappear behind wooden shopfronts after only a few steps. That closeness is the point. Gazi Husrev-beg did not build a monument set apart from the city. He built the city around a place of prayer.

So take one more look before you go. The water still runs into the stone basin. The lead domes still catch the light. Somewhere above you, the slim minaret still marks the centre of old Sarajevo, as it has for nearly five hundred years. As you walk on into the lanes of Baščaršija, listen for the next call to prayer. It will follow you further than you expect.
