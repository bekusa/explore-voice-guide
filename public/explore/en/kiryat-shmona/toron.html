The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look east. The Hula Valley spreads out below in long green and gold strips, and the air carries a faint smell of cut fields and damp earth. Behind you, the Naftali ridge rises steeply, a wall of pale limestone and scrub. Somewhere overhead, you may hear birds calling as they pass. This is Kiryat Shmona, at the very top of the Galilee, and the name Toron belongs to this northern edge.

First, a quiet word of honesty. Toron is not a place with a thick guidebook entry behind it, and we would rather tell you what is true than fill this walk with invented dates. So we will do two things together. We will look closely at what is around you. And we will follow the old name Toron, because it has a real story in these hills, one that is much older than the town itself.

That story begins with the Crusaders. In the early twelfth century, a European knight named Hugh of Saint Omer built a castle on a hilltop to the west of here. It stood in what is today southern Lebanon, across the border. The castle was called Toron, and it became the centre of a lordship. A lordship was a feudal territory, a piece of land ruled by one noble family, who owed loyalty to the Crusader king in Jerusalem.

The lords of Toron controlled roads, villages, and farmland across these upper Galilee hills. Their family took the castle's name, so you will find knights called Humphrey of Toron in the chronicles of the period. Then, in 1187, the great Muslim leader Saladin swept through the region and took the castle. Its later history moved back and forth between rulers. So when you hear the word Toron here, you hear an echo of that medieval frontier.

And here is the first surprise. This corner of the land has been a frontier for most of its recorded history. Crusader lords watched these valleys from their towers. Later empires drew and redrew their lines across the same ridges. Today, an international border runs along the hills just a few kilometres from where you stand. The geography has not changed. The valley still sits low and open, and the heights still look down on it.

Now look at the town itself. Kiryat Shmona is a young place. It was founded in 1949, soon after the State of Israel was created, and many of its first residents were new immigrants. You will notice that the buildings are mostly plain and practical. Concrete apartment blocks, low shops, wide roads. This is not a town of old stone lanes. It is a town that was built quickly, by people who needed homes.

The name holds the second surprise. Kiryat Shmona means Town of the Eight. It honours eight people who were killed in 1920 defending Tel Hai, a small Jewish settlement on the hill just north of here. The best-known of them was Joseph Trumpeldor, a one-armed former soldier who became a legend in Israeli memory. So every time someone says the town's name, they are remembering that small fight on the hill above.

Turn your attention back to the valley floor. The Hula Valley was once a large lake surrounded by swamps. In the 1950s, much of it was drained to create farmland. Later, part of it was flooded again to bring back the wetland. Today the valley is one of the great resting places for migrating birds. In autumn and spring, huge flocks of cranes pass over on their way between Europe and Africa.

If you look northeast on a clear day, you will see the long shape of Mount Hermon. In winter its upper slopes turn white with snow. The water that melts from the Hermon feeds the springs that become the Jordan River. Those springs rise only a short drive from here. So this flat green valley in front of you is one of the places where the Jordan begins its journey south.

Now listen. The sounds of Kiryat Shmona are ordinary town sounds. Traffic on the main road, voices from a café, a bus pulling away. But under them you can often hear water. Small streams and irrigation channels run through this area, fed by the northern springs. In summer, the air in the valley can feel heavy and hot. Up on the ridge, the breeze is cooler, and the view opens wider.

This is also a town that has lived through hard years. Because it sits so close to the border, Kiryat Shmona has faced rocket fire many times across the decades. In recent years, many residents had to leave their homes for long periods. You may see public shelters along the streets, painted and marked. They are part of daily life here. Treat them with respect, and know where the nearest one is.

So what does Toron mean to you, standing here today? It is a thread that ties this modern town to a much older map. A Crusader name, a hilltop castle beyond the border, and a frontier that has shifted again and again. The town around you is only a few generations old. But the hills above it have carried names, armies, and farmers for many centuries.

Before you move on, take one more look. Follow the line of the ridge from north to south. Let your eye drop to the fields, then lift again toward the snow line of the Hermon. Notice the light on the limestone and the colour of the valley floor. When you walk away, listen for the water and the birds. They were here long before the castle, and long before the town.
