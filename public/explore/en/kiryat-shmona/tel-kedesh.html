The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here, the wind moves through dry grass and thistle, and it carries the faint clank of a cowbell from somewhere in the valley. Under your feet the ground is uneven. It is soft earth, broken limestone, and the odd shard of pottery. You are standing on Tel Kedesh, a broad, flat-topped hill in the Upper Galilee. It is a short drive from Kiryat Shmona, close to the Lebanese border. It looks like a natural rise. It is not. It is a city, stacked on itself.

A tel is a special kind of hill. The word comes from Hebrew and Arabic, and it means a mound built by people, not by nature. Each generation built on the ruins of the last one. Mud brick collapsed, stone was reused, floors were laid over older floors. Over thousands of years, the layers grew higher and higher. Kedesh is one of the larger mounds in the Galilee. When you walk across its top, you walk across the roof of many lost towns.

The name Kedesh comes from a Semitic root that means holy. That fits. This place appears several times in the Hebrew Bible. It is often called Kedesh in Naphtali, after the Israelite tribe whose land surrounded it. The Book of Joshua lists Kedesh as one of the cities of refuge. These were towns where a person who had killed someone by accident could flee. There, they could find protection from revenge until a fair hearing took place. It is an old idea, written into the landscape: a safe hill, visible from a distance.

The Book of Judges adds a story. It names Kedesh as the home of Barak, the commander who fought alongside the prophetess Deborah against a Canaanite army. In the biblical account, Barak gathered his forces here before the battle. Archaeologists cannot confirm the details of that story. But the tradition is old, and it has kept the name of Kedesh alive for a very long time. Some local traditions also point to nearby tombs as burial places of biblical figures. These are beliefs, not proven history.

The Bible also records a darker moment. In the eighth century before the common era, the Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser the Third swept through the Galilee. The Second Book of Kings lists Kedesh among the towns he captured, and it says he took the people into exile. Assyria was the great empire of northern Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq. For the people on this hill, that campaign was the end of a world. Then, slowly, the hill filled with people again.

Now look around at the land itself. To the east, the ground drops toward the Hula Valley and the Jordan's upper streams. To the north and west, the hills rise toward Lebanon. The valley below the tel is green in winter and gold in late summer. Springs and good soil made this spot worth holding. Roads between the coast and the interior passed nearby. A fortified town here could watch traffic, collect taxes, and feed itself. That simple geography explains much of what happened here.

Here is the first surprise. Some of the most important finds at Kedesh are not from the biblical period at all. Excavations led by American universities uncovered a large administrative building from the Persian and Hellenistic eras. Hellenistic means the centuries after Alexander the Great, when Greek-speaking kingdoms ruled the region. Inside, archaeologists found an archive room. The documents themselves, written on papyrus, are long gone. But the clay seals that once closed them survived.

There were around two thousand of these small clay seal impressions, called bullae. Officials pressed a seal into a lump of wet clay to close a rolled document. When the building burned, the papyrus turned to ash. The fire baked the clay seals hard, and they survived. Many show images of gods, rulers, and Greek symbols. In a strange way, the fire that destroyed the archive also preserved part of it. Without the documents, scholars read the seals instead, like a shadow of the lost paperwork.

In that period, Kedesh seems to have looked west, toward the great port city of Tyre on the Lebanese coast. The ancient historian Josephus describes Kedesh as a Tyrian stronghold, often hostile to the Jewish villages of the Galilee. The First Book of Maccabees also describes a battle near Kedesh. In it, the Jewish leader Jonathan faced the army of a Greek-speaking Seleucid king. So this quiet hill sat on a real frontier, between coast and hinterland, between different peoples and loyalties.

The second surprise is a short walk from the mound. Look for the remains of a Roman temple, which many visitors miss. It is one of the better-preserved Roman temples in this part of the country. Its walls of cut limestone still stand in places, and carved doorframes and decorated blocks lie around it. Scholars generally date it to the Roman imperial period, probably the second century. Inscriptions and the style suggest worship of a sky or sun god. The exact dedication is still discussed.

Stand near the temple's main doorway and look closely at the stone. You can see the work of a chisel, the crisp edges of moulded frames, and patches of weathering where rain has run down the face for centuries. Some blocks have fallen and lie at odd angles in the grass. Nearby, there are also ancient tombs and stone coffins carved from the rock. Together, they show that Roman Kedesh was a prosperous town, with money for fine building and careful burial.

The story does not stop with Rome. Later, an Arab village called Qadas stood near here, and its name carried the ancient word forward. The village was emptied during the war of 1948, and its residents left. Today you may notice cut stones, terraces, and old field walls from that later life. The hill holds every layer, including the recent ones. Walking here, you share the ground with all of them, ancient and modern.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment on the top of the tel. Feel the uneven earth under your shoes. Look at the scattered stones, the dry grass, and the long valley below. Somewhere beneath you are floors, hearths, and burned seals still waiting in the soil. As you walk back down, watch the ground near the path. A small piece of red or grey pottery may catch your eye. Leave it where it lies. It belongs to the hill, and to the next person who stops to listen.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in early morning to avoid harsh midday heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose stones and uneven ground.
Bring water since shade on the mound is limited.
Combine the tel with a stop at the Roman temple.
