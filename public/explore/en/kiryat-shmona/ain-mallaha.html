The guide

The story

Listen for the water first. Somewhere close by, a spring rises out of the ground and feeds the wet, green floor of the Hula Valley. Reeds move in the wind. Birds call over the fields. To the west, the ridge of the Naftali hills holds the afternoon light. This quiet corner just south of Kiryat Shmona does not look like much at first glance. Yet the ground under your feet holds one of the oldest village stories anywhere on Earth.

This place is ʿAin Mallaha, which also appears in books under its Hebrew name, Eynan. The name means the salty spring, and that spring is the reason people came here at all. Around twelve thousand years ago, long before farming, long before pottery, a community of hunters and gatherers decided to stop moving. They built houses here. They buried their dead here. They came back season after season, and eventually they stayed for most of the year.

Archaeologists call these people the Natufians. The name comes from Wadi en-Natuf, a valley in the hills further south, where the British archaeologist Dorothy Garrod first identified their tools in the late 1920s. The Natufians lived across the Levant, the lands along the eastern Mediterranean. But ʿAin Mallaha became one of the clearest windows into their daily life. Here, more than almost anywhere else, researchers could see a people in the middle of a huge change.

Think about what that change means. For hundreds of thousands of years, humans had followed animals and plants across the land. A camp lasted days or weeks. Then, in places like this, people began to build in stone and to invest in one spot. They had a reason. The valley offered fresh water, fish and waterbirds from the old lake, deer and gazelle on the slopes, and wild cereals and nuts on the hillsides. Staying put simply made sense.

Excavations began in the 1950s under the French archaeologist Jean Perrot. Later French teams continued the work over several decades. What they found was a cluster of round houses. Each one was dug partly into the slope, so the floor sat below ground level. Low walls of stacked stone lined the pit. Above that, the walls and roof were probably made of wood, brush and hides — materials that have long since disappeared. Inside, people kept hearths and worked at stone tools.

The objects from these floors are quiet but telling. There are heavy stone mortars and pestles for crushing seeds and nuts. There are small flint blades with a polished shine along the edge. That shine has a simple cause. It comes from cutting stalks of wild grasses again and again, because plant stems contain tiny particles of silica that slowly polish the stone. In other words, these people were harvesting wild cereals with sickles, thousands of years before anyone here planted a field.

Now comes the find that surprises almost every visitor. In one grave at ʿAin Mallaha, excavators uncovered an elderly person lying on one side, with a hand resting on the body of a puppy. The burial is roughly twelve thousand years old. Scientists published it in the late 1970s, and it became one of the earliest known signs of a close bond between humans and dogs. It is hard to read that gesture as anything other than affection.

The dead were never far away in this village. Many burials were placed among or beneath the houses, close to the living. Some people were buried with ornaments made from shells and bone. Shells from the Mediterranean, and in some Natufian sites from much further away, show that these communities were part of wider networks of exchange. A string of beads could travel across many valleys before it reached a family here beside the spring.

It helps to picture the landscape as it once was. Below this hillside lay Lake Hula, a shallow lake surrounded by swamps and papyrus. For thousands of years it shaped life in the valley. In the 1950s, the modern state drained most of the lake and its marshes for farmland. Today a smaller wetland has been restored nearby as a nature reserve. So the green fields you see were, for most of human history, open water and reeds.

That lake explains a lot about the Natufians here. Fish bones and bird bones turn up alongside the bones of gazelle and deer. The people of ʿAin Mallaha did not depend on one food. They took a little from the water, a little from the hills, and a little from the wild grasses. This mix gave them security. Some researchers argue that this security, and the habits of storing and processing plants, helped prepare the ground for true farming later on.

Be honest with yourself about what you can see today. ʿAin Mallaha is an archaeological site, not a reconstructed open-air museum. Much of what was found now sits in collections and laboratories, and the excavated areas may be covered, fenced or overgrown. Access can depend on the season and on local arrangements. The real experience here is the setting itself: the spring, the slope, the view across the valley, and the knowledge of what lies beneath.

So use your imagination as a tool. Look at the gentle slope and picture a ring of low stone walls, each one the base of a hut. Imagine smoke rising from hearths, the sound of stone grinding on stone, children near the water's edge. Look west to the hills, where women and men would have walked out to gather acorns and cut wild barley. Then look east, toward the far ridge of the Golan, catching the light across the valley.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and listen again. The spring is still running. The birds still cross the valley on their long migrations between Europe and Africa, as they did when the first stone walls rose here. Almost everything else has changed — the lake, the forests, the language, the borders. But the reasons people chose this spot are still around you. Water, shelter, open land, and a view of home.
