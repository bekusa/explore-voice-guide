The guide

The story

Stand at the centre of the bridge and rest a hand on the parapet. The granite is cool and slightly rough, flecked with tiny grains that catch the light. Below you, the Thames moves with a weight you can feel more than hear. Gulls call over the water, buses rumble behind you, and on a still morning you might smell mud and river weed rising from the banks. Welcome to Kew Bridge, one of the quieter crossings of west London.

This bridge links two very different places. To the north lies Brentford, an old riverside town with a long working past of wharves, waterworks and industry. To the south lies Kew, with its village green, its church, and the famous botanic gardens just beyond. For centuries the river divided two historic counties here — Middlesex on the Brentford side, Surrey on the Kew side. The bridge you are standing on joined them.

Here is the first surprise. The crossing under your feet is not the first bridge on this spot. It is the third. Before any bridge existed, people crossed here by ferry. Then, in the mid-eighteenth century, a timber bridge was built. Like many private river crossings of that era, it charged a toll. Travellers paid a small sum to avoid the long trip round to another crossing point.

Wooden bridges on a tidal river do not last. Water, ice and rot work on them year after year. Before the end of the eighteenth century, the timber bridge was replaced with a stone one. It was a handsome structure for its time. But London was growing fast, and traffic grew with it. Carts, carriages, and later trams and motor vehicles all needed more room. By the late nineteenth century, the old stone bridge was too narrow for the job.

So a new bridge was planned, and that is the one you see today. It was designed by the engineer John Wolfe Barry, working with his partner Cuthbert Brereton. Wolfe Barry is better known to most visitors for a much larger project downstream — he was the engineer behind Tower Bridge. Here at Kew, his task was simpler and more modest. He needed a strong, wide, dignified road bridge that would carry modern traffic across the river.

The new bridge opened in 1903. King Edward VII and Queen Alexandra came to open it in person, and the bridge was officially named after the King. Its formal name is the King Edward VII Bridge. Almost nobody uses that name today. Locals, map makers and bus drivers simply call it Kew Bridge. It is a small reminder that the names people choose often outlast the names on official papers.

Now walk to one side and look along the outer face of the bridge. You will see three wide arches, shallow and elegant, spanning the water. The bridge is faced in granite, a hard stone that resists weather and river damage far better than softer building stone. Notice how the curves of the arches look smooth and calm. The design is not showy. It was built to work, and to look solid and respectable while doing it.

Take a moment to watch the water itself. This stretch of the Thames is tidal. Twice a day, the tide pushes the river inland, and twice a day it pulls back again. At high tide, the water can rise close to the paths along the banks. At low tide, it drops away to show muddy foreshore, stones, and old timber. If you come back a few hours later, the view under these arches can look like a completely different river.

Now turn and look north, towards the Brentford side. Not far from the bridge, you may spot a tall Victorian tower rising above the rooftops. That tower belongs to an old waterworks, which once pumped water to homes across London. Today the site is the London Museum of Water and Steam. Inside, enormous steam engines are preserved, some of the largest of their kind. It is a second surprise for many visitors — one of the great stories of London's public health sits right beside this bridge.

Next, look south, towards Kew. The road leads past Kew Green, a wide open lawn edged with old houses. On the green stands Saint Anne's Church, a small parish church linked to the royal family who once spent time at Kew. Beyond the green are the Royal Botanic Gardens, a place known around the world for plant science. Many people cross this bridge without realising how close they are to the gardens' gates.

Downstream, to the east along the north bank, a narrow riverside path leads to Strand-on-the-Green. It is a row of old houses and pubs facing the water, with small front gardens and flood steps. Walkers, runners and cyclists use the towpaths on both banks. On many days you may also see rowers in narrow boats, sliding past under the arches, with a coach calling to them from the bank.

Think for a moment about the role this crossing has played. For most of its life, Kew Bridge has been less a landmark and more a working part of daily life. People cross it on the way to work, to school, to the shops, to the gardens. It has carried horses and trams, and now it carries buses, cars and bicycles. Its quiet granite has seen the slow change of London, from a city of horses to a city of engines.

Before you leave, stand still once more and look across the river. Follow the line of the arches down to the water. Listen to the traffic behind you, and the softer sound of the river below. As you walk away, glance back at the bridge from the bank. From there, you can see its full shape — three calm curves of stone, holding two old neighbourhoods together, as they have for well over a century.
