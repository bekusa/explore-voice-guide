The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under your feet, the old floorboards creak. Around you, the light falls through narrow windows set deep in pale stone. It catches the edge of a breastplate, a curved helmet, the polished flank of a carved wooden horse. The air is cool and still. You are inside the White Tower, the oldest building at the Tower of London, and you are standing among the collection of the Royal Armouries.

The Royal Armouries is the national museum of arms and armour for the United Kingdom. Its main galleries today are in Leeds, in the north of England, and it has another site near Portsmouth. But the story of the collection begins here, inside these walls. For centuries, the Tower was a working arsenal. Weapons were made, stored, repaired and issued from this site. The museum grew out of that working store, which is why it is often described as one of the oldest museums in Britain.

Before you look at a single sword, look at the building around you. The White Tower was begun by William the Conqueror, the Norman duke who took the English crown in 1066. He wanted a fortress that would show the people of London exactly who was in charge now. The great keep was finished around the year 1100. Its walls are very thick at the base, and they were built to stand up to siege, not to welcome guests.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The original entrance to the tower was not at ground level. It was set high up, on the first floor, and reached by an outside stair that could be removed or defended. The wooden staircase you probably climbed today follows that old idea. Inside, the Normans used fine pale stone brought across the sea from Caen, in Normandy, for many of the carved details. So part of this English fortress is, quite literally, made of France.

Now turn your attention to the horses. The display known as the Line of Kings is one of the oldest continuous visitor attractions anywhere. It began in the seventeenth century, after the return of the monarchy, when the Stuart kings wanted to remind the public of a long and unbroken royal line. Wooden horses carried suits of armour, and each was presented as the armour of a past king. Some of those labels were more patriotic than accurate. But the spectacle drew crowds for generations.

Look closely at the carved horses themselves. Several of them are historic objects in their own right, carved by skilled craftsmen centuries ago. Notice the shape of the heads, the flare of the nostrils, the careful texture of the manes. They were made to impress, and they were made to last. Many people walk past to reach the armour. Pause instead, and you will see that the sculptors gave each horse its own expression.

The figure who dominates these rooms is Henry the Eighth. Henry loved armour. He set up his own royal workshops at Greenwich, with skilled armourers, many brought from continental Europe, to make suits of the highest quality. The collection holds armour from different stages of his life. One suit belongs to the athletic young prince who loved the tournament. Another belongs to the much heavier king of his later years. Standing between them, you can see a whole life told in steel.

Here is the moment most visitors remember. On one of Henry's later armours, you will notice a very large, very prominent codpiece. It was not unusual for the fashion of the day. But Henry's is notably bold. For centuries, visitors were said to treat it with a kind of cheeky superstition, and there are popular stories about people seeking good fortune from it. Treat those tales as folklore. Still, it is a reminder that armour was also about image, power and display.

That is the real surprise of this collection. Much of the armour here was never meant for the battlefield. Some suits were made for the tournament, where knights rode at each other with lances for sport and glory. Others were made for parades and ceremonies. Look for gilding, for etched patterns, for blued steel that once shone a deep, dark colour. These were the luxury objects of their age, as much about status as protection.

As you walk on, notice how the weapons change. Early displays show swords, staffs and polearms, the long wooden shafts topped with blades and spikes that foot soldiers carried. Later displays show firearms. That change mirrors a slow revolution in warfare. As guns grew more powerful and more common, heavy full armour became less useful. Over time it shrank to breastplates and helmets, and then it largely disappeared from the battlefield altogether.

Take a moment, too, for the Chapel of Saint John, on an upper floor of this building. It is one of the finest surviving Norman church interiors in England. Its style is Romanesque, the rounded-arch architecture of Europe before the pointed Gothic style arrived. There is no armour here. Just thick round columns, plain stone and quiet. After so much steel, the stillness of the chapel feels very deliberate, and it shows the tower was a place of prayer as well as power.

The Tower of London is a whole world of its own, and the armour is only one part of it. Outside, you will find the Crown Jewels, the old walls, the ravens and the Yeoman Warders in their dark uniforms. But the White Tower holds the oldest story. This is where the fortress began. And this is where the long, strange idea of a national collection of weapons and armour first took shape.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Look at the light on the stone, the way it softens the edges of the steel. Listen to the footsteps on the boards, to voices in many languages. People have walked through these rooms, looking at these same horses and helmets, for a very long time. As you step back out into the open air of the Tower, look up at the pale walls above you. You have just been inside the heart of them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Your Tower of London ticket covers the White Tower displays.
Visit the White Tower early before tour groups arrive.
Allow at least an hour for the armour galleries.
Wear comfortable shoes for steep stairs and stone floors.
Save time for the Chapel of Saint John upstairs.
