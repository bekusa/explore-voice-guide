The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement of Stockwell Road for a moment. Listen to the buses grinding past, the voices drifting over from Brixton Road, and the low thud of a sound check leaking through the doors. Look up at the pale, curved front of the building. On a gig night the queue runs along the wall, and the air smells of rain on warm tarmac and food from the stalls nearby. This is Brixton Academy, one of the best-loved music halls in London.

The building did not begin as a rock venue. It opened in 1929 as the Astoria, a large cinema for south London. This was the golden age of the picture palace. Going to the cinema was a night out, and owners wanted to give working people a taste of luxury for the price of a seat. Brixton was a busy shopping district then too, and a grand cinema here made good business sense.

The Astoria was built in what is called an atmospheric style. That means the interior was designed to make you feel you were sitting outdoors, not inside a hall. Instead of a plain ceiling and flat walls, designers created the illusion of an Italian garden at dusk. There were classical facades, balconies, trees and statues around the edges. Above you, the ceiling was painted to suggest an open evening sky.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. That Italianate fantasy is still there. When you walk into the auditorium tonight, look past the lights and the speaker stacks. Along the sides you will see ornate towers, arches and carved details, like the walls of a Mediterranean town. The proscenium arch, the great frame around the stage, still rises above the performers. Few rock venues in the world have a setting quite like this.

The second surprise is under your feet. The main floor slopes down towards the stage. This is a leftover from the cinema days, when the slope helped everyone see the screen over the heads in front. For concerts, it works beautifully. Even if you stand near the back, you can usually see the stage clearly. Musicians often say the sloped floor makes the crowd look like a great wave rolling towards them.

Like many grand cinemas, the Astoria struggled as television arrived in British homes after the war. Audiences shrank, and the building passed through several owners and names. By the 1970s the cinema had closed. For years the hall had an uncertain future. It hosted different uses and some concerts, but it was not yet the venue Londoners know today. A building this large is expensive to heat, repair and run.

The turning point came in the early 1980s. A young promoter named Simon Parkes took over the building. According to his own account, he bought it for a single pound, which tells you how little anyone else wanted it. He and his team set about turning a tired old cinema into a working music hall. It was risky. Brixton at that time had a difficult reputation, and many people doubted that crowds would come.

They did come. Through the 1980s and 1990s the Academy became one of the key stages in London. Punk, reggae, rock, dance and indie acts all played here. The Clash performed in this hall. The Smiths played their final concert in Britain here in 1986, a night still remembered by fans. Later decades brought hip hop, grime, metal and electronic music. The venue holds a crowd of nearly five thousand people, yet it still feels close.

That feeling of closeness is part of the secret. Arenas can feel distant and cold. This hall sits in a sweet spot. It is big enough to mark a real step up in an artist's career, but small enough that the singer can see faces in the crowd. For many bands, selling out Brixton is a moment that says they have truly arrived. Some return again and again for the atmosphere.

The building is protected as a listed building, which means its historic architecture is officially recognised and cannot be changed freely. In recent years it has carried the name of a sponsor, and you may see it called the O2 Academy Brixton. Most Londoners still simply call it Brixton Academy, or just Brixton. The history has not always been easy. In December 2022 a crowd crush outside the doors cost two people their lives, and the venue closed for a long period before reopening with new safety measures.

When you step back outside, take a look at the neighbourhood around the venue. Brixton has been shaped by many communities, especially the Caribbean families who settled here after the Second World War. Their influence runs through the music, the food and the markets. A short walk away, Brixton Market and Electric Avenue fill with stalls selling fruit, fish, spices and fabric. The area's sound system culture is one reason this venue feels so connected to its streets.

Before a show, many people eat nearby rather than inside. The covered market arcades hold small restaurants serving food from around the world. On a summer evening, the pavements outside pubs fill with people in band shirts. After the show, the crowd pours out onto Stockwell Road all at once. It can take a while to reach the Underground station, so many people walk slowly, still talking about the set, ears still ringing.

So take one last look before you move on. Notice the plain brick and pale render of the outside, and think of the painted garden hidden within. Nearly a century ago, people came here to watch films and imagine they were somewhere warmer. Today they come to hear music, and that small escape still happens inside these walls. As you walk away, listen. The buses, the voices, perhaps a distant bass line... this is still Brixton, carrying on.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to queue along the wall on Stockwell Road.
Bring earplugs; the hall gets very loud near the front.
Stand towards the back to use the sloped floor's sightlines.
Eat in Brixton Market's arcades before the show.
Expect a slow walk to the tube after concerts end.
