The guide

The story

Stand on Whitechapel Road for a moment and listen. Buses rumble past. Market traders call out prices for mangoes and fabric. Somewhere a shop shutter rattles open. Then your eye lifts, past the red-brown brick and the shop signs, to a golden dome catching the grey London light. The East London Mosque does not hide behind a quiet square. It sits right on one of the oldest and busiest roads in the East End, and the city flows around it all day long.

This is one of the largest mosques in Britain, and one of the most used. On an ordinary weekday, people step in and out between errands. On a Friday, the pavement outside fills with worshippers who arrive from offices, schools, and market stalls. The building is not a museum piece. It is a working place of prayer, learning, and community life. As you look at it, you are looking at something that thousands of Londoners treat as part of their weekly routine.

The story begins long before this building existed. In the early twentieth century, a small group of Muslims and supporters in London began raising money for a mosque in the capital. At that time, the Muslim population of the city was small and scattered. Many were sailors, students, and traders connected to the British Empire. For decades, the community prayed wherever it could... in rented rooms, borrowed halls, and private homes.

During the Second World War, the community finally secured premises of its own. It took over a set of terraced houses on Commercial Road, not far from where you are standing. They were ordinary houses, converted for worship. Imagine prayer rugs laid out in narrow rooms, with the sounds of a city at war outside. For more than forty years, those modest houses served as the community's mosque.

Then the neighbourhood changed. From the 1960s and 1970s onward, large numbers of families from Bangladesh, and before that from what was then East Pakistan, settled in Tower Hamlets. Many came from the Sylhet region in the north-east of the country. They worked in the garment trade, in restaurants, and in factories. The old houses on Commercial Road became far too small. The community needed a purpose-built home, and it chose this site on Whitechapel Road.

The building you see now opened in 1985. Look at the materials. It is built of warm brick, the same material as the Victorian terraces and warehouses all around it. This was a deliberate kind of conversation with the street. The arches and the dome speak an Islamic architectural language, but the brick ties the mosque to the East End. It looks like it belongs here — because, in a real sense, it was built from the same stuff as its neighbours.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. Soon after it opened, this mosque became one of the first in Britain to receive permission to broadcast the call to prayer, the adhan, through loudspeakers. The adhan is the spoken announcement that invites Muslims to prayer five times each day. If you are here at the right moment, you may hear it rise above the traffic. For a few minutes, the sound of the street changes.

The mosque did not stop growing in 1985. Next to it stands the London Muslim Centre, opened in the early 2000s. It added prayer halls, classrooms, offices, and space for community services. Later, another extension, the Maryam Centre, created dedicated space for women, including prayer areas and facilities for classes and support services. Together, these buildings form a single complex that stretches along the street. From the pavement, try to spot where one phase ends and the next begins.

Now think about the ground beneath your feet. Whitechapel has been a place of arrival for centuries. In the seventeenth century, French Protestant refugees called Huguenots settled nearby and worked as silk weavers. In the late nineteenth century, Jewish families fleeing persecution in Eastern Europe filled these streets. Synagogues, kosher shops, and Yiddish theatres lined the area. Then came the Bengali community. Each group arrived, built its places of worship, and left its mark on the brick and the street names.

There is a second surprise just around the corner. Directly behind the mosque, on Fieldgate Street, stood a small synagogue that served the Jewish community for many years. For a long time, the two places of worship sat back to back, sharing a wall in all but name. As the Jewish population moved away from the East End, the synagogue's congregation dwindled. That closeness of mosque and synagogue tells the story of this neighbourhood better than any plaque could.

If you walk a few minutes west, you reach Altab Ali Park. It is named after a young Bangladeshi garment worker who was killed in a racist attack nearby in 1978. His death led to large protests and became a turning point for the community's confidence and political voice. A short walk further brings you to Brick Lane, with its curry houses, vintage shops, and street art. The mosque sits at the heart of this whole web of streets and memories.

If you would like to go inside, remember that this is an active place of worship. Dress modestly, with arms and legs covered. Women are usually asked to cover their hair, so carry a scarf. You will remove your shoes before entering the prayer halls. Avoid arriving during congregational prayers, especially on Friday around midday, when the halls are full. The mosque has welcomed school groups and visitors of all faiths, so it is worth checking ahead about arranged visits.

Inside, the mood shifts. The noise of Whitechapel Road fades. Carpets soften every footstep. Light falls across wide, open prayer halls with no pews or chairs, because worshippers stand, bow, and kneel together on the floor. Look for the mihrab, the niche in the wall that shows the direction of Mecca. Everyone in the room faces the same way. That simple orientation links this hall in East London to mosques across the world.

Before you leave, step back onto the pavement and look up once more at the golden dome. Then look along the road at the market stalls, the hospital, the shopfronts in Bengali and English. This mosque grew out of rented rooms and terraced houses into one of the largest Muslim centres in the country. Yet it still stands shoulder to shoulder with the old East End. As you walk on, listen to the street. Many languages, many histories... all sharing the same stretch of brick and pavement.

Key facts

Before you go

Practical tips

Avoid Friday midday, when congregational prayers fill every hall.
Carry a scarf so you can cover your hair inside.
Wear clothing that covers arms and legs before entering.
Contact the mosque ahead to arrange a group visit.
Walk west afterwards to Altab Ali Park and Brick Lane.
