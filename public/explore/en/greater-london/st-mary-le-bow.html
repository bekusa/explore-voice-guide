The guide

The story

Stand on Cheapside for a moment and listen. Buses hum past. Office workers hurry by with coffee in hand. Then, above all of it, a pale stone steeple rises in stacked tiers, with a gilded dragon turning slowly at the very top. This is St Mary-le-Bow. For centuries, the sound of its bells defined who counted as a true Londoner. Before we go inside, take a breath and look up. That steeple is where our story begins.

You have probably heard the old saying. A true Cockney, the tradition goes, is someone born within the sound of Bow Bells. In a quiet medieval city, those bells carried a long way across the rooftops. Today, with traffic and glass towers, their reach is much smaller. Some people joke that very few babies are now born close enough to qualify. Still, the idea holds on. These bells became a kind of audible border for working-class London identity.

The name of the church has a surprising origin. It has nothing to do with archery or ribbons. The word bow here means arch. Beneath your feet lies a Norman crypt, built in the eleventh century by the French-speaking rulers who arrived after 1066. Its stone arches were unusual in London at the time. People noticed them, and the church took its name from them. So the oldest thing about this building is hidden underground, not up in that steeple.

Those same arches gave their name to something else. The Court of Arches is the church court of the Archbishop of Canterbury, the senior bishop of the Church of England. It took its name from this crypt, where it met in the Middle Ages. The court still has a connection to this building today. New bishops of London have their election confirmed here. It is a quiet, legal side of the church that most passing visitors never hear about.

The medieval church had a dramatic life. Chroniclers recorded that in 1091 a violent storm, often described as a tornado, tore the roof from the building. It is one of the earliest storms of its kind written down in England. Later, in the Middle Ages, the bells here also rang a curfew in the evening. That bell told citizens it was time to stop work and head home. Imagine that sound rolling down a street of timber houses and market stalls.

That street is Cheapside, and it matters to this story. In medieval London, Cheapside was the great market street of the city. The word cheap comes from an old English word for buying and selling. Look at the names of the lanes around you: Bread Street, Milk Street, Honey Lane. They remember what was once sold here. The church stood in the middle of daily trade, noise and gossip, and its bells marked the rhythm of the day.

Now for the most famous tale linked to these bells. According to the popular legend, a poor boy called Dick Whittington gave up on London and walked away north. Resting on a hill, he heard the Bow Bells ringing. Locals say they seemed to call, turn again, Whittington. He returned, and became Lord Mayor. A real Richard Whittington did serve as mayor in the Middle Ages. But the bells calling him back belong to folklore, not to the historical record.

In 1666, the Great Fire of London destroyed this church along with most of the old city. The task of rebuilding fell to Sir Christopher Wren, the architect who also designed Saint Paul's Cathedral, just a short walk west of here. Wren rebuilt dozens of city churches, but this steeple was one of his most ambitious. Look at it again from the street. It climbs in layers of columns and curved stone brackets, growing lighter as it rises toward that dragon.

The second great disaster came in the Second World War. In 1941, during the Blitz, German bombing set the church on fire. The bells crashed down and were broken. Only the outer walls and Wren's steeple survived. Here is a detail many visitors miss. During the war, the BBC used a recording of Bow Bells as a sound signal in broadcasts to occupied Europe. For listeners far away, the sound of these bells meant London was still there.

After the war, the church was slowly rebuilt and reconsecrated in the 1960s. New bells were cast and hung in the old tower. When you step inside, you will notice that the interior feels bright and modern compared with the old stone outside. The colourful stained glass windows date from this post-war rebuilding, and they are the work of the artist John Hayward. Take a moment to look at how the glass changes the colour of light on the floor.

There is another unusual feature inside. The church has two pulpits facing each other, the raised platforms from which a preacher speaks. This arrangement allows two speakers to hold a public conversation, one on each side, with the audience between them. The church has hosted talks and debates in this way. It fits the spirit of Cheapside, a street that was always full of argument and trade. Here, conversation itself became part of the life of the building.

Outside again, find the small churchyard beside the church. Here stands a statue of Captain John Smith, the English adventurer linked with the early English settlement at Jamestown in Virginia. He lived in this parish. It is a strange thing to find in the heart of the City of London: a reminder that people from these streets helped shape places on the other side of the ocean. Stand beside the statue and look back up at the steeple from this quieter angle.

As you leave, pause one more time on Cheapside. Around you, glass offices and shops now fill the old market street. Yet the shape of the lanes, the names on the signs, and this tall pale steeple all carry traces of the medieval city. If you are lucky, you may hear the bells ring. Listen to them carefully. That same sound once marked the edge of what it meant to belong to London.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cross to the far side of Cheapside to see the whole steeple.
Visit the crypt to stand among the oldest stonework.
Check the church calendar for lunchtime talks or bell ringing.
Walk nearby Bread Street and Milk Street for market history.
Head west afterwards for a short walk to Saint Paul's Cathedral.
