The guide

The story

Lean in close to the glass. The surface in front of you is dark, almost black, and polished smooth in places. Then your eye catches something strange. A ring of rough grooves radiates out from a square hole at the centre, like the spokes of a broken wheel. Around those scars, faint columns of hieroglyphs survive. This is the Shabaka Stone, and it carries one of the most unusual texts to survive from ancient Egypt.

You are standing among the Egyptian sculpture galleries of the British Museum, in the heart of Bloomsbury. Around you rise colossal kings, granite sarcophagi and carved gods. Many visitors walk past this slab without stopping. It is flat, damaged and modest in size. But scholars have argued over its words for more than a century. This dark stone holds an ancient Egyptian idea about how the world itself began.

First, the king whose name it carries. Shabaka ruled Egypt around 700 BCE. He was not from the Nile Delta or from Thebes. He came from Kush, the powerful kingdom to the south, in what is now Sudan. His family conquered Egypt and ruled as pharaohs. Historians call them the Twenty-fifth Dynasty. These Kushite kings deeply respected Egypt's ancient past. They restored temples, revived old traditions and often copied much older styles of art and writing.

The stone tells us its own origin story. At the top, the inscription explains that Shabaka found an ancient text in the temple of the god Ptah at Memphis. The text had been eaten by worms. It could no longer be read from beginning to end. So the king ordered it copied onto stone, to preserve it forever. It is a rare moment in ancient history. A ruler is openly rescuing a damaged document from decay.

But here is the first surprise. Many modern scholars doubt that story. The language on the stone looks very old, almost like Old Kingdom writing from about two thousand years earlier. For a long time, people believed the text really was that ancient. Today, many experts think the text was written in Shabaka's own time, in a deliberately old-fashioned style. The worm-eaten papyrus may have been a way to give new ideas the authority of great age.

So what does the text actually say? Part of it tells a familiar Egyptian myth. The god Geb judges a quarrel between Horus and Seth, two rival gods. At first he divides Egypt between them. Then he gives the whole land to Horus. The story ties together Upper and Lower Egypt, the south and the north. And it places the city of Memphis at the centre of that union.

The most famous section is something different. Scholars call it the Memphite Theology. In it, the god Ptah, the patron of Memphis and of craftsmen, creates the world. He does not shape it with his hands. He creates it with thought and speech. The text says that things came into being through the heart and the tongue. For ancient Egyptians, the heart was the place of thinking, and the tongue was the tool of speaking.

Pause on that idea for a moment. A god thinks of something, names it, and it exists. Visitors who know the opening of the Gospel of John, where the Word comes first, often notice the echo. Scholars are careful not to claim a direct link between the two. But the parallel is striking. Long before Greek philosophers wrote about reason and the spoken word, this Egyptian text described creation as an act of the mind.

Now look again at those grooves and the square hole. Here is the second surprise. At some point after ancient times, this sacred inscription was turned into a millstone. Someone cut a socket into the centre and used the slab to grind grain, or perhaps other materials. The turning stone wore away the middle of the text. Whole lines of hieroglyphs simply vanished under the friction. The damage you see is the mark of practical, everyday work.

The columns themselves show another kind of damage too. In several places, parts of Shabaka's royal names were deliberately chiselled out. Kings who came after the Kushite dynasty sometimes tried to erase their predecessors from public memory. So this single object carries two layers of loss. One was political, aimed at a ruler's name. The other was purely practical, aimed at nothing at all. Both changed what we can read today.

How did the stone reach London? It was given to the British Museum in 1805 by George John, the second Earl Spencer, an English aristocrat and a keen collector. Its earlier journey from Egypt is not fully documented. For decades after it arrived, nobody could read it properly. Hieroglyphs were still a mystery in 1805. Only after the decipherment in the 1820s did scholars slowly begin to understand what this battered slab really said.

That story connects it with a far more famous neighbour. The Rosetta Stone, which helped unlock hieroglyphs, is displayed in these same galleries. Crowds gather around it all day. The Shabaka Stone usually gets a quieter audience. Yet the two objects belong to one story. One gave scholars the key to the script. The other gave them one of the deepest ideas that script ever recorded. Standing here, you see both halves of that achievement.

Take a final look before you move on. Notice how the surviving hieroglyphs run in neat vertical columns along the edges, far from the worn centre. Notice the deep cut of the square socket, and the grooves spreading outward from it. Somewhere in that missing middle were words about gods, cities and creation. Then turn back towards the gallery, with its giant statues and its moving crowds. Every voice around you, every spoken word, is a small act of making things real.
