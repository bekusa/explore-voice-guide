The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close, a river boat churns against the pier, and gulls call over the grey water of the Thames. Underfoot, the ground changes from smooth paving to old setts, worn by carts and boots. Around you stand long brick buildings with tall windows and solid doors. Today they hold flats, cafés and offices. For much of three centuries, though, this ground made the weapons of an empire. Welcome to the Royal Arsenal, in Woolwich, in south-east London.

The site began as a place called the Warren. In the late seventeenth century, the state started to use this stretch of riverbank to store and test military supplies. The location made sense. Ships could load cargo straight from the Thames, and the marshes downstream offered empty land for testing guns. Over the following decades, storage grew into manufacture. Workshops, foundries and laboratories spread across the riverside. In 1805, during a visit by King George the Third, the Warren took the grander name you know today: the Royal Arsenal.

In the early eighteenth century, the army needed a reliable place to cast bronze cannon. Casting molten metal was dangerous work, and the result had to be perfect. A flawed barrel could burst and kill its own crew. So a purpose-built brass foundry rose here. The Royal Brass Foundry still stands, a handsome brick building with a grand doorway. Its design is often linked to the circle of the architect Sir John Vanbrugh, though the exact authorship is debated by historians.

The Arsenal did not stand alone. Woolwich became a military town in every sense. The Royal Artillery, the army's gunners, formed here in the early eighteenth century. The Royal Military Academy, which trained officers in gunnery and engineering, also had its first home inside the Arsenal grounds. So young officers studied mathematics within earshot of the foundries that cast their guns. Theory and practice sat side by side. The town grew around this single purpose: equipping Britain's forces on land and at sea.

Now for the first surprise. Look for the name Dial Square. It refers to a sundial that once marked a workshop entrance here. In 1886, a group of workers from these workshops started a football team. They called it Dial Square. The club soon became Royal Arsenal, then Woolwich Arsenal. In 1913 it moved north across the river to Highbury. Today the world knows it simply as Arsenal. The cannon on the club badge is a direct link to this riverside.

Through the nineteenth century, the Arsenal expanded with every war and every new technology. Iron replaced bronze. Rifled barrels replaced smooth ones. Shells, cartridges, fuses and gun carriages all came off its production lines. The site grew into a small city of its own, with internal railways, docks and workshops stretching far along the riverbank. It was one of the largest industrial sites in Britain. Few visitors today realise how far east the old works once reached.

The First World War brought the Arsenal to its peak. The demand for shells was enormous, and the workforce grew to many tens of thousands. Large numbers of these workers were women. They filled shells with explosives in conditions that were often hazardous. Many who handled the chemical TNT found their skin turned yellow, and they earned the nickname Canaries. Their labour kept the guns firing on the Western Front, at a real cost to their own health.

The Second World War brought danger directly to the site. As a vital munitions centre, the Arsenal was a clear target for bombing raids. Production was also spread out to new factories elsewhere in Britain, so that one strike could not halt supply. After the war, the need for a vast arsenal on the edge of London faded. Manufacturing wound down, and the Royal Ordnance Factory here closed in the late 1960s. The Ministry of Defence finally left the remaining site in the 1990s.

Then came a long, quiet period. Fine Georgian buildings stood empty behind high walls, while the town outside lost its biggest employer. Woolwich felt the loss deeply. Redevelopment began around the turn of the millennium. Historic workshops were restored and converted, and new apartment blocks rose between them. Walk slowly and you can read the layers: old brick next to fresh glass, and military gateways now open to the public.

Here is the second surprise. Look out for a group of cast-iron figures standing together on the open ground. This sculpture is called Assembly, by the artist Peter Burke. The figures are hollow and have no heads, which can feel strange at first. Many people read them as a quiet tribute to the thousands of anonymous workers who passed through these gates. Stand among them for a moment. They are roughly human height, so you become part of the crowd.

The riverside is the best place to understand why the Arsenal was here at all. Walk to the water's edge and look across the Thames. Boats once moored here to carry guns and stores out to the fleet. Today a pier serves the river bus, linking Woolwich with Greenwich and central London. The Thames is wide at this point, and the tide moves fast. On a clear day, the towers of the Docklands rise upstream.

The Arsenal is also easy to reach from below ground. The Elizabeth line, opened in 2022, has a station on the edge of the site. Trains run under the river and through the heart of the capital. This connection changed how people think about Woolwich. A place once defined by its walls is now one of the quicker journeys into central London. The new station sits on ground that once served the old works.

As you wander, notice the mix of life around you. Residents walk dogs past cannon displayed as ornaments. Children play on lawns where carts once carried shells. Some buildings now host a theatre and creative workspaces. The tone of the place has turned from secrecy to openness, but the old structure is still visible. The long ranges, the gateways and the straight internal roads all follow the plan of a working factory.

Before you leave, pause and look back along one of these straight avenues. Picture the noise that once filled them... the hiss of furnaces, the clatter of carts, thousands of footsteps at the change of shift. Then listen to the present. A café door opens. A river boat sounds its horn. Pigeons settle on a brick cornice. The weapons have gone, but the buildings that made them remain, and the river keeps moving past them, as it always has.

Practical tips

Arrive by river bus for the best approach to the site.
Use the Elizabeth line station for a quick link to central London.
Walk the Thames path east and west from the pier.
Visit on a clear afternoon for long views upstream to Docklands.
Combine your visit with a walk through Woolwich town centre.
