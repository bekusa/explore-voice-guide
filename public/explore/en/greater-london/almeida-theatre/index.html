The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, Upper Street hums with buses, cafe chatter and the clink of glasses. Here, a few steps down a side street, the noise drops away. In front of you stands a pale, classical facade with tall windows and a calm, symmetrical face. It looks more like a lecture hall than a playhouse. That impression is closer to the truth than you might think. This is the Almeida Theatre, one of the most closely watched small stages in London.

The building was not made for drama. It went up in 1837, the year Victoria came to the throne. It was built for the Islington Literary and Scientific Institution, a society for self-improvement. Back then, curious Londoners came here for lectures, a library, and talks on science and ideas. Islington was still growing from a village on the edge of the city into a crowded suburb. A place like this marked a neighbourhood that wanted to be taken seriously.

Take a look at the name on the street sign. Almeida is not an English word. It is the name of a fortress town in Portugal, near the Spanish border. That town was fought over during the Napoleonic Wars, when British troops campaigned across Spain and Portugal. The street, and later the theatre, carry its name. So a small corner of Islington remembers a hilltop citadel more than a thousand kilometres away. Most people who come here for a play never notice.

The institution did not last forever. Over the following century, the building passed through several lives. For a long period it served the Salvation Army, the Christian movement known for its brass bands and street missions. Later it stood in use as a showroom and warehouse, and then it fell into neglect. By the 1970s, the old hall was tired and close to being lost. Many buildings like it across London were simply demolished.

The rescue came from an unexpected direction. Pierre Audi, a young director born in Lebanon, saw the empty hall and imagined a theatre inside it. He led the effort to restore the building and open it as a performance space. The Almeida began staging work around 1980. In its early years it became known for festivals of new music and bold international work. It was never a commercial West End house. From the start, it was a place for risk.

Step inside, if you can, and the surprise of the building appears. The auditorium is intimate, with a little over three hundred seats. They curve around the stage in a tight horseshoe. Behind the acting area, the back wall is often left as bare, old brick. Designers frequently use that wall as part of the set rather than hiding it. The result feels raw and close. Actors stand only a few metres from the front row, and you can hear every breath.

In the 1990s, the theatre's reputation grew far beyond Islington. Under the joint leadership of Jonathan Kent and Ian McDiarmid, the Almeida drew major stars to this small room. Some of the best-known actors in the English-speaking world played here. Ralph Fiennes appeared as Hamlet in a production that later transferred to Broadway. Suddenly, a converted lecture hall in north London was sending work to New York. People queued in this side street for a seat.

Here is something many visitors miss. In the early 2000s, the building closed for a major refurbishment. The company did not stop working. Instead, it moved into temporary spaces elsewhere in London, including a converted former bus garage near King's Cross. Audiences followed the plays into these rough, industrial rooms. That habit of making theatre in unlikely places says a lot about the Almeida. The building matters, but the company matters more.

In more recent years, the Almeida has become one of the main birthplaces of new British plays. Mike Bartlett's King Charles III, written partly in verse like Shakespeare, premiered here before moving to the West End and Broadway. Chimerica, a play about a photograph from Tiananmen Square, began here too. So did Ink, James Graham's play about the early days of a famous tabloid newspaper. Classic plays are also reinvented on this stage, often in radical modern form.

That mix is the theatre's personality. On one night you might see an ancient Greek tragedy stripped down and set in a modern kitchen. On another, a brand-new play about politics, money or family. Critics from national newspapers come here regularly, because what starts on this small stage often travels. For a theatre of this size, the reach is unusual. A production seen by a few hundred people a night here can end up in front of audiences around the world.

Now look around the street itself. Almeida Street is narrow and residential, lined with brick terraces. Upper Street, just behind you, is one of Islington's main roads, full of restaurants, pubs and small shops. Before a performance, the pavement outside the theatre fills with people holding drinks and reading programmes. The theatre has its own bar and cafe, and on warm evenings the crowd spills outside. It feels less like a grand night out and more like a gathering of neighbours.

If you return in the evening, watch the light. When the sun goes down, the windows of the facade glow warm against the darker street. The stucco front catches the streetlamps and turns soft and creamy. Just before the show, a bell or announcement calls people in, and the pavement empties within minutes. Then the street goes quiet again. Somewhere behind those walls, a few hundred people are sitting very still in the dark.

Before you move on, take one more look at the front of the building. It has been a hall of lectures, a home for religious meetings, a warehouse, a ruin, and now a theatre. The walls carry all of those lives, even if the paint hides most of them. As you walk back toward Upper Street, the traffic noise will rise again around you. Listen for that change. It marks the edge of a small, quiet room where London keeps trying out new stories.
