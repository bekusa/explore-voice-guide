The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the column for a moment and tilt your head back. The pale stone rises straight up from the pavement, ribbed with long vertical grooves, until it ends in a burst of gold. Around you, traffic hums along King William Street and office workers cut past with coffee cups. But look up again. That gold is not a statue or a cross. It is a bronze urn of flames, catching whatever light the London sky allows.

This is the Monument to the Great Fire of London. Locals simply call it the Monument, and the nearby Underground station takes its name. It marks the disaster of September 1666, when a fire broke out in a bakery on Pudding Lane, just a short walk from where you are standing. Over four days the flames spread through a city of timber houses, narrow lanes and riverside warehouses packed with oil, pitch and coal.

The scale of the loss is hard to picture. The fire destroyed roughly thirteen thousand houses and close to ninety parish churches. Old Saint Paul's Cathedral, a huge medieval building on the hill to the west, burned and collapsed. The official count of deaths was very small, only a handful of people. Many historians now suspect the true figure was higher, because the poor and the unrecorded left little trace in the written sources.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. The column is 202 feet tall, a little over sixty metres. And the bakery on Pudding Lane stood 202 feet from its base. So the height and the distance are the same. If the column could fall eastward, its tip would land roughly where the fire began. The builders turned a measurement into a memorial — a silent pointer to the spot.

The design came from two remarkable men. Sir Christopher Wren, the architect who later rebuilt Saint Paul's Cathedral, led the rebuilding of the city's churches. His close colleague Robert Hooke was a scientist, inventor and surveyor. Both men worked on the Monument, and historians usually credit Hooke with much of the final design. Construction ran through the 1670s, a decade when much of the City was still a building site.

The column follows the Doric order. That is the plainest of the classical styles from ancient Greece and Rome, with simple capitals and strong proportions. It is built from Portland stone, a pale limestone from the south coast of England. Portland stone became the face of the rebuilt city. When you walk past Saint Paul's and many City churches later, you will see the same creamy grey stone, softened and darkened by centuries of rain and smoke.

Now the second surprise. The Monument was also designed as a scientific instrument. Hooke planned the hollow centre of the column as a giant vertical tube. It could serve as a zenith telescope, pointing straight up at the stars, and as a space for experiments with falling objects and air pressure. There is even a small chamber below ground. The idea did not work well. Vibrations from the busy streets around it disturbed the delicate measurements.

Walk around the base and look at the large square pedestal. Its sides carry long Latin inscriptions. They describe the fire, the destruction and the rebuilding of the city under King Charles the Second. On the west side, a carved relief shows the king and his brother, surrounded by allegorical figures, including a figure of the ruined city slumped on the ground. The sculptor was Caius Gabriel Cibber, a Danish-born artist working in London.

Those inscriptions carry a darker story as well. In the years after the fire, many Londoners blamed Catholics, despite no real evidence. During a period of strong anti-Catholic feeling, a line was added to the inscription that accused them directly of starting the blaze. It stayed on the stone for well over a century. It was finally removed in the nineteenth century, after laws against Catholics were relaxed. You can still see where the old wording was cut away.

Inside, a narrow stone staircase spirals up through the column. There are 311 steps and no lift. The stairs turn tightly around the hollow centre, so the climb can feel dizzying. Take it slowly, and use the small landings to catch your breath. The stone walls keep the air cool, and you will hear the footsteps of other climbers echoing above and below you.

At the top, a small viewing gallery wraps around the column, just below the golden urn. Today a metal mesh cage encloses it. That cage was added in the nineteenth century, after several people fell or jumped from the gallery. From up here, you look across a city that the fire forced to rebuild. Glass towers now rise all around, but you can still trace the line of the Thames and pick out church spires below.

Think about what this view meant when the Monument was new. Back then, it was one of the tallest structures in London, and its gallery gave people a rare look over rooftops. Today the towers of the modern City stand much higher. The column has become a small figure among giants. Yet it still holds its place. Climbers traditionally receive a certificate to prove they made it to the top, a small reward that many children treasure.

When you come back down to the street, take one more look upward at the gilded urn. Then walk a few steps east toward Pudding Lane. The street is quiet and ordinary now, lined with offices. Nothing about it suggests the disaster that began there. That is why this column matters. It keeps a memory alive in a place that has otherwise moved on — and it points, quite exactly, to where it all started.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the 311 steps slowly, using landings to rest.
Climbing the column requires a separate admission ticket.
Wear comfortable shoes for the narrow spiral staircase.
Walk east afterwards to see Pudding Lane itself.
Ask for your climber's certificate at the bottom.
