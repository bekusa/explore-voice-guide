The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, a train rattles along the line, and cars hiss past on a wide suburban road. In front of you, a pale dome rises above rooftops and car parks, framed by slender minarets. It is an unexpected sight here in Morden, on the southern edge of London. Semi-detached houses, bus stops and corner shops sit all around. And then, quite suddenly, this building appears, calm and bright against the grey or blue of the London sky.

This is Baitul Futuh Mosque. The name comes from Arabic and is usually translated as the House of Victories. The victories in question are not military ones. The community that built this mosque describes them as spiritual victories, the winning of hearts through faith and good conduct. When it opened in 2003, the mosque was widely described as one of the largest in Western Europe. That claim still surprises many visitors, who expect such a building in central London, not out here in the suburbs.

The mosque belongs to the Ahmadiyya Muslim Community. This is a movement within Islam that began in British India in the late nineteenth century. Its followers believe that their founder, Mirza Ghulam Ahmad, was a reformer sent to renew the faith. The community is led by a spiritual head called the Khalifa, or Caliph. Many other Muslims reject Ahmadi beliefs, and in some countries Ahmadis face serious legal restrictions and persecution. That background helps explain why this building matters so much to the people who use it.

Here is the first surprise. In 1984, the fourth Khalifa of the community, Mirza Tahir Ahmad, left Pakistan after new laws made it a crime for Ahmadis to call themselves Muslims. He moved to London. For decades after that, the worldwide leadership of the movement was based in this city. So a community with followers across Africa, Asia and beyond was guided from a corner of south-west London. Baitul Futuh became one of the most important centres of that global network.

The Ahmadiyya connection to London is older than many people realise. A few kilometres north of here, in Southfields, stands the Fazl Mosque. It opened in the 1920s and is often described as the first purpose-built mosque in London. So when the community began planning a much larger mosque, they were building on a long history in the capital. Baitul Futuh was their answer to a growing congregation that the smaller mosque could no longer hold.

Now look at the ground beneath your feet. This site was not always sacred. It was once a dairy, a working industrial plant run by Express Dairies. Milk lorries once came and went where worshippers now arrive for Friday prayers. The community bought the land and transformed it. Mirza Tahir Ahmad laid the foundation stone, and he opened the finished mosque in October 2003. The construction was paid for largely through donations from community members, many of whom gave small amounts over years.

Let your eye travel up to the dome. It is the clear focal point of the complex, smooth and rounded, catching the light in a way that changes through the day. On an overcast morning it looks soft and pale. In low evening sun it can glow almost gold. The minarets beside it are tall and slim. In many mosques, minarets were once used to call people to prayer. Here they mostly act as a visual signal, marking the building as a place of worship.

The design mixes traditional Islamic forms with clean, modern lines. You will see arches, geometric patterns and calligraphy, but the overall effect is restrained rather than ornate. Inside, the prayer halls are wide, open spaces with soft carpet underfoot. There are separate halls for men and women, which is common practice in mosques around the world. Thousands of people can pray here at once, and on major festivals the halls and grounds fill with families from across Britain.

As you walk around, you may notice a short phrase displayed on signs and banners. It reads: Love for All, Hatred for None. This is the motto of the Ahmadiyya community, and you will find it at their mosques and events worldwide. It is a simple line, but it sits at the heart of how the community presents itself to its neighbours. Here in Morden, it greets commuters, dog walkers and shoppers as they pass by every day.

The second surprise is how much of this complex is not a mosque at all. Baitul Futuh includes offices, meeting rooms, a library and spaces for community events. Over the years it has hosted interfaith gatherings, charity drives and a yearly event called the Peace Symposium. Politicians, faith leaders and diplomats have attended, alongside local residents. The building works less like a quiet sanctuary and more like a busy civic centre with a prayer hall at its core.

The mosque has also faced hardship. In 2015, a large fire broke out in one of the buildings in the complex. Smoke rose high over south London, and many firefighters worked to bring it under control. The main prayer area survived, but parts of the site were badly damaged. The community set about rebuilding, again relying on donations from its members. If you notice newer sections of the complex, that rebuilding is part of the reason.

Visitors of any faith or none are generally welcome here, especially at open days and organised tours. If you go inside, dress modestly and expect to remove your shoes before entering the prayer halls. Volunteers are often happy to answer questions, whether about the building, the community or Islam more broadly. Many visitors say that these conversations are the most memorable part of the visit. A respectful question is usually met with a warm, patient answer.

Before you leave, take one last look from the road. Notice how the dome sits above the ordinary roofs, the bus shelters, the passing traffic. This is a place where a global story and a local street meet. The trains will keep running behind you, and the shops will keep opening their shutters. And the mosque will stay where it is, a former dairy turned into a place of prayer, quietly part of everyday life in Morden.
