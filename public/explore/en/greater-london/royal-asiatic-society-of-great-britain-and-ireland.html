The guide

The story

Step away from the roar of Euston Road, and the city changes its voice. Buses fade behind you. A side street opens up, plain and almost ordinary. Brick, glass, railings, a few bicycles chained nearby. Nothing here tells you that behind one of these doors sit two centuries of curiosity about Asia. Manuscripts, paintings and letters are kept here, along with arguments about languages and empires. This is the Royal Asiatic Society of Great Britain and Ireland, and it is worth slowing down for.

Let us start with a surprise. Many people imagine a learned society like this in a grand stucco palace on a Georgian square. Instead, the Society works from a modest building near one of London's busiest railway stations. That choice says something. This place is meant for work, not show. Researchers come here to read, to compare texts, and to ask questions. They rarely come to admire a marble staircase. The real treasures are quiet, flat and fragile, and they live on shelves and in boxes.

The story begins in 1823. That year, a group of scholars and former officials met in London to found a society for the study of Asia. A central figure was Henry Thomas Colebrooke, a scholar of Sanskrit. Sanskrit is the classical language of ancient India, and the language of many Hindu and Buddhist texts. Colebrooke had spent many years in India, and he wanted Britain to take Asian learning seriously. A royal charter followed soon after, and the word Royal entered the name.

Colebrooke and his colleagues did not invent the idea. Decades earlier, in 1784, the scholar William Jones had founded the Asiatic Society in Calcutta, in Bengal. Jones is remembered for noticing deep links between Sanskrit, Greek and Latin. That observation helped to shape the modern study of language families. The London society grew in that same spirit. Its founders wanted to collect, translate and understand. They wanted to bring Asian literature, science, religion and law into conversation with European scholarship.

It is important to be honest about that world. The Society was born during the expansion of British power in Asia, especially through the East India Company. Many early members were soldiers, administrators and merchants as well as scholars. Some objects and documents reached London through that system of trade and rule. Today, historians read these collections with that context in mind. The knowledge gathered here is real and valuable. But it also carries the marks of the unequal relationships that produced it.

So what is actually inside? The Society holds a library and archive built up over two centuries. There are manuscripts in many Asian languages, including Persian, Arabic and Sanskrit. There are drawings and paintings, many made by Asian artists. There are maps, rare printed books and photographs. There are also the private papers of travellers and scholars. Their letters and notebooks show how people once tried to make sense of places far from home. It is a working collection, consulted by specialists from around the world.

One of the Society's longest traditions is publishing. For generations, it has produced a scholarly journal, the Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society. Its pages have carried research on archaeology, history, literature and religion across Asia. Think about the range of that. One issue might discuss an inscription from the Middle East. The next article might discuss a Chinese poem or a Japanese temple. For a reader in the nineteenth century, this journal opened a window onto worlds most would never visit.

Here is a second detail most visitors miss. The Society did not stay inside London. Over time, related societies and branches grew in several parts of Asia. Some became independent institutions with their own long histories, in places such as Hong Kong, Korea and Malaysia. In each city, local members gathered for lectures, published research, and built their own libraries. So this quiet building near Euston is one point in a much wider network. That network still links scholars across many countries.

Now look at the building and its street again. The architecture here is practical, not theatrical. Look for clean lines, large windows and sensible brick. It looks like a modern office, not a museum. That suits the Society's purpose. Natural light matters for reading. Stable conditions matter for paper, ink and pigment, which can fade or crack over time. Behind the walls, the real effort goes into care. That means careful storage, cataloguing and conservation, so a fragile page can still be read in another hundred years.

The neighbourhood adds its own layer. This corner of London has long been a district of learning. University buildings, libraries and research institutes stand within walking distance. The British Library is close by, just along Euston Road. Students hurry past with laptops and coffee. Trains rumble into the great stations nearby, carrying people from the north of England and Scotland. Standing here, you feel two kinds of movement at once. There is the fast pace of travel, and there is the slow pace of study.

If you want to experience the Society more fully, the best way is through its public programme. It regularly hosts lectures and talks on Asian history, art and culture. Some events are open to visitors, and many talks are also shared online. The library and archive are mainly for members and researchers, so access usually needs planning in advance. Do not expect to wander in like a gallery. Contact the Society before you visit, and explain what you hope to see.

It is worth thinking about what membership has meant over the years. For much of its history, this was a society where amateurs and professionals sat side by side. A retired official might share a room with a university professor. A young student might debate a well-known translator. That mix gave the Society its energy. Today its members come from many backgrounds and many countries, including from across Asia itself. The conversation that began in 1823 now includes many more voices.

Before you leave, pause on the pavement for a moment. Listen to the traffic on Euston Road, and to the footsteps of people heading for their trains. Behind you, on shelves you cannot see, there are pages written centuries ago in Isfahan, Delhi, Kyoto or Colombo. Someone carried them a very long way. Someone else has kept them safe ever since. As you walk on, notice how ordinary the street looks. Some of London's most remarkable stories sit behind its plainest doors.
