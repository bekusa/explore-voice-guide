The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches under passing feet, a wood pigeon calls from the cedars, and somewhere a gardener's barrow rattles across the lawn. In front of you stands a house of deep red brick, warm as a terracotta pot left in the sun. Its curved gables rise against the sky like the fronts of merchants' houses in Amsterdam. It does not look like a palace at all. And that is the first surprise of Kew Palace.

This is the smallest of the British royal palaces. It is closer in size to a wealthy family home than to a castle or a court. That is because it began life as exactly that. The house was built in 1631 for Samuel Fortrey, a London merchant whose family had roots in the Low Countries. For a long time, people simply called it the Dutch House, and you can see why the name stuck.

Look above the main doorway. Carved into the brickwork you will find the date 1631 and a set of initials. These are generally read as the initials of Samuel Fortrey and his wife, Catherine. It is a small, personal mark, the kind a couple might leave on a new home. Nearly four centuries later, it is still there, slightly softened by weather, above the door that kings and queens would one day use.

Now look closely at the bricks themselves. They are laid in a pattern called Flemish bond. In each row, a long side of a brick alternates with a short end. This gives the walls a fine, woven texture that catches the light at an angle. The rounded, stepped gables on top are often called Dutch gables. In the early seventeenth century, this style showed that the owner had money, taste, and connections across the North Sea.

So how did a merchant's house become royal? In the eighteenth century, the Hanoverian royal family fell in love with Kew. The Hanoverians were the German-born dynasty that included the four King Georges. Several royal houses once stood on this riverside land. Most of them are gone. The great White House nearby was pulled down, and an ambitious castle-style palace for King George the Third was started but never finished, then demolished in the late 1820s. Only this modest brick house survived.

For King George the Third and Queen Charlotte, Kew was a family retreat. They had fifteen children, and Kew gave them space away from the formality of London. The king had a strong interest in farming, science, and botany. He became known affectionately as Farmer George. Here, a royal family could live a quieter, more domestic life, surrounded by gardens rather than courtiers.

But the house also holds a harder story. George the Third suffered long periods of mental illness. In the early 1800s, he stayed at Kew during some of these episodes, under the care of doctors whose treatments seem harsh to us today. Inside, the palace tells this part of his life with care. It is a reminder that behind the portraits and titles, this was a family dealing with fear, love, and uncertainty, much as any family might.

In 1818, two very different events took place under this roof within a few months. In July, a double royal wedding was held here, in Queen Charlotte's drawing room. Two of her sons married that day. One of them, the Duke of Kent, would become the father of Queen Victoria. The other would later reign as King William the Fourth. Then, in November of that same year, Queen Charlotte died here at Kew.

After Charlotte's death, the house fell quiet. Later in the nineteenth century, Queen Victoria allowed it to open to the public, and it has welcomed visitors since then. Today the palace is cared for by Historic Royal Palaces, the charity that also looks after the Tower of London. A major restoration was completed in the 2000s, bringing the rooms back to the look of George the Third's time.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. When the restorers worked on the house, they chose to leave part of the upper floor largely as they found it. Instead of fresh paint and new wallpaper, you can see bare boards, old layers of decoration, and the bones of the building. It feels like walking from a finished stage set into the backstage. If you go inside, compare the restored rooms with these untouched spaces. The contrast tells its own story.

Step around to the back of the house, and you will find the Queen's Garden. It was laid out in the twentieth century in the style of a seventeenth-century garden, to match the age of the house. Look for clipped hedges, gravel paths, and plants chosen because people grew them for food or medicine in that period. It is a garden of herbs and order, quite different from the open lawns and great glasshouses elsewhere at Kew.

Remember, too, where you are standing. This palace sits inside the Royal Botanic Gardens, which is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. UNESCO is the United Nations body that recognises places of outstanding value to all of humanity. The gardens grew partly from the royal family's own interest in plants. So this small brick house is not just a royal home. It is part of the story of how Kew became one of the world's great centres for the study of plants.

Before you move on, take one more look at the front of the house. Notice how the low sun turns the brick from red to almost copper. Listen again to the birds, the voices, the wind in the trees. For nearly four hundred years, this house has watched merchants, kings, and gardeners come and go. Now you are part of that line of visitors. As you walk away along the paths, glance back once. The Dutch House will still be there, quiet and warm, waiting for the next family to arrive.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Queen's Garden directly behind the palace.
Check the palace's seasonal opening before crossing the gardens.
Stand back on the lawn to frame all the gables.
Come in late afternoon for warmer light on brick.
Combine your visit with a riverside walk nearby.
