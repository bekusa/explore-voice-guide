The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere beneath this part of Clerkenwell, the air is cool and still, and it smells faintly of dust, oil and old brick. Down there, a narrow tunnel curves away into the dark, just wide enough for a small train. For most of the twentieth century, that tunnel carried something nobody ever saw pass by: the post of London, moving under the feet of millions of people who had no idea it was there.

This is the London Post Office Railway, known since the 1980s as Mail Rail. It was a private underground railway built for one purpose only, to move letters and parcels across the city without touching the traffic above. It carried no regular passengers. It had no timetable posted on any wall. For most of its working life, it did not even have drivers. Today you can ride a short section of it, which is a privilege its original workers would have found very strange.

The problem it solved was simple. By the early twentieth century, the streets of central London were packed with horses, carts, buses and early motor vehicles. Mail vans moving between the great railway stations and the sorting offices were stuck in the same traffic as everyone else. Every delay meant late letters, and in a city that ran on written messages, late letters cost money. The Post Office wanted a route that no traffic jam could ever block.

This was not the first idea of its kind. In the nineteenth century, London had already experimented with a pneumatic railway, a system that pushed small cars through tubes using air pressure. That earlier scheme did not last. The Post Office took a different path. It chose electric power and a narrow track, with small driverless trains controlled from switch cabins at each station. It was a practical, careful design, and it worked for decades.

Parliament gave permission for the railway shortly before the First World War, and digging began during the war years. Then the war slowed everything down. Labour and materials were needed elsewhere, and the half-finished tunnels sat empty. According to accounts from that period, some of them were used as a safe store for treasures from London's museums and galleries, protected from air raids deep under the streets. It is a strange picture: priceless art waiting in the dark where mail trains would one day run.

The railway finally opened in 1927. Its main line ran roughly east to west across central London, linking Paddington in the west with Whitechapel in the east, about six and a half miles in total. Along the way it served sorting offices and main railway stations. The heart of the system was right here, beneath Mount Pleasant, one of the largest sorting offices in the city. Here is a surprise many visitors miss. Before it was a sorting office, this site held a prison, Coldbath Fields.

Now picture the scale of the work. The tunnels lie around twenty metres below the surface, deeper than many Underground lines. The track gauge is just two feet, which is roughly sixty centimetres, so everything is built small. Along the running tunnels, the walls press close to the trains. In the stations, the tunnels open into wider chambers with platforms, where staff loaded and unloaded the mail by hand. Lifts and chutes connected these platforms with the busy sorting floors above.

The trains themselves were the clever part. They carried containers of letters and parcels, and they ran on their own. Electric current reached them through the track, and the controllers in each station cabin set their routes and speeds. A train could leave one station, travel through the dark, and stop at the next platform without a single person on board. At its busiest, the railway moved millions of items of post every working day, around the clock, mostly unseen and unheard.

For many decades, this system simply did its job. Through the Second World War, through the rebuilding of London, through the arrival of new technology above ground, the little trains kept running. But the world of post was changing. More mail moved by road and by air. Sorting work moved to new centres outside the middle of the city. In 2003, the Royal Mail closed the railway. The trains stopped, the lights went out, and the tunnels fell quiet.

They stayed quiet for well over a decade. Then The Postal Museum, which you are visiting now, took on part of the old network. New passenger trains were built to fit the original narrow tunnels, and in 2017 a section of the railway opened to the public as a ride. This is the second surprise. You are not on a replica. You are travelling through the real tunnels, past real platforms, where the real mail once moved.

When you board, notice how low and snug the carriages feel. They had to be. The tunnels were designed for mail containers, not for adults sitting upright, so the passenger cars are compact and close to the rails. As the train moves off, listen to the sound change. The noise of the wheels bounces off curved brick and concrete, and the air grows cooler as you go deeper. Along the route, sound and light shows bring the old working life of the stations back for a few minutes.

Look closely at the details around you. There are old signs on the walls, worn surfaces where carts and containers once rolled, and pieces of equipment left from the working years. Some of the original trains are on display in the museum spaces nearby. Their shapes are short and sturdy, built for work rather than looks. Each one tells you something about the problem the engineers faced: carry as much as possible, through as little space as possible.

When you come back up into the daylight, take a moment before you move on. Look at the street outside, the traffic, the buses, the people walking past with their phones. Almost none of them know that a railway still runs beneath their feet, even if it now carries visitors instead of letters. As you walk away from this place, listen to the city around you. And remember that for nearly eighty years, under all of that noise, London's post travelled quietly through the dark.
