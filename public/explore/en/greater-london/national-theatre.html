The guide

The story

Stand at the river's edge and look up. The concrete rises in horizontal layers, raw and unapologetic, like a modernist cliff face dropped onto the South Bank. The surface is rough under your fingers — board-marked, textured, weathered by fifty years of London rain and Thames wind. This is the National Theatre, and it does not try to charm you. It announces itself.

The building opened in 1976, designed by Denys Lasdun, an architect who believed concrete could be noble. He called his approach 'urban landscape' — terraces that step down toward the water, public walkways threading through the mass, a building you can walk over as much as into. Prince Charles famously called it a nuclear power station. Lasdun took the insult as a compliment. He wanted weight, presence, permanence.

But the story begins decades earlier. The idea of a national theatre for England had been debated since the eighteen forties. George Bernard Shaw championed it, Laurence Olivier demanded it, committees formed and dissolved. Money was raised, sites were proposed, wars intervened. By the time ground broke in 1969, the project had become a kind of national obsession — proof that Britain still built great civic monuments, even as the empire contracted.

Lasdun's design houses three auditoriums under one roof. The Olivier Theatre, the largest, seats over a thousand people around an open stage — no proscenium arch, no curtain, actors and audience sharing the same room. The Lyttelton is a traditional proscenium house, intimate by comparison. The Cottesloe, now called the Dorfman, is a black-box studio that reconfigures for each production. Walk the foyers and you will see all three auditoria stacked and interlocking, a three-dimensional puzzle of sightlines and backstage corridors.

The concrete is not plain grey. Look closely. The aggregate is Thames gravel, flecked with brown and amber, so the walls carry the colour of the river. The formwork left horizontal striations, a rhythm of shadow and light that shifts as the sun moves. Lasdun refused to clad or paint the surface. He wanted the material to age honestly, to show its history. Fifty years on, the building has darkened, stained, softened — and somehow become more itself.

Inside, the foyers are vast and layered. Staircases zigzag through double-height spaces, balconies overhang the bookshop, the theatre bar spills onto an outdoor terrace. On performance nights, the building hums with movement — audiences crossing paths, strangers leaning on railings, the river glinting beyond the glass. Lasdun designed these circulation spaces as social territory, places to see and be seen, a democratic counterpoint to the velvet-and-gilt West End.

The National Theatre Company itself predates the building. Laurence Olivier founded it in 1963, performing first at the Old Vic in Southwark. Olivier directed, acted, fought with boards and critics, and built a troupe that included Maggie Smith, Derek Jacobi, Anthony Hopkins. When the company finally moved to the South Bank in 1976, Olivier was already stepping back. Peter Hall took over as director, steering the institution through its turbulent first decade in the new building.

The repertoire has always been restless. Shakespeare and Sophocles, yes, but also new writing, translations, adaptations, work from playwrights who had never seen a stage before. The building's three theatres allow risk — a classic in the Olivier, an experimental piece in the Dorfman, a crowd-pleaser in the Lyttelton. On any given week, you might see Greek tragedy, contemporary satire, and a musical revival all running in parallel.

Outside, the terraces are public space, free to wander whether or not you hold a ticket. Skateboarders claim the undercroft to the east, their wheels echoing against the concrete. The river walk stretches west toward the Tate Modern and the Millennium Bridge. On warm evenings, the outdoor bars fill, and the building becomes a gathering point, a place to watch the Thames darken and the city lights blink on.

The theatre's technical capabilities are formidable. The Olivier stage includes a massive revolve and a drum revolve within it, allowing entire sets to spin and reconfigure mid-scene. Trap doors drop actors into the basement, fly systems hoist scenery into the roof. The building was designed as a machine for performance, and that machinery is still tested nightly.

Controversy has never left the building. Brutalism divides opinion — some see Lasdun's concrete as heroic, others as oppressive. Proposals to soften or renovate the exterior surface every few years, and preservationists push back. The building is Grade Two Star listed now, protected as a landmark of post-war architecture. Its ugliness, if you call it that, has become heritage.

Walk the length of the terrace as you leave. Notice the way the building refuses symmetry, the way each facade is different, responding to the river, the street, the sky. The concrete is warm under the late sun, almost golden. Lasdun built a theatre that looks like a city, layered and lived-in, and fifty years on, it still feels like the future — or at least, like a future we once believed in.
