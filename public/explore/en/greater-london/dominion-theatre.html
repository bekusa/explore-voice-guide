The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement for a moment and listen. Buses brake at the lights. Footsteps hurry towards the Underground. Somewhere above you, a sign glows over the entrance. This is the corner where Tottenham Court Road meets Oxford Street and New Oxford Street, one of the busiest junctions in central London. Right here, wedged into the traffic and the crowds, rises the broad, pale face of the Dominion Theatre.

Before we talk about the stage, let us talk about the ground under your feet. Long before any theatre stood here, this site belonged to a brewery, the Horse Shoe Brewery. It made porter, a dark, strong beer that working Londoners drank in large quantities. Brewers stored it in enormous wooden vats, some of them taller than a house. Those vats lead to one of the strangest stories in London's history.

In October 1814, a metal hoop around one of the great vats gave way. The vat burst. Its collapse broke open others nearby. A wave of beer, many thousands of litres, rushed out of the brewery and into the narrow streets behind it. This was a poor, crowded neighbourhood, close to the slum known as the Saint Giles rookery. Houses flooded. Walls fell. Eight people died. Londoners still call this event the London Beer Flood, and it happened on the very spot where you now stand.

That is the first surprise. Most people queue here for a big musical and never guess that this corner was once the scene of a disaster made of beer. The brewery continued for many years after the flood. It finally closed in the early twentieth century, and the land was cleared. The city around it was changing fast, and a new kind of building was arriving on London's busy streets — the large, modern entertainment palace.

The Dominion opened in 1929. It was built as a theatre for live performance, and its first production was a musical comedy called Follow Through. Look at the size of the building. The architects designed it to hold a very large audience, with seating for around two thousand people. Even today, it counts among the largest theatres in London's West End. The scale was a statement. This was a building made for crowds, for spectacle, for big nights out.

The late 1920s were a difficult moment to open a theatre. Sound had just arrived in the cinema, and talking pictures pulled audiences away from live shows. Within a few years, the Dominion turned to film. It became one of London's grand picture palaces, where people came not only for the movie but for the whole evening — the velvet seats, the warm lights, the sense of occasion inside a large, decorated hall.

Here is the second surprise. In 1931, the British premiere of Charlie Chaplin's film City Lights took place here, and Chaplin himself came. Chaplin had grown up poor in South London, so this was a kind of homecoming. Picture the crowds pressing along this pavement to catch sight of the most famous comedian in the world. Later, in the late 1950s, the film musical South Pacific played here for years, filling the wide screen with colour and song.

Now look up at the facade. The front is long and smooth, with clean lines and a strong horizontal shape. It carries the confident, streamlined style of its era, often called Art Deco — a design fashion of the 1920s and 1930s that loved geometry, symmetry and a feeling of modern speed. The building sits on its awkward corner plot with a calm, solid presence. Its importance is officially recognised, and the theatre is a listed building, protected for its architectural value.

Over the decades, the Dominion moved back from cinema to live performance. It became a home for large-scale musicals, concerts and special events. Its big stage and its huge auditorium suit productions with elaborate sets and loud, powerful sound. The Royal Variety Performance, a long-running charity show attended by members of the royal family, has been staged here on a number of occasions. Each time, this ordinary street corner turned into a red-carpet event.

Many Londoners connect the Dominion with one show above all. From 2002, the Queen musical We Will Rock You played here for about twelve years. During that run, a tall golden statue of Freddie Mercury, the band's singer, stood above the entrance. His fist was raised to the sky. For a generation of visitors, that figure was the landmark you looked for when you came up out of the station. Fans still remember meeting friends under it.

Take a moment to look around the junction itself. Across the road rises Centre Point, the tall tower from the 1960s that once divided London opinion. Below you runs Tottenham Court Road station, now also served by the Elizabeth line, the newer railway that crosses London from east to west. To the north stretches Tottenham Court Road, long known for its shops. To the west lies Oxford Street. The theatre sits at the meeting point of all this movement.

That location explains a lot about the Dominion's long life. A theatre needs people, and this corner never runs short of them. Shoppers, commuters, students from nearby universities and visitors heading towards the British Museum all pass this door. On a show night, the mood shifts. Groups gather early. People check their phones for tickets. Some stand still and simply look up at the lights, the way audiences have done here for almost a century.

So before you walk on, look once more at this building. Think of the brewery vats, the rush of dark beer through narrow streets, the crowds waiting for Chaplin, the golden statue with its raised fist. All of it happened on this one patch of London. Listen again to the buses and the footsteps. The city keeps moving past the Dominion, and the theatre keeps opening its doors to the next audience.
