The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Underground trains rumble beneath the pavement, and buses circle the plane trees of Sloane Square. Then turn toward the red brick and pale stone of this theatre. It is a modest building, with a Victorian front facing the square, and it is easy to pass without a second look. Yet some of the most argued-about plays in modern British history began here. The Royal Court has a reputation that is far larger than its doors.

The building in front of you opened in 1888. It replaced an earlier theatre nearby on the same side of the square. Look at the facade. Warm red brick sits against carved stone, with tall windows and a gently curved roofline. It is a late Victorian design, made for an audience arriving by carriage and later by the new underground railway. Notice how close the station is. The two buildings have been neighbours for well over a century.

Here is a detail most visitors never hear. Inside Sloane Square station, a large iron pipe crosses above the platforms. That pipe carries the River Westbourne, one of London's lost rivers. The river once ran openly through this part of the city. Today it flows unseen, a few steps from the theatre's front doors. So while you stand here, a buried river runs past you on its way to the Thames.

The theatre's first great chapter came in the early twentieth century. Between 1904 and 1907, the managers Harley Granville Barker and John Vedrenne ran a famous series of seasons here. They championed new and serious drama at a time when London stages loved light entertainment. Many plays by George Bernard Shaw, the Irish playwright known for sharp social comedy, were staged here in those years. The idea of the Royal Court as a home for new writing begins in this period.

Then came the war. During the Second World War, the building was damaged in bombing in 1940. For years the stage stood dark. The theatre reopened in the early 1950s, but its future was uncertain. A small Victorian playhouse on the edge of Chelsea did not look like a place that would change British theatre. That change, when it came, was quick and loud.

In 1956, a new group called the English Stage Company took over the theatre. Its artistic director was George Devine. His mission was simple to say and hard to do. He wanted to put living writers at the centre of the stage. Instead of reviving old classics, the company would look for new voices. Many of those voices were young, angry, and unwilling to be polite.

In May 1956, the company staged John Osborne's play Look Back in Anger. It showed a frustrated young man in a cramped flat, raging at the comfortable values of postwar Britain. Many critics disliked it at first. Others saw at once that something new had arrived. The press soon used the phrase angry young men for a generation of writers. Historians of theatre often describe this production as a turning point for British drama.

The theatre also played a part in a bigger fight. For centuries, an official called the Lord Chamberlain had the power to censor plays in Britain. In 1965, the Royal Court staged Edward Bond's play Saved, which included a shocking scene of violence. The production led to a legal case. That case added pressure on the system, and stage censorship was finally abolished in 1968. Every playwright in Britain today works with a freedom this building helped to win.

Now for a surprise of a very different kind. In 1973, a small musical opened in the theatre's upstairs studio. It was The Rocky Horror Show, with its glittering costumes and cheerful strangeness. Few people expected it to become a cult favourite around the world. Yet it began here, in a small room above the main stage. The same building that hosted fierce political drama also gave the world a singing, dancing science-fiction comedy.

That upstairs room matters. The Royal Court has two performance spaces. There is the main auditorium downstairs, and a much smaller studio theatre upstairs. The small space lets the theatre take risks with new writers and unusual work. Over the decades, writers such as Caryl Churchill and Sarah Kane saw important plays performed here. Sarah Kane's first play, Blasted, opened at the Royal Court in 1995 and caused a fierce debate.

Around the turn of the millennium, the building went through a major renovation. The designers kept much of the old Victorian character while making the theatre work for modern audiences. Inside, you may notice rough brick, exposed surfaces, and a sense of the building's age left visible on purpose. Below street level, the bar and café extend into space beneath the square. So when you have a drink before a show, you are sitting under Sloane Square itself.

Step back and look at the setting. Across the square stands the long, curved glass front of the Peter Jones department store. The King's Road runs west from here, a street famous for fashion in the 1960s and the punk era of the 1970s. Chelsea's wealth sits all around you. Yet the theatre has often staged plays that question power and comfort. That contrast is part of the place's character.

If you go inside, notice how close the seats feel to the stage. The main auditorium is intimate, not grand. Actors can speak quietly and still reach the back rows. This closeness suits plays that are personal, direct, and sometimes uncomfortable. The theatre still focuses on new writing, and it supports young playwrights through workshops and development programmes. The company's original idea from the 1950s is still the centre of its work.

Before you walk on, take one more look at the front of the building. Watch people gather by the doors, checking their tickets, talking over the traffic. Listen again for the trains below your feet, and remember the buried river crossing above the platforms. It is an ordinary London corner on an ordinary day. But many of the stories that changed British theatre were first spoken aloud just behind those doors.
