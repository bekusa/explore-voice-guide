The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches under someone's shoes. Geese call from the water behind you, and the traffic of Kensington High Street is only a low hum through the trees. In front of you stands a long, calm building of warm red brick. It does not look much like a palace. That surprise is the first thing worth noticing, because this place began not as a fortress or a showpiece, but as a country house where a king could breathe.

In 1689, William the Third and Mary the Second took the English throne together. William suffered badly from asthma. The old royal palace of Whitehall sat beside the Thames, and its damp air and coal smoke made his chest worse. So the couple looked west, to the village of Kensington, which was then surrounded by fields. They bought a modest Jacobean mansion called Nottingham House. Here, the air was cleaner, and London was still close enough to reach by carriage.

The new king and queen needed more space than a private house could offer. They turned to Sir Christopher Wren, the architect who was rebuilding Saint Paul's Cathedral after the Great Fire. Wren did not tear the old house down. Instead, he wrapped new wings and courtyards around it. That is why the palace feels so domestic. It grew room by room, almost like a family home extended over generations, rather than appearing all at once from a single grand plan.

The red brick is part of that character. Wren and his team used brick with stone trim, in a restrained style that suited William's Dutch tastes. Look at the long rows of tall sash windows and the clean rooflines. There are no towers and no battlements. For a monarch who had grown up in the Netherlands, this quiet order felt natural. Many visitors walk past expecting gold and marble outside. The gold, as you will see, is kept indoors.

This was a house of grief as well as power. Queen Mary died here of smallpox in 1694, still in her early thirties. William lived on alone and died here in 1702, after a fall from his horse. Mary's sister Anne then became queen, and she too died within these walls in 1714. In just a few decades, the palace saw three monarchs come to the end of their lives. The cosy brick hides a great deal of royal sorrow.

Under the first Georgian kings, the interiors became far more theatrical. In the 1720s, George the First hired the painter and designer William Kent to decorate the State Apartments. If you go inside, climb the King's Staircase slowly. Kent painted its walls with a crowd of courtiers who lean over painted balconies and look down at you. Among them is a boy known as Peter the Wild Boy, found living in a German forest and brought to the court as a curiosity. Kent is said to have painted himself into the scene too.

The Cupola Room is the other great showpiece of the State Apartments. Its ceiling is painted to look like a deep coffered dome, but it is a trick of the eye. The surface is much flatter than it appears. Gilded statues stand around the walls, and blue and gold patterns cover every surface. It was a room for ceremony and display, designed to impress foreign visitors. Look up, and then look again, and notice how your sense of depth changes.

Now turn your attention to the gardens around you. Much of their shape comes from Queen Caroline, the clever and determined wife of George the Second. She worked with garden designers to create broad avenues, the Round Pond, and the long body of water that runs into the Serpentine. Before her, these grounds were more formal and enclosed. After her, they opened into the green spaces Londoners still walk through today. When you hear those geese, you are hearing part of her design.

After George the Second died here in 1760, the reigning monarchs stopped using Kensington as their main home. But the palace did not fall silent. It became a place for younger royals and extended family. And in 1819, a baby girl named Victoria was born here. She grew up in these rooms under a strict routine set by her mother. Early one morning in June 1837, she was woken and told that she was now queen. She was just eighteen.

That is why a white marble statue of Victoria stands on the east side of the palace, facing the Broad Walk. It shows her as a young woman at the time she became queen. The sculptor was her own daughter, Princess Louise, a trained artist. On the south side stands a bronze statue of William the Third. It was a gift in 1907 from the German Kaiser, Wilhelm the Second, who was Victoria's grandson. Two statues, two very different royal stories.

For many people alive today, this palace means one name above all: Diana, Princess of Wales. She lived here for many years, and after her death in 1997, the gates were buried under a sea of flowers. Visitors left cards, candles and toys, and the scent of cut flowers hung in the air for weeks. In 2021, her sons unveiled a statue of her in the Sunken Garden, surrounded by children. Parts of the palace are still private homes for members of the royal family today.

Before you leave, find the Orangery, a long and elegant building close to the palace. It was built in the early eighteenth century for Queen Anne, and its design is usually linked to the architect Nicholas Hawksmoor, who worked closely with Wren. It was meant for tender plants in winter and for summer entertaining. Its tall arched windows let the light pour in. Even from the outside, it shows how this royal house mixed ceremony with simple pleasures.

So, take one more look at that red brick wall in front of you. It has sheltered a sick king, a grieving queen, a girl who woke up to an empire, and a princess the world could not stop watching. Yet it still feels like a home. As you walk away, listen again — the gravel, the geese, the distant traffic. The palace was built for exactly this: a little quiet, just at the edge of the city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the King's Staircase slowly to see every painted face.
Walk the Broad Walk for the best view of the east front.
Visit the Sunken Garden early for a quieter moment.
Combine your visit with a walk to the Round Pond.
Book State Apartments entry in advance on busy weekends.
