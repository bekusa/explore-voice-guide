The guide

The story

Stand at the rail for a moment and listen. The river runs wide and brown beneath you, and the wind carries the smell of mud and water weed up from the banks. Above your head, painted ironwork rises in pale green and gold. It looks less like a road crossing and more like a decoration someone forgot to take down. This is Hammersmith Bridge, one of the oldest suspension crossings on the River Thames.

The idea of a suspension bridge is simple. Instead of resting the road on a row of arches, engineers hang it from chains. Those chains pass over tall towers and anchor deep into the ground on each bank. The weight of the road pulls down, the chains pull back, and the whole structure balances. Look up at the towers now, and you can trace the chains as they sweep down toward you in long, shallow curves.

The bridge you are standing on is not the first one here. In 1827, an earlier suspension bridge opened on this spot. It was designed by the engineer William Tierney Clark, and it was the first suspension bridge to cross the Thames. For its time, it was a bold experiment. Before it, people crossed here by ferry, or they walked a long way round to find another bridge.

That first bridge had a problem, though. It was built for horses and carts, not for the crowds and heavier traffic of a growing city. By the later nineteenth century, it was struggling. The answer came from Sir Joseph Bazalgette. Londoners mostly remember him for something hidden — the great sewer network he built beneath the city after years of cholera and foul river smells. He also designed bridges, and this one is his.

Bazalgette's bridge opened in 1887. Here is a detail most visitors miss. He did not start from nothing. His new bridge reused the stone piers and foundations of the older crossing. So, beneath the Victorian ironwork you see today, part of the bridge from 1827 still holds everything up. Two eras of engineering stand on top of each other in the same spot, and the join is invisible from the deck.

Now look closely at the towers. They are not plain. They carry ornate ironwork, small pointed caps, and a row of painted coats of arms. The royal arms sit at the centre, and the shields around them belong to places connected with London and its surroundings. The Victorians liked their public works to look proud. A bridge was not just a way across the water — it was a statement about a confident, expanding city.

The colours matter too. The green and gold paint makes the bridge feel almost playful against the grey river and the open sky. On a bright day, the gilded details catch the sun. On a misty morning, the towers fade into the haze, and the chains look like lines drawn in pencil. Few London bridges change their character so much with the weather.

The bridge also has a darker story. It has been a target for bombs more than once. In 1939, during an Irish Republican Army campaign in England, a device was placed here. According to the accounts, a passer-by spotted a smoking suitcase and threw it into the river. In 1996, another bomb was planted but failed to explode. Then in 2000, a bomb did go off and damaged the structure. Each time, the bridge survived.

In recent years, its biggest enemy has been age. In 2019, engineers found serious cracks in the cast-iron parts that support the chains. The bridge closed to cars and buses. For a time, in the summer heat of 2020, it closed even to people on foot, and boats were stopped from passing beneath. Later, walkers and cyclists were allowed back. The long debate about how to repair and pay for it has become part of the bridge's story.

There is one small, human detail worth finding. Somewhere on the railing, look for a modest plaque. It remembers a young officer who, after the First World War, jumped from the bridge into the river at night to save a woman who was drowning. He was honoured for his bravery. It is easy to walk past this plaque, but it turns a piece of engineering into a place where one person made a choice.

This stretch of river is also famous for rowing. Every spring, the Oxford and Cambridge Boat Race runs from Putney upstream to Mortlake, and the crews pass under this bridge. On other days, you will often see smaller boats from local rowing clubs gliding through. Listen for the coach calling out from a launch, and the steady splash of oars landing together. The river here feels like a working, living sports ground.

Look along the banks now. On the Hammersmith side, a riverside path called the Upper Mall runs upstream past old pubs, boathouses, and Georgian and Victorian houses. On the Barnes side, the bank feels greener and quieter, with trees reaching down toward the water. The bridge links two very different moods of west London — one more urban and lively, one closer to a village by the river.

As you stand here, notice how the bridge moves with you. Footsteps, bicycles, and the wind all pass through the deck. The chains above hold it all, as they have for well over a century. Before you walk on, take one last look at the green towers and the gold shields. Then look down at the water, still running toward the sea, under a crossing that people have argued over, attacked, repaired, and kept alive.
