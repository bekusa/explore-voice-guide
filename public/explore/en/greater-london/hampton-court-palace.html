The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look up. Above you, a forest of twisted brick chimneys rises against the sky. Each one is different... some spiral, some zigzag, some are carved into patterns like a honeycomb. Under your feet, the gravel crunches softly. Somewhere behind the walls, the River Thames moves slowly past. This is Hampton Court Palace, and the first thing it gives you is not a date or a name. It gives you colour: warm, rose-red brick, glowing in almost any light.

That brick tells you something important. In the early sixteenth century, brick was a fashionable, expensive material in England. Great houses were often built of stone. Here, the builders chose brick and used it with pride. Look closely at the walls and you may spot darker bricks set into diamond shapes. This pattern is called diapering. It was decoration, but it was also a quiet message: the owner of this place had money, taste, and ambition.

That owner was not, at first, a king. He was Thomas Wolsey — a butcher's son from Ipswich who rose to become a cardinal and the most powerful minister in Henry the Eighth's England. Wolsey took over the site in the 1510s and turned an older manor into a palace fit to receive kings and ambassadors. Visitors said it outshone the royal homes. That is a dangerous thing to be said about a servant's house.

Wolsey's fortune did not last. When he failed to secure the annulment of Henry's marriage to Catherine of Aragon, he lost the king's favour. Hampton Court passed into Henry's hands around the end of the 1520s. Wolsey died not long afterwards, on his way to face charges of treason. So the palace you are walking through began as one man's triumph... and became, in a sense, the price of his fall.

Henry did not simply move in. He expanded everything. His most dramatic addition is the Great Hall, where hundreds of courtiers could dine. When you step inside, let your eyes go straight up. The roof is a hammerbeam ceiling, a medieval English technique where short wooden beams jut out from the walls to carry the weight of the roof. It is carved, painted and gilded. Hidden among the decoration, look for the initials of Henry and Anne Boleyn, linked together.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Anne Boleyn fell from power and was executed in 1536. After her death, her badges and initials were removed from much of the palace. Some carvers worked quickly, and a few traces of her survived. It is a small, strange survival — the mark of a queen left behind by accident, in a room built to celebrate her marriage.

Now walk through to Clock Court and find the great Astronomical Clock above the gateway. It was made for Henry in the 1540s. Look carefully at its face. The sun moves around the earth, because that was how most people understood the heavens when it was made. The clock also shows the month, the phases of the moon, and the time of high water at London Bridge. That last detail was practical. Courtiers travelled up and down the river by boat, and the tide mattered.

And food mattered enormously. Henry's court could number around a thousand people, and they all had to be fed. The Tudor kitchens here are some of the largest that survive from the period in England. Walk through the long rooms and you will see vast fireplaces, open hearths built for roasting meat on spits. On some days, costumed cooks work here with real fire and real ingredients. Breathe in. Wood smoke and roasting meat are an honest way to meet the sixteenth century.

Not every story here is documented history. Legend has it that Catherine Howard, Henry's fifth wife, broke free from her guards in 1541 and ran along a corridor to beg the king for mercy. That corridor is now called the Haunted Gallery. Some visitors and staff say they have felt a sudden chill there, or heard a cry. Historians are not sure the escape ever happened. Still, it is worth walking the gallery slowly and deciding for yourself.

Then the palace changes character. Step into Fountain Court and you seem to enter a different building. In the late seventeenth century, the joint monarchs William the Third and Mary the Second asked Sir Christopher Wren — the architect of Saint Paul's Cathedral in London — to rebuild the palace in a newer style. The plan was to sweep away much of the Tudor building. Money and time ran short, so only part of it was replaced. That is why Hampton Court has two faces: Tudor red brick on one side, calm classical stone and brick on the other.

The palace is also a place where words changed. In 1604, King James the First held a meeting of churchmen here, now known as the Hampton Court Conference. Out of that meeting came the decision to make a new English translation of the Bible. The result became the King James Bible, one of the most influential books in the English language. Many phrases people still use today come from it.

Outside, the gardens stretch towards the river. Find the maze, planted around 1700 for William the Third. It is often described as the oldest surviving hedge maze in Britain. The paths feel short until you are inside them. Nearby, under glass, grows the Great Vine. It was planted in the eighteenth century under the head gardener Lancelot "Capability" Brown, and it still produces black grapes. It is one of the oldest and largest vines of its kind anywhere. One more surprise: there is a real tennis court here, an older indoor form of the game, and it is still played today.

The palace has survived its own disasters. In 1986, a fire broke out in the King's Apartments, the rooms Wren built for William. The fire caused serious damage. Craftspeople spent years restoring the rooms using traditional methods. When you walk through those apartments, part of what you see is late twentieth century skill, used to protect seventeenth century work. The repair is now part of the palace's story too.

Before you leave, come back to where you began, under those chimneys. Listen to the gravel, the birds in the gardens, and perhaps the faint sound of boats on the Thames. Monarchs, cooks, gardeners and servants all walked across this same ground. Take one last look at the red brick as you go. Notice how the colour shifts when a cloud passes overhead... warmer, then cooler, then warm again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to explore the Tudor kitchens before crowds.
Wear comfortable shoes for gravel paths and long galleries.
Save the maze for later when your energy returns.
Check for costumed cooking demonstrations on the day.
Walk to the riverside edge for quieter views of the palace.
