The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The sound in here is soft and close: the hum of old lighting, the creak of a floorboard, the faint tap of someone's shoe on a stair. Glass cases stand in tight rows, packed with objects the size of your thumb. There is no grand entrance hall and no towering statue to greet you. Instead you are in a quiet upper room of a university building in Bloomsbury, surrounded by thousands of small things that people in ancient Egypt once held in their hands.

This is the Petrie Museum of Egyptian Archaeology, part of University College London. It sits just off Malet Place, between lecture halls and libraries, and many Londoners walk past without knowing it exists. Its collection holds around eighty thousand objects. That makes it one of the largest collections of Egyptian and Sudanese archaeology anywhere outside Egypt. Yet it feels nothing like a palace of treasures. It feels like a working archive, and that is exactly what it was built to be.

The story begins with a woman, not with the man whose name is on the door. Amelia Edwards was a Victorian novelist and travel writer. In the 1870s she sailed up the Nile, and the journey changed her life. She wrote a popular book about it, then helped found the Egypt Exploration Fund to support careful excavation. When she died in 1892, she left her collection and her money to University College London. Her bequest created the first university post in Egyptology in Britain.

The first person to hold that post was William Matthew Flinders Petrie. He was largely self-taught, and he was obsessive about measurement. Many excavators of his time dug for gold, statues and objects for display. Petrie cared about broken pottery, beads, scraps of cloth and the exact layer where each one lay. He believed these ordinary fragments could tell the real story of how people lived. Over decades of digging, he sent huge numbers of finds back to London, and they formed the heart of this museum.

That focus on the everyday is the first surprise here. Look at the cases of pottery. They may seem repetitive at first glance, row after row of jars and bowls. But Petrie used changes in pottery styles to put ancient graves into order, even when no written dates survived. His method, called sequence dating, helped shape modern archaeology. The quiet shelves in front of you are, in a real sense, a laboratory where a new way of reading the past was tested.

Now for one of the most remarkable objects in the building. Look for a pale linen garment with fine pleats along the sleeves. It is known as the Tarkhan dress, named after the cemetery where it was found. Scientific dating places it in the late fourth millennium before Christ, more than five thousand years ago. That makes it one of the oldest known woven garments in the world. It still has creases at the elbows, as if someone wore it and moved their arms.

Near that sense of a real person, you will find another set of faces that hold attention. Petrie excavated at Hawara, near the Fayum oasis, and there he found painted portraits from the Roman period in Egypt. These were painted on wooden panels and placed over the faces of the mummified dead. Unlike older Egyptian art, they look strikingly modern. The eyes are large and direct. Some show earrings, curls of hair and the shadow of a beard. They were made for burial, but they look like people you might pass on a London street.

The museum also holds objects from the town at Lahun, where workers and officials lived during the Middle Kingdom, a period roughly four thousand years ago. Here you see the texture of daily life: tools, games, simple toys and household goods. Papyrus fragments from the town include writing on medicine and mathematics. These are not the possessions of pharaohs. They belong to cooks, builders, scribes and children, and that is what gives the room its unusual warmth.

As you move between cases, notice how the museum looks. Much of the display still uses old wooden and glass cabinets, crowded with small labels and numbered items. To a modern eye it can feel overwhelming. But this layout tells its own story. It shows how a university teaching collection was meant to be used: objects grouped for comparison, so that students could study change across centuries. You are not only looking at ancient Egypt here. You are also looking at the history of how people studied it.

This history has darker parts, and the museum does not hide them. Petrie lived in an age when many scholars held views on race that we now reject. He had links with the eugenics movement, which grew strongly at University College London in that period. In recent years the university has openly examined this legacy. The museum now raises questions about who collected these objects, how they left Egypt, and whose stories they should tell. It is an honest and important conversation.

Here is one more fact that most visitors never hear. Petrie died in Jerusalem in 1942 and was buried there. However, his head was separated from his body and sent to London for scientific study, at a time when scholars studied skulls in this way. It is a strange footnote, and a reminder of how far ideas about science and the human body have changed. It is not on display here, but it adds a curious final chapter to the life of the man behind these shelves.

Take a moment to think about the scale of what surrounds you. Many famous museums show a few great objects under bright light. This place does something different. It asks you to slow down, lean close to the glass and read the tiny details: a thumbprint in clay, a chipped bead, a knot of linen thread. The rewards here are small and personal. Often the object that stays with you is one you almost missed.

Before you leave, stand still for a final moment. Notice the low light, the quiet, the rows of numbered labels. Each one marks a piece of a life that ended thousands of years ago, carried here by boat and train to a corner of Bloomsbury. When you step back outside onto Malet Place, you will hear students talking and traffic on Gower Street. Listen to it for a second. The people who made these objects also filled their streets with ordinary noise.
