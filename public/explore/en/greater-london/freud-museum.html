The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Leafy Hampstead traffic hums faintly beyond the hedges. The garden path crunches underfoot, and a red brick house waits behind its front door. Inside, the air carries old paper, polished wood, and the dry smell of a room kept as it was. This is where Sigmund Freud spent the last year of his life. He was the Viennese doctor who taught the twentieth century to talk about the unconscious mind.

Freud did not choose London lightly. For almost his whole adult life, he lived and worked in Vienna. In March 1938, Nazi Germany annexed Austria, and life for Jewish families there turned dangerous almost overnight. Freud was in his eighties and seriously ill. Even so, he resisted leaving. Friends and admirers had to work hard, and pay heavily, before the authorities let him and much of his family go.

One of those friends was Princess Marie Bonaparte, a psychoanalyst and a great-grandniece of Napoleon's brother. She used her wealth and connections to help secure the family's exit. In June 1938, Freud travelled across Europe and arrived in London. He wrote that he wanted to die in freedom. After a short stay elsewhere in the city, the family moved into this house in the autumn of that year.

Here is the first surprise. The room in front of you is not a recreation built by curators from photographs. Freud's household goods, his books, and his desk came here from Vienna. His family and staff arranged them to look as close as possible to his old consulting rooms. So you are looking at a Viennese study, carefully moved and rebuilt inside an English suburban house.

And there is the couch. It is long, low, and heaped with cushions. A richly patterned Persian rug covers it, in deep reds and browns. This is the actual couch his patients lay on during psychoanalysis. Freud sat behind them, out of sight, in the green armchair at its head. He believed patients spoke more freely when they could not watch his face. That simple arrangement shaped therapy rooms around the world.

Now look past the couch, at the shelves and the desk. They are crowded with small figures. There are Egyptian gods, Greek and Roman statuettes, and Chinese pieces. Freud collected antiquities with real passion, and the collection runs to well over a thousand objects. He compared his work to archaeology — digging down through layers to recover something buried. On his desk, some figures face his chair. Visitors often say they seem to be keeping him company.

Freud was not well during his time here. He had suffered from cancer of the jaw for many years. He went through many operations and wore a painful prosthesis in his mouth. He was also a lifelong cigar smoker, and he found it very hard to stop. Yet he kept working. In this house he saw patients, received visitors, and finished his final book, Moses and Monotheism.

The visitors could be remarkable. The writer Stefan Zweig brought the young Spanish painter Salvador Dalí here in the summer of 1938. Dalí admired Freud's ideas about dreams, which fed directly into the Surrealist movement — art built from dream images and the irrational mind. Dalí made sketches of the old man during the visit. It is a strange picture to hold in your head. Two very different men, meeting in a quiet North London room.

Freud died in this house in September 1939, only weeks after the Second World War began. He was eighty-three. But the story of the house does not end there. His youngest daughter, Anna Freud, lived here for more than four decades after his death. She kept her father's study largely as he had left it.

Anna was a major figure in her own right. She became a pioneer of child psychoanalysis — the study of how children think, feel, and develop. During the war she helped run nurseries for children separated from their families. She later founded a centre in London for treating and studying children, which still carries her name today. When you climb the stairs, you enter her world. Her own rooms sit above her father's.

Upstairs, look for her weaving loom. Anna enjoyed weaving and knitting, often while she listened to patients and colleagues. It is a small, human detail, and many visitors walk past it. After Anna died in 1982, she left the house to become a museum. It opened to the public a few years later. Today the collection also includes family photographs, letters, and personal objects that show the Freuds as a real household.

Before you leave, step out toward the back of the house. Freud loved this garden. In his last months, he spent time here when his health allowed, sitting near the flowers and the lawn. The view from the study window opens onto this green space. Compare it in your mind with a crowded Vienna apartment, and you can feel the change. London gave him exile, illness, and grief — but also light, quiet, and safety.

So take one last look around you. The traffic still hums in the distance, and the brick still glows warm in the afternoon. Somewhere behind you, the couch rests under its patterned rug, with the small stone gods watching from the desk. As you walk back down the street, notice how ordinary these houses look. Behind one of these quiet front doors, an old man finished his life's work, a long way from home.
