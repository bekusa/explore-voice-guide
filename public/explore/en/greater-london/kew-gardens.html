The story

A short train ride from the centre, the streets give way to one of the largest and oldest botanical gardens in the world. Glasshouses glint among the trees, a tall pagoda rises above the canopy, and paths wind past plants gathered from every continent. This is Kew, where botanical science and a grand public garden have grown together for more than two centuries.

Kew's roots reach back to the eighteenth century, when royal gardens here began to be planted with rare and useful species; the gardens were founded in 1759 and grew into a national institution. Today they are recognised as a World Heritage Site, valued both for their landscape and for the scientific work carried on behind the scenes.

The signature building is the Palm House, a great curving structure of iron and glass from the middle of the nineteenth century, built to hold tropical palms under one warm roof. Stepping inside, into the heat and damp and green, is like walking into a rainforest, with a spiral stair leading to a walkway up among the treetops.

It is not the only glasshouse. The larger Temperate House shelters plants from milder climates, and another conservatory holds a series of climate zones under one roof, from desert to swamp. Between them they let you cross the world's vegetation in an afternoon, whatever the British weather happens to be doing outside.

Among the trees stand surprises. A ten-storey Chinese-style pagoda from the 1760s rises high above the gardens, a folly from an age fascinated by the wider world. Elsewhere a treetop walkway lifts you into the canopy, and a striking modern structure renders the activity of a beehive into light and sound.

Behind the public paths lies a serious scientific institution. Kew's botanists study and store plants from around the world, hold one of the great plant collections and seed banks, and work on conservation as habitats shrink. The garden you stroll through is also a working research centre of real global importance.

The grounds themselves are vast and varied, more landscape than flowerbed. Long tree-lined vistas, a lake, woodland, and broad lawns give room to wander for hours, and the planting is arranged so that there is something in leaf, flower, or colour in every season of the year.

Time of year shapes the visit. Spring brings bulbs and blossom, summer the full glasshouses and long evenings, autumn a blaze of colour through the trees, and winter the warm escape of the tropical houses and, some years, a trail of lights after dark. There is no wrong season, only different ones.

A word of warning: Kew is large, and seeing it properly takes most of a day and a good deal of walking. Comfortable shoes, water, and a relaxed plan all help. A land train runs between the main points for those who would rather ride part of the way than cover the whole distance on foot.

Unlike the central museums, Kew charges admission, and booking online ahead of time is cheaper and avoids queues. It lies in the southwest of the city, easily reached by Underground and rail to a station a short walk from the gate, which makes it a simple half-day or full-day trip out from the centre.

Kew sits beside the Thames, far upstream of the city's bridges, and the riverside path along its edge is a pleasant way to arrive or leave. The setting, green and quiet, feels a world away from central London despite being on the same train line as the crowds.

Kew is also a place of serious purpose in a warming world. Its scientists work to name and preserve plant species before they vanish, and its seed bank stores the future of countless plants against disaster. A walk in the gardens is, quietly, a walk through one of the front lines of conservation.

Families find plenty here beyond the plants. Play areas, wide lawns for running, and seasonal events keep children happy, while the glasshouses and the treetop walk hold their own kind of wonder. It is as much a great day out as a scientific collection.

Find a bench on a lawn or a quiet corner of a glasshouse and sit for a while among the plants. Kew is both a day out and a kind of living library of the plant world, assembled over centuries. When you take the train back into the city, a little of that green calm travels back with you.
