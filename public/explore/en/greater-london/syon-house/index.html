The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beyond the gravel and the clipped lawns, the Thames moves past slowly and almost silently. On a still morning you may hear geese out on the water meadows. You may also hear the low roar of a plane dropping towards Heathrow. Ancient pasture and modern flight paths sit side by side here, and that contrast is a good way to begin at Syon House.

From the outside, the house looks almost severe. It is a pale, square block with battlements along the roofline and very little decoration. You could mistake it for a fortress or a college. Most visitors expect more drama from a ducal palace. The drama is there, but it waits inside, and the plain exterior is part of the surprise.

The story of this site begins with nuns, not dukes. In the fifteenth century, King Henry the Fifth founded an abbey for the Bridgettine order. This was a community of religious women who followed the rule of Saint Bridget of Sweden. The abbey moved to this stretch of riverbank and took the name Syon, after Mount Zion in Jerusalem. It became one of the wealthiest religious houses in England.

That wealth did not protect it. In the sixteenth century, Henry the Eighth broke with the Church in Rome and closed the monasteries across England. Syon Abbey was dissolved, and its lands passed to the Crown. The buildings soon found darker uses. Catherine Howard, Henry's fifth wife, was held here before her execution. The quiet riverside abbey had become a place of royal punishment.

Now for a story you should hold lightly. When Henry the Eighth died, his coffin is said to have rested at Syon overnight on its way to Windsor. Legend has it that the coffin burst open in the night, and dogs were found licking at the king's remains. Locals tell it as divine payback for his treatment of the abbey. It is a vivid tale, but treat it as folklore rather than documented history.

After the abbey, the site passed to powerful men at court. Edward Seymour, Duke of Somerset, who ruled England for the young Edward the Sixth, built a grand house here on the old monastic ground. The core of the building in front of you grew from that Tudor house. Its square plan, arranged around an inner courtyard, still follows that early shape. Here is a fact many visitors miss: in 1553, Lady Jane Grey was offered the crown at Syon. She reigned for only nine days.

By the end of the sixteenth century, Syon had come to the Percy family, the Earls of Northumberland. Remarkably, it has stayed with them ever since. The Dukes of Northumberland still own the house, and it remains a family home today. That continuity is rare. Few great houses this close to central London have stayed in the same family for more than four centuries.

The great transformation came in the eighteenth century. The first Duke of Northumberland hired the Scottish architect Robert Adam to remodel the interiors. Adam was a leading figure in the neoclassical style, which drew its forms from ancient Rome and Greece. He did not change the plain outer walls. Instead, he created a sequence of rooms inside that moves from cool and grand to rich and warm.

The Great Hall is the first room in that sequence. It is a tall, pale space with a black and white marble floor that runs the full length of the room. Classical statues stand around the walls, including a cast of the Dying Gaul, a famous ancient figure of a wounded warrior. The light is soft and grey, and voices echo. Adam designed the room to cool and calm the visitor before the colour begins.

Step into the Ante Room and the mood changes completely. Deep green columns stand against walls of gold and colour. Many of these columns are made of scagliola, a mix of plaster and pigment polished to look like marble. Some are said to be genuine ancient marble, brought from Rome. Look closely at the gilded figures above the columns. They catch the light from the windows and make the room glow, even on a cloudy day.

Outside, the landscape tells a parallel story. Lancelot Brown, the gardener known as Capability Brown, reshaped the park in the eighteenth century. He favoured sweeping lawns, curving water and carefully placed trees, so that the land looked natural rather than formal. Between the house and the Thames, the tidal water meadows still flood and drain with the river. They are among the last meadows of this kind in London, and across the water lie the Royal Botanic Gardens at Kew.

Then there is the Great Conservatory. Built in the 1820s, it is a curving structure of glass and metal with a central dome. It predates the famous glasshouses at Kew. Many people think it helped inspire the age of great Victorian glass buildings. Inside, the air is warm and damp, and it smells of soil and green leaves. On a cold day, stepping through its doors feels like changing climates.

Before you leave, look up at the roofline of the house. A lead lion stands there, with its tail stretched out straight behind it. This is the Percy lion, the family emblem. It once stood on Northumberland House, the family's town palace near Charing Cross. When that house was demolished in the nineteenth century, the lion came here to the river.

Take a final look at the plain stone walls and the battlements. Behind them are the marble, the gilding and the green columns you may have just seen. Around them are the meadows, still shaped by the tide. As you walk back along the drive, listen again for the geese and the planes overhead. Nuns, kings, dukes and gardeners have all left their mark on this ground, and the river has kept flowing past them all.
