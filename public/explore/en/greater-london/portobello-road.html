The story

On a Saturday morning the long curving street is packed shoulder to shoulder, stalls spilling antiques and odds and ends across the pavements, traders calling out, and the smell of street food drifting between pastel-painted houses. This is Portobello Road in Notting Hill, home to one of the largest antiques markets in the world and one of the liveliest streets in west London.

The market is the main event, and it changes character along its length. The famous antiques section, with its silver, jewellery, old prints, and curiosities, runs at the southern end and is at its fullest on Saturdays. Further along come fruit and vegetable stalls, then vintage clothing and second-hand goods, so a single walk shifts from treasure to everyday and back.

The antiques trade is what made the street's name. Hundreds of dealers, in covered arcades and at open stalls, sell everything from fine silver to cheap trinkets, and gentle haggling is expected rather than rude. Even if you buy nothing, browsing the stalls is like wandering through a scattered, living museum of other people's pasts.

The setting is half the appeal. Notting Hill is a district of elegant nineteenth-century terraces painted in soft pastels, grand garden squares, and a layered history of the many communities who shaped it. The neighbourhood became widely known abroad through a romantic film named after it, and some of the crowd comes partly for that.

The area's other great moment comes in late summer, when the Notting Hill Carnival fills these streets. Born from the city's Caribbean community, it is one of the largest street festivals in Europe, days of music, costume, and dense crowds, and it has shaped the identity of the whole district well beyond carnival week itself.

Food is woven through the market. Stalls sell hot dishes from around the world, bakeries and cafes line the road, and the side streets hide quieter places to sit. Coming hungry is a good strategy here; eating your way slowly along the street is part of how locals and visitors alike enjoy it.

It is worth knowing the rhythm. Saturday is the full spectacle, crowded and loud; on other days the street is far quieter, with many antiques stalls closed but the shops, cafes, and a smaller market still open. Which you prefer depends on whether you want the event or the everyday street behind it.

The street is more than its stalls. Independent shops, record stores, bookshops, and small galleries line it and the lanes nearby, and the further you walk from the busiest section, the more you find the ordinary, lived-in neighbourhood that surrounds the spectacle.

A few practical notes. Come early on a Saturday to beat the thickest crowds and see the antiques at their best; keep an eye on your belongings in the press of people; and bring some cash, as a number of stalls still prefer it. Wear shoes you can stand and walk in for hours.

The street runs through Notting Hill in the west of the city, reached by Underground stations at either end, so you can enter from the top and walk down, or the reverse, letting the market change around you as you go from one section to the next.

More than any single thing to see, Portobello is about atmosphere — the colour of the houses, the calls of the traders, the mix of visitors, locals, and dealers, the sense of a street that has been trading and changing for generations. It rewards an unhurried wander rather than a hunt for one particular object.

The street has a long trading history that predates its antiques fame. It began as a country lane and grew into a market for everyday goods as the city spread west, and the antiques trade arrived more recently, layering treasure-hunting on top of an ordinary neighbourhood market that still feeds local kitchens.

Stop and talk to the dealers if you have the time. Many know the history of their corner of the trade and the objects they sell, and a few minutes of conversation often turns a stall of old clutter into a set of small stories. It is a sociable market as much as a commercial one.

Pick up something small, or nothing at all, and let the current of the market carry you down the road. Portobello is less a sight than an experience of a particular corner of London, equal parts treasure hunt, street party, and ordinary neighbourhood going about its day. When the crowd finally thins at the lower end, the quiet comes as a gentle surprise.
