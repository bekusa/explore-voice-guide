The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on this slope and feel the ground tilt beneath your feet. The pavement climbs east, gently but steadily, toward a great grey dome. Buses grind uphill in low gear. Behind you, the road dips down into a valley you can barely see anymore. This rise is Ludgate Hill, and somewhere close to where you are standing, a gate once closed the road at night.

Ludgate was the westernmost gate in the old wall of London. That wall wrapped around the city for well over a thousand years. Travellers coming from the west, from Fleet Street and Westminster, passed through here to enter the City proper. Today there is no arch and no gatehouse. There is only the name, a busy road, and a few quiet clues for anyone who knows where to look.

The story begins with the Romans. They built a defensive wall around their town of Londinium, and this western road needed an opening. Roman custom placed burials outside the walls of a town, so the land beyond this gate, toward the river valley below, held graves and roadside monuments. The gate itself was rebuilt many times over the centuries. Each new version stood on roughly the same line, guarding the same climb.

Now for the name. Legend has it that Ludgate was built by King Lud, a ruler of ancient Britain. According to this tale, London itself takes its name from him. The story comes from medieval writers, above all Geoffrey of Monmouth, who mixed history with invention. Historians treat King Lud as folklore. A more likely explanation is plainer. Many scholars think the name comes from Old English words for a back gate, or a gate that swings.

Even so, the legend mattered to Londoners. When the gate was rebuilt, carvings of King Lud and his two sons were placed on it. A statue of Queen Elizabeth the First joined them. Here is the first surprise. Those figures did not vanish when the gate came down. They were moved a short walk west, to the church of Saint Dunstan-in-the-West on Fleet Street. You can still see them there today.

The Elizabeth statue is the one to seek out. It was carved during her own reign, which makes it rare. Most images of her that you see in London were made long after her death. She stands in a niche above the street, slightly weathered, crowned and holding the symbols of royal power. King Lud and his sons sit nearby, worn by centuries of London rain.

The second surprise is darker. For centuries, Ludgate was not only a gate. It was also a prison. From the late fourteenth century, rooms in the gatehouse held debtors. These were often citizens and freemen of the City who could not pay what they owed. Conditions were hard, and prisoners depended on charity. Some begged from a grated window that faced the street. Passers-by heading uphill would have heard their calls.

In 1666 the Great Fire of London swept across the City. Ludgate burned along with much of the old town around it. The gate was rebuilt once more after the fire. But the age of city walls was ending. Gates had become obstacles for carts and coaches rather than defences. In 1760, the City authorities had Ludgate pulled down to widen the road. The traffic you hear right now is the reason the gate is gone.

So where exactly was it? Look for the church of Saint Martin within Ludgate, on the north side of the hill. Its slim dark spire rises above the shopfronts. This church stood right beside the gate, and a plaque on its wall marks the site. The church was rebuilt by Christopher Wren after the Great Fire. Its thin spire was designed to frame the much larger dome of Saint Paul's behind it.

Now turn and look downhill, to the west. At the bottom lies Ludgate Circus, a busy road junction. Below it, hidden underground, runs the River Fleet. In the past, this was an open river valley. The Fleet was later covered over and turned into a sewer. That dip in the road is the old river valley. It explains why the gate stood here, at the top of the slope. Defenders always prefer the high ground.

For many years, a railway bridge crossed Ludgate Hill and blocked the view of the cathedral. Victorian photographs show trains steaming across the street, with the dome peeping out behind them. That bridge was removed late in the twentieth century, when the railway was rebuilt underground. Today the line of sight is clear again. The view up this hill toward Saint Paul's is one of the most carefully protected views in London.

Think about the people who walked this same route. Medieval merchants, royal processions, prisoners led to the gatehouse cells, printers from Fleet Street, and office workers with coffee cups. All of them climbed this hill toward the cathedral. The buildings have changed again and again. The slope has not. Your legs feel exactly what theirs felt, the same steady effort upward.

Before you move on, pause by the church and look up the hill. Notice how the street narrows your view and pulls your eye toward the dome. Listen to the traffic where the gate once stood. As you walk away, whether uphill or down, remember that you are crossing an old threshold. For centuries, this point marked the edge of London itself. Now it is simply a step on a busy pavement, if you know where to put your foot.

Practical tips

Come early on a weekend for quieter streets and clearer photos.
