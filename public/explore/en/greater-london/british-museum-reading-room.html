The story

You step out of the crowded galleries and into a vast covered courtyard, where light pours down from a curving glass roof. In the middle stands a great round building, pale and quiet. You walk toward it, your footsteps echoing on the stone floor. This is the Reading Room of the British Museum, one of the most remarkable spaces in London.

The round building sits at the very center of the museum, hidden for much of its life behind surrounding walls and offices. For around 150 years almost no ordinary visitor ever saw it. Only in the year 2000, when the courtyard around it was opened up and covered with a glass roof, did the Reading Room finally stand in full public view.

That courtyard is worth a pause of its own. Called the Great Court, it was designed by the architect Norman Foster and opened in 2000. Its roof is made of thousands of triangular glass panels, no two exactly alike, curving down from the round Reading Room to the square walls around it. It is the largest covered public square in Europe.

The Reading Room itself is much older, built between 1854 and 1857. The architect was Sydney Smirke, whose elder brother Robert had designed the museum's main building decades earlier. It was put up to give the growing crowd of readers a proper home, filling the open inner courtyard that had stood empty at the heart of the museum since it was first built.

Step inside, if the doors are open, and look up. A great dome rises above you, painted in soft blue, cream, and gold. It measures around 42 meters across, which made it one of the widest domes in the world at the time. Its size was matched only by ancient buildings such as the Pantheon in Rome, built many centuries before.

The room was designed for reading and for nothing else. Desks radiate outward from the center like the spokes of a wheel, each fitted with a long shared table. Around the walls rise shelves of reference books, climbing toward the dome. The whole shape was meant to keep books close at hand and to fill the room with even, gentle light.

For nearly a century and a half, this served as the main reading room of the British Library, before that library moved away to its own new building in 1997. To use it, a reader needed a special printed ticket, and the list of people who once held one reads almost like a history of modern thought itself.

Karl Marx sat here for years, reading and writing much of his great work on economics at one of these desks. The novelist Charles Dickens came here, as did Oscar Wilde, Virginia Woolf, and Bram Stoker, who gathered notes for the novel Dracula. Arthur Conan Doyle, who created Sherlock Holmes, studied here too.

Mahatma Gandhi read in this room during his years as a student in London. The revolutionary later known as Lenin used it as well, under a different name to avoid notice. For generation after generation, people who would go on to change the world came first to sit quietly beneath this dome.

Imagine the room in its working years. Hundreds of readers bent over their desks. The soft rustle of turning pages. Attendants carrying books from the stores along narrow iron walkways. A request for a single volume might take an hour to arrive, and readers waited, and thought, and wrote by the light falling from above.

Today the Reading Room no longer serves as a library. Its use has changed over the years, and it is not always open to visitors, so you may find the doors closed. Even then, its round outer wall shapes the Great Court around it, and you can circle it slowly, looking up to read the carved inscription that runs around the upper level of the court.

Whether you are able to step inside or not, stand for a moment beside its curving outer wall and rest your hand on the cool stone. Think of all the ideas that were born in the great circle on the other side, ideas that traveled out into the world and never came back to it quite unchanged.

Some places are famous for what they hold. This one is famous for what it made possible. Beneath its quiet dome, countless readers turned curiosity into understanding. Stand here, in the hush of the Great Court, and you stand at one of the places where the modern mind was shaped.
