The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beneath the traffic of West Smithfield there is a low rumble, as trains pass through tunnels under your feet. Look up and you see cast iron, painted in muted colours, and stone carved with the shields of the City. The air here once smelled of sawdust, cold meat and diesel from delivery lorries. This corner of London has fed the city for centuries. Now it is becoming the home of the city's own story.

You are at the new home of the London Museum. For decades this institution was called the Museum of London. It stood a short walk south, at London Wall, inside a concrete complex built among postwar offices. That site closed to visitors in late 2022. Around the same time, the museum shortened its name to London Museum. The plan was simple and bold. Move the museum of London into the old market halls of Smithfield.

Why here? Because few places in the city carry so many layers. Smithfield began as open ground just outside the Roman and medieval walls. The name is usually explained as a corruption of smooth field, a flat stretch of grass. People came here to trade horses and cattle, to hold fairs, and to watch tournaments. It was busy, noisy and often dangerous. It was also, for long periods, a place of public execution.

That darker history is well recorded. The Scottish leader William Wallace was executed at Smithfield in 1305. In 1381, during the Peasants' Revolt, the rebel leader Wat Tyler met the young King Richard the Second here. Tyler was struck down during that meeting. In the sixteenth century, under Queen Mary the First, Protestants were burned at the stake on this ground. A small memorial plaque on the wall of the nearby hospital remembers them.

That hospital is Saint Bartholomew's, usually called Barts. It was founded in the early twelfth century, and it still treats patients today. Close by stands the church of Saint Bartholomew the Great, one of the oldest churches in the city. Its round Norman arches and heavy stone pillars give a sense of the medieval priory that once filled this area. Few visitors realise how much early London survives within a few minutes' walk of the museum.

For centuries, live animals were driven through these streets to market. By the nineteenth century, this had become a serious problem. Writers described mud, blood and frightened cattle running through crowded lanes. In the 1850s, the live market moved north to Islington. Smithfield became a market for meat instead. A great covered market was built here in the Victorian period, designed by the City architect Horace Jones. He is the same architect behind Tower Bridge.

The buildings the museum now occupies grew up around that main market. They include the former General Market and neighbouring market structures. Notice the scale of them. These halls had to hold carts, porters and huge quantities of food. That is why the spaces are tall, open and full of light. Under the floors sit vaults and railway sidings, which once brought goods directly into the market from across the country.

The architects chosen to transform the site were Stanton Williams and Asif Khan. Their task was delicate. They needed to keep the character of tired, weathered market halls, while turning them into galleries. The approach has been to repair rather than erase. Old brick, iron and stone remain visible. New work sits alongside it. The idea is that the building itself becomes one of the objects on display.

So what does the museum actually hold? The collection is one of the largest of any city museum in the world. It runs from prehistoric tools found along the Thames, through Roman London, into the medieval city, and on to the present day. There are clothes, shop signs, toys, posters and protest banners. There are also sound recordings and photographs of ordinary Londoners. The focus is not only kings and lord mayors. It is everyone who lived here.

Here is a detail many people miss. The museum looks after the Lord Mayor's State Coach, a gilded carriage from the eighteenth century. Once a year it leaves the museum and rolls through the streets in the Lord Mayor's Show. So one of the collection's largest objects is also still in working use. Another treasure is the Cheapside Hoard, a cache of jewels and gems found by workmen in the early twentieth century, buried beneath a building on Cheapside.

Whoever hid that hoard never came back for it. Nobody knows for certain why. The jewellery dates roughly to the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. It includes rings, chains and coloured stones from far beyond Europe. It shows how connected London already was to world trade. For many visitors, standing over that glittering case is the moment the city's past feels personal. Someone, once, made a choice and simply disappeared from the record.

The museum's move also connects with the future of Smithfield itself. The meat market has worked here for generations, often through the night. Plans have been made for the traders to relocate away from the City. When that happens, a working rhythm that shaped these streets will end. The museum is part of how this neighbourhood will be remembered. Its sister site, London Museum Docklands, continues to tell the story of the river and the port.

Before you move on, stand still for a minute. Look at the ironwork above the street and the carved dragons that guard the City's shields. Feel the stone pavement worn smooth by centuries of carts and boots. Listen again for that rumble below. Trains, traders, crowds and executions have all passed through this square. Now the city has chosen to tell its own story here. As you walk away, notice how much of London still sits quietly in plain sight.
