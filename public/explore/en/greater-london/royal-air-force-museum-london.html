The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Inside these hangars, the air feels still and slightly cool, and every footstep on the hard floor echoes up into the steel roof. Above you, the shapes of aircraft hang in the half-light. Their wings stretch wide, and their propellers are frozen mid-turn. A faint smell of oil and old metal hangs in the air. You are standing on ground that has served aviation for more than a century.

This is the Royal Air Force Museum London, in Colindale, in the north of the city. The land under your feet was once an airfield called Hendon. Today it sits among housing estates, shops and the steady traffic of north London. But before the houses came, this was open grass. Early aviators took off here in fragile machines of wood, wire and fabric. Planes and the people who flew them shaped this place from its very first years.

Hendon's story begins before the First World War. An aviation pioneer named Claude Grahame-White helped turn the field into one of Britain's first serious aerodromes. He ran a flying school here, and he built aircraft here too. People in the early twentieth century were amazed by flight, and crowds came out from the city to watch. For many Londoners, Hendon was the first place they ever saw a human being leave the ground.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. One of the historic buildings on this site is linked to that early aircraft factory. The museum preserved it and moved it to a new position, so the brick and timber structure survived the redevelopment of the area. When you walk through it, you are walking through a real working space from aviation's infancy. You are not looking at a modern copy.

In the years between the two world wars, Hendon became famous for its air displays. The Royal Air Force was still a young service. It had formed in 1918, as the first independent air force in the world. The displays at Hendon showed the public what this new force could do. There were formation flying, mock battles and aerobatics. Large crowds gathered on the grass to look up at the sky.

The airfield later lost its flying role. London grew around it, and the runways could not grow with it. Then, in the early 1970s, part of the site found a new purpose. The museum opened here in 1972, with a mission to tell the story of the Royal Air Force. That story covers the people as well as the machines. It covers the pilots and the ground crews, the engineers and the families waiting at home.

The collection is spread across several large buildings, and each one has its own character. Some halls feel like a cathedral of metal. The aircraft are packed close together, and some hang from the ceiling so that you look up at their bellies. Other galleries are more personal, with letters, uniforms, medals and photographs. Take your time. The small objects in the cases often say as much as the largest bomber.

The Second World War sits at the emotional centre of the museum. You can see fighters of the kind that defended Britain in 1940, during the Battle of Britain. The Supermarine Spitfire is the best known of these, with its thin elliptical wings. Look closely at the Hawker Hurricane as well. It gets less fame today, but Hurricanes made up a large part of the defending fighter force that summer.

Now for a second surprise. Among the bombers is an Avro Lancaster with a long combat record. Look near the cockpit and you will see rows of small painted bomb symbols. Each one marks an operation flown over enemy territory. Every symbol stands for a crew of seven young men who climbed aboard in the dark and did not know if they would return. Thousands of Bomber Command aircrew never came home.

The museum does not stop at 1945. Later galleries follow the Royal Air Force into the jet age and the Cold War. In those decades, aircraft became faster and heavier, and much more complex. The machines became sleek, angular shapes of grey metal, designed for speed and height. Some galleries also bring the story into recent decades. They look at peacekeeping, humanitarian missions and the changing technology of modern air power.

The museum was redeveloped for the hundredth anniversary of the Royal Air Force in 2018. New galleries began to tell the story through individual people. You will find accounts from mechanics, nurses, radar operators, and men and women from across the Commonwealth and beyond. These voices remind you that every aircraft needed many hands on the ground before it ever flew. A single sortie depended on dozens of people the public never saw.

As you move between the halls, notice how the light changes. In some buildings, daylight falls through high windows and lands on painted fuselages and riveted panels. In others, the lighting is low and focused, and polished metal shines in the dim space. Children press close to the cockpits. Older visitors sometimes stand quietly in front of one particular aircraft. Perhaps a father or grandfather once flew in it, or worked on it.

Before you leave, pause outside the hangars and look around you. The houses, roads and trains of modern London now cover most of this old flying ground. Yet the sky above is the same sky that drew those first aviators here. As you walk back towards the street, listen for a plane passing high overhead... and remember that this ordinary patch of London helped Britain learn to fly.

Practical tips
