The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment at the edge of Bank junction. Traffic and cyclists stream around you, and the air carries the hum of buses and the echo of footsteps from the station stairs below. Then look up at the wide stone front with its row of tall columns. Pale Portland stone, softened by soot and cleaning over the centuries, catches whatever light the City gives it. This is Mansion House, and it is still somebody's home.

That somebody is the Lord Mayor of the City of London. This is not the same person as the Mayor of London. The Mayor of London runs the whole modern capital. The Lord Mayor is a far older office. It is tied to the square mile of the old City, its livery companies, and its financial trade. Each Lord Mayor serves for just one year. For that year, this building is both their residence and their working office.

Here is the first surprise. For most of the City's long history, its Lord Mayors had no official house at all. They ran their year of office from their own homes or from the halls of the livery companies. Livery companies are the old trade guilds of London, such as the goldsmiths and the fishmongers. Only in the eighteenth century did the City decide that its leader needed a permanent, dignified residence. This building was the answer.

The design came from George Dance the Elder, who was the City's own surveyor. Work began in the 1730s and continued into the 1750s. Dance worked in the Palladian style. That means a classical look inspired by the Italian architect Andrea Palladio, with strict symmetry, columns, and calm proportions borrowed from ancient Rome. Look at the front. The six Corinthian columns, with their leafy carved tops, hold up a triangular pediment like the front of a temple.

The ground under the house has its own story. Before the mansion rose here, this spot held a market known as the Stocks Market. It sold meat, fish, and later fruit and vegetables. So the place where London's merchants now meet for formal dinners was once a place where ordinary Londoners haggled over food. The trade never really left this corner. It simply changed its clothes.

Now raise your eyes to the pediment. The carved figures there are by the sculptor Robert Taylor. The central figure represents the City of London. Look closely and you will see the City shown triumphing over Envy, a defeated figure at her feet. Nearby is a figure representing the River Thames. It is a confident message in stone. The City placed it above the door of its leader's house so that every visitor would see it on arrival.

Walk your gaze around the junction and you will see why this location mattered. Across the road stands the Bank of England, behind its long blank walls. The Royal Exchange, with its own columned front, faces the same open space. Together these three buildings frame one of the old centres of British money and power. Beneath your feet, the tunnels of Bank station carry thousands of people every hour.

Inside, the grandest room is the Egyptian Hall. The name confuses many visitors, because there is nothing Egyptian about the decoration. The term comes from the Roman architect Vitruvius. He described an Egyptian hall as a large room lined with columns, built for banquets and ceremony. Dance followed that ancient description. So the hall is Egyptian only in its plan, not in its pharaohs. It is the second surprise for many people who step inside.

That hall is the setting for one of the oldest habits in British public life. Each year, the Chancellor of the Exchequer, the government minister in charge of the economy, gives a speech at a dinner here for bankers and merchants. People call it the Mansion House speech. Journalists and markets listen carefully, because it often sets out how the government sees the financial world. The tablecloths may be formal, but the words can move money.

The building was not only for feasting. For a long time, Mansion House also held a court of justice. The Lord Mayor acted as chief magistrate of the City. Cases were heard inside, and the building had holding cells for prisoners. The court continued well into the twentieth century. Few people walking past today imagine that this elegant house once dealt with pickpockets and drunken disputes from the streets outside.

The house also holds a quiet treasure. Inside hangs the Harold Samuel Collection, a group of Dutch and Flemish paintings from the seventeenth century. That was the period often called the Dutch Golden Age, when painters in the Netherlands created detailed scenes of daily life, landscapes, and portraits. The collection was left to the City by the property developer Harold Samuel. Seeing these intimate canvases inside a ceremonial mansion is a lovely contrast.

The building has changed over the years. When it was new, its roofline looked different, with taller attic structures rising above the main body of the house. Later these upper additions were taken away, leaving the cleaner outline you see today. Like many London landmarks, it has been repaired, cleaned, and adapted again and again. The stone shows that long history of care, in patches of slightly different colour and texture.

Visiting the interior takes some planning. Mansion House is a working residence, not a museum that opens its doors every day. Guided group tours run on certain days, and they usually need to be booked ahead. If you cannot go inside, the outside still rewards a slow look. Step back toward the Royal Exchange for the widest view of the full front and its columns.

Before you move on, look once more at the steps and the great doorway. Imagine a new Lord Mayor arriving here each autumn, ready for a single busy year. Then listen again to the junction around you, to the buses, the voices, and the footsteps rising from the station. The City keeps changing at speed. This calm stone house stays where it is, quietly watching the traffic of money and people pass by.
