The guide

The story

Feel the slope under your feet first. The grass rises steadily, and the paths climb in long, easy curves. Somewhere behind you a dog barks, a child shrieks with joy, and a bicycle bell rings on the road below. Then you reach the top. The ground levels out, the wind picks up, and the whole of London suddenly opens in front of you, spread across the basin of the Thames like a map laid flat on a table.

This is Primrose Hill, a rounded green rise just north of Regent's Park. Its summit stands a little over sixty metres above sea level. That is not high by mountain standards, but in a city this flat, it is enough. From here the skyline arranges itself in layers. Nearby trees come first, then rooftops, then the towers of the City and the river crossings far beyond.

The view is not just beautiful. It is protected. London's planning rules name a small number of official viewpoints, and Primrose Hill is one of them. The line of sight from this summit towards Saint Paul's Cathedral is safeguarded, so new buildings are not supposed to block the famous dome. Look for it now, low and grey among the glass towers. Generations of Londoners have stood here and found that same shape on the horizon.

For most of its history, this hill was not a public park at all. Like neighbouring Regent's Park, it was once part of a royal hunting ground claimed by Henry VIII in the sixteenth century. Over time the land passed through different hands, and for a long period it belonged to Eton College. In the 1840s the Crown acquired it, and an Act of Parliament secured the hill as open space for the public. It has been a park ever since.

The name sounds gentle, and the origin is likely simple. Wild primroses, the pale yellow flowers of early spring, are said to have grown on these slopes. Today the hill is mostly open grass, mown and worn by thousands of feet. But the name has stayed, and it has spread. The whole surrounding neighbourhood of pastel houses and small shops now carries it too.

Here is a darker chapter that many visitors never hear. In 1678, a London magistrate named Sir Edmund Berry Godfrey was found dead in a ditch near the foot of this hill. His death came during a period of intense religious fear, and it fed wild rumours of a Catholic plot against the king. Innocent men were later executed. The case remains a mystery, and historians still argue about who really killed him.

Now a stranger and more poetic moment. In 1792, a Welsh poet and antiquarian known as Iolo Morganwg gathered a group of fellow enthusiasts here. They held a ceremony inspired by the ancient bards of Wales. This gathering is remembered as the first meeting of the Gorsedd, an order of poets and musicians that still plays a central role in Welsh cultural festivals today. A plaque on the hill marks the event. So a living Welsh tradition began, of all places, on a London hilltop.

Writers have loved this place, too. Near the summit, look at the stone edging around the viewpoint. You will find a carved line from the poet and painter William Blake, who described a vision of the spiritual sun seen on Primrose Hill. Blake was a Londoner with an intense inner life, and he saw angels in ordinary streets. Reading his words here, with the real sun over the city, is a quiet thrill.

The hill has a role in fiction as well. In H. G. Wells's novel The War of the Worlds, the Martian invaders make their final stand on Primrose Hill. And in Dodie Smith's book The Hundred and One Dalmatians, dogs across London pass messages by barking at dusk — the Twilight Bark — and this hill is part of that story. On a busy evening, with so many real dogs running across the grass, the idea feels surprisingly believable.

As you walk down the slopes, notice the trees. Some are old and broad, with heavy branches that spread low over the grass. One oak on the hill is linked to a celebration of Shakespeare's three hundredth birthday in the 1860s, when an oak was planted here in his honour. The original tree did not survive, but a replacement continues the tradition. It is a small reminder that this landscape has been shaped by ceremony as much as by nature.

The streets around the park are worth a slow walk. Rows of nineteenth century houses line the roads, many painted in soft creams, blues, and pinks. Blue plaques mark the homes of notable former residents. The poet Sylvia Plath lived nearby, and the Irish poet W. B. Yeats spent part of his childhood in the same area. Small cafés, bookshops, and pubs give the neighbourhood the feel of a village, even though central London is only a short distance away.

Listen carefully, and you may notice one more surprise. London Zoo sits on the edge of Regent's Park, just across the road and the canal from the foot of this hill. On a still day, locals say you can sometimes hear unfamiliar calls drifting across the grass. Whether or not you catch one, it is strange to remember that exotic animals live so close to this very English scene.

The hill changes with the hours. In early morning, runners and dog walkers have the paths almost to themselves, and mist can soften the distant towers. At sunset, people gather at the top with blankets and picnics to watch the light turn gold, then pink, across the glass of the City. On certain festive nights, crowds come to watch fireworks across the skyline. Each visit feels a little different.

Before you go, take one more look from where you are standing. Find the dome of Saint Paul's again, then let your eyes move across the rooftops in between. Every one of those buildings holds a story you will never know. Then turn, and walk slowly back down the slope. Feel the grass under your feet, hear the city noise return, and let the view stay with you a moment longer.
