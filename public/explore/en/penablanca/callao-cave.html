The guide

The story

The air changes first. As you climb the concrete steps up the hillside, the warm, damp heat of the Cagayan Valley presses against your skin. Then, at the mouth of the cave, a cooler breath rises to meet you. It smells of wet stone, earth, and something faintly sweet... the scent of moss and old limestone. Underfoot, the ground turns slick and uneven. Welcome to Callao Cave, in the hills of Peñablanca, in the far north of the Philippines.

This is limestone country. Over a very long span of time, rainwater seeped through cracks in the rock. It carried a weak natural acid that slowly dissolved the stone. Hollows became passages, and passages became chambers. The whole Peñablanca area is full of caves for this reason, and many remain barely explored. Callao is the best known, and the most visited. It sits inside a protected landscape that stretches toward the Sierra Madre, the long mountain range that runs down Luzon's eastern side.

The climb itself is part of the visit. The staircase is long, close to two hundred steps by most counts, and the tropical humidity makes it feel longer. Take it slowly. There are places to pause and look back over the treetops. Below, you may glimpse the Pinacanauan River, a green ribbon that winds through the valley. By the time you reach the entrance, you have earned the cool air waiting inside.

Callao is not one single space. It is a chain of chambers, usually counted as seven, linked one after another into the hill. Each has its own character. Some are tall and open, others low and dim. The first chamber is the one that stops most visitors in their tracks — and it is worth standing still for a moment when you enter it.

Look up. High in the ceiling, openings in the rock let daylight fall into the cave in soft shafts. On a clear day, the light lands on the floor in pale pools that shift slowly as the sun moves. Dust and moisture in the air make the beams almost solid. This natural skylight is the reason the first chamber feels less like a cave and more like a hall.

And here is the first surprise. This chamber holds a chapel. Local people placed an altar and simple pews among the rocks, and the space has been used for Catholic worship. Mass is sometimes celebrated here, with daylight from the ceiling falling across the altar. For many families in Cagayan, this is not only a tourist site. It is a place to pray, to mark special days, and in some cases to marry.

As you move deeper, the light fades and the formations grow stranger. Stalactites hang from the ceiling like slow drips frozen in stone. Stalagmites rise from the floor to meet them. Where the two join, they form columns. These shapes grow from mineral-rich water, one drop at a time, over thousands of years. Touching them leaves oils from your skin that can stop that growth, so it is kindest to look and not touch.

Now for the second surprise — and it is a big one. Callao Cave is not only a place of natural beauty. It is one of the most important sites in Asia for the story of human origins. In 2007, archaeologists working in the cave found a single small foot bone. Dating showed it was very old, at least around 67,000 years. At the time, it was among the oldest human remains ever found in the Philippines.

Further excavation brought up more finds: teeth, and small bones from fingers and toes, belonging to more than one individual. In 2019, the research team published their conclusion. These remains did not match any known human species. They named a new one, Homo luzonensis, meaning "human from Luzon". The finds suggest a small-bodied people, with teeth and curved finger and toe bones that combine very old and more recent features.

This raised a question scientists are still working on. How did early humans reach Luzon at all? This island has long been separated from the Asian mainland by deep water. Whoever arrived here, many tens of thousands of years ago, must have crossed the sea somehow. The answer is not yet known. So when you walk through these chambers, you are walking through a place that changed how researchers think about our human family.

The cave also has living residents. Bats roost in the darker chambers, and you may hear a faint rustle or a high squeak overhead. In the wider Peñablanca area, great numbers of bats leave their caves at dusk to feed. Seeing them pour out into the evening sky, like a ribbon of smoke, is one of the quieter wonders of this region, and many visitors stay in the valley to watch.

The walk through the chambers asks for some care. Paths can be wet, and some sections are dim, with uneven rock underfoot. Good shoes with grip help a great deal. Many visitors combine the cave with a boat ride on the Pinacanauan River below, past limestone cliffs and green banks. Local guides know the cave well and can point out formations you might pass without noticing.

Before you leave, go back to the first chamber, if you can. Stand near the altar and look up once more at the light coming through the rock. Listen to the drip of water somewhere in the dark, the echo of voices, the soft movement of bats. Very long ago, small people of a vanished human species lived among these hills. Today the same stone holds a chapel, a staircase, and you... a visitor from much later, standing in the same cool air.
