The guide

The story

Feel the packed earth under your feet. It is warm, a little red, and it rises gently in a long bank. Now look out. Flat water runs toward the horizon, so far that the far shore blurs into a line of trees. A breeze moves across the surface. You can hear water lapping, and somewhere behind you, the clink of a food stall. You are standing beside a baray, one of the great reservoirs of Angkor. It is easy to mistake it for a lake that nature made.

It is not a natural lake. Every metre of this shoreline was planned. The word baray is used across Cambodia for a reservoir, and the Khmer kings who ruled from Angkor built several of them. Each one is a vast rectangle, defined by high earthen banks called dykes. The builders did not dig deep into the ground. Instead, they piled up soil to form walls, then let the monsoon rains and diverted streams fill the space inside. The result is shallow water spread over an enormous area.

The largest that still holds water is the West Baray. It runs roughly eight kilometres from east to west and about two kilometres from north to south. Those numbers are hard to picture, so try this. A brisk walk along one long side would take well over an hour. Historians generally date its construction to the eleventh century. It is linked with the reign of Suryavarman the First, and the work is thought to have been finished under a later king, Udayadityavarman the Second.

Think about the labour involved. There were no engines and no steel machines. The dykes were raised by hand, basket by basket, by thousands of workers over many years. Sit with that for a moment as you look along the bank. The straight edge in front of you is not a road built recently. It is a human line drawn across the land nearly a thousand years ago... and it is still holding.

Here is the first surprise. Nobody is fully certain why the barays were built. For much of the twentieth century, a French scholar named Bernard-Philippe Groslier argued that Angkor was a great hydraulic city. In his view, the barays fed rice fields through canals and allowed several harvests a year. That theory made Angkor sound like a precise machine for growing food. It became the standard story told to visitors for decades.

Later researchers questioned it. Some pointed out that there was little clear evidence of outlet channels feeding fields directly from the barays. Others argued that the reservoirs were mainly religious. In Hindu cosmology, the home of the gods is Mount Meru, a sacred mountain surrounded by oceans. A temple in the middle of a great rectangle of water could stand for that mountain. Today many scholars think the truth sits in between. The barays may have managed water, and they also carried deep sacred meaning.

That sacred idea becomes visible at the centre of the West Baray. On a small island stands the West Mebon, a temple ruin. You may see it from the shore as a low cluster of stone and trees. In 1936, workers there found parts of a huge bronze statue of Vishnu, the Hindu god who preserves the world. He is shown reclining, as if resting on the cosmic ocean. The surviving upper part of the statue is now kept in the National Museum in Phnom Penh.

That discovery is the second surprise for many visitors. Angkor is famous for carved sandstone, so people rarely imagine large works in bronze. Yet the Vishnu from the West Mebon is one of the finest bronze sculptures known from Southeast Asia. Picture it in its original setting. A god lying on the water, at the heart of a man-made sea, surrounded by a rectangle of earth. The meaning would have been clear to anyone who stood here.

Not every baray survived as water. To the east of Angkor Thom lies the East Baray, which is older. It was built in the late ninth century under King Yasovarman the First, and it is also several kilometres long. Today it is dry. If you drive across it, you see fields, villages and palm trees where water once stood. At its centre is the East Mebon temple, which was once on an island. Now it sits on flat land, and you reach it by road.

The contrast between the two reservoirs tells a story. A baray needs care. Banks erode, channels silt up, and rivers shift their course. When the people who maintain a system move away or change their priorities, the water slowly leaves. The West Baray still fills partly because it has been repaired and used in modern times. The East Baray shows what happens when that care stops. Some researchers see these water problems as one part of the decline of Angkor.

Now look at the people around you. On the southern bank of the West Baray especially, the reservoir is a place for weekend outings. Cambodian families come to swim, to eat, and to lie in hammocks under simple wooden shelters. You may smell grilled fish or chicken, and see vendors selling fruit and cold drinks. Small boats often wait to take visitors out toward the island temple. This is less a monument than a shared shoreline, part of everyday life.

That everyday use is worth noticing. Many of the temples at Angkor are now quiet places for visitors, separated from daily routines. The baray is different. Children shout as they jump into the water. Fishermen work the shallows with nets. Water still moves through the landscape and still serves the people who live here. In that sense, this is one of the few Angkorian structures that still does something close to its original job.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Watch the light on the surface. In the late afternoon it turns gold, and the distant trees become a dark band. Follow the straight line of the dyke with your eyes until it disappears. Then remember that someone planned that line, and many hands built it. As you walk away, listen for the water behind you... still lapping against an edge that was made, a very long time ago, on purpose.

What to look for

The perfectly straight line of the raised earthen dyke.
The low island ruin of West Mebon mid-reservoir.
Wooden hammock shelters lining the southern shoreline.
Fishermen casting nets in the shallow water.
Reddish laterite soil exposed along the bank.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the West Baray's southern bank for local weekend life.
Come in late afternoon for softer light on the water.
Ask boatmen at the shore about trips to West Mebon.
Bring sun protection; the dykes have little natural shade.
See the Vishnu bronze at Phnom Penh's National Museum.
