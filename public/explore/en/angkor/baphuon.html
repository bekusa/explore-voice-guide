The guide

The story

Start at the eastern gate and look down. Under your feet runs a long raised walkway of grey sandstone. It sits on rows of short round columns, like a bridge over dry ground. The stone is warm by midmorning, and the air smells of dust and leaf litter. At the far end, a great stepped pyramid rises in tiers. That is the Baphuon, and the walk toward it is part of the design.

This causeway is unusual. Most Khmer temples are approached at ground level, but here the path is lifted on pillars. Some scholars think it echoes a rainbow bridge, a link between the human world and the home of the gods. Others see it simply as a ceremonial route for processions. Either way, the effect is the same. You walk above the earth, slowly, and the temple grows larger with every step.

The Baphuon was built in the middle of the eleventh century, under King Udayadityavarman the Second. It was a state temple, dedicated to Shiva, one of the great gods of Hinduism. In Khmer thinking, a temple like this was a model of Mount Meru. Meru is the sacred mountain at the centre of the universe in Hindu and Buddhist belief. Each tier you see is a step closer to the summit, and closer to the divine.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The Baphuon is older than the walls around it. The great city of Angkor Thom, with its gates and moats, came later, at the end of the twelfth century. So this temple was already old when the city grew up around it. Just to the south stands the Bayon, with its stone faces. To the north lies the old Royal Palace enclosure.

In its time, the Baphuon was likely one of the tallest buildings in the Khmer world. Its summit once carried a central tower, which is now gone. A Chinese envoy, Zhou Daguan, visited Angkor in the late thirteenth century and wrote an account of the city. He described a tower of bronze, taller than the golden tower nearby. Many historians believe he was describing the Baphuon. Picture it covered in gleaming metal, catching the tropical sun.

But the builders had a problem, and it was underground. The temple mountain is not solid stone all the way through. Its core is a huge mound of packed sand, held in by an outer skin of sandstone blocks. Over the centuries, heavy monsoon rains soaked into that fill. The sand shifted. The walls bulged and slumped, and large sections of the temple slowly collapsed.

Now walk around to the western side, and look carefully at the long wall there. At first it seems like a messy pile of stone. Then, slowly, a shape appears. It is a giant reclining Buddha, lying on its side along the face of the temple. Around the fifteenth or sixteenth century, as Cambodia turned toward Theravada Buddhism, people took stones from the temple itself to build this figure. You can make out the head, the long body, and the line of the arm.

That change tells a quiet story. A Hindu mountain for Shiva was reshaped into a Buddhist image, using its own blocks. Nothing was thrown away. The temple was not abandoned but reused, by people whose faith had changed. It is a reminder that Angkor was never frozen in one moment. It was a living place, adapted again and again by the people who prayed here.

The modern story of the Baphuon is just as remarkable. In the 1960s, French archaeologists from the French School of the Far East began a major rescue. They used a method called anastylosis. That means taking a structure apart, stone by stone, and rebuilding it on a stable base. Workers numbered each block and laid them out across the surrounding forest. Hundreds of thousands of stones were spread over the ground.

Then war came. During the years of conflict and the Khmer Rouge period, the work stopped completely. The records that explained where each stone belonged were lost. When the archaeologists returned in the 1990s, they faced a forest full of carved blocks with no plan. People began to call it the largest jigsaw puzzle in the world. The team worked from old photographs, drawings, and the shapes of the stones themselves.

The restoration was finally completed in 2011, around half a century after it began. Look closely as you climb, and you may notice small differences in the stone. Some blocks are original, softened and dark with age. Others are newer pieces, added where the old ones were missing or broken. Each one fits a space that someone had to work out by hand.

If you climb the steep stairs, take your time and use the rails. On the way up and around the galleries, look for small carved panels on the walls. The Baphuon is known for these little framed scenes, set like squares on a page. They show stories from the great Indian epics, the Ramayana and the Mahabharata. You will find gods, warriors, animals, and lively everyday figures. The Baphuon style, named after this temple, is famous for that delicate, story-telling carving.

From the upper levels, the view opens across the treetops. Below, you can trace the line of the causeway you walked along. Monkeys sometimes move along the walls, and cicadas fill the trees with a high, steady buzz. The forest presses close on every side. It is easy to see why the old builders wanted height here. From this level, the temple rises above everything around it.

When you come back down, pause once more on the causeway and turn around. Look at the tiers, the patched stone, the long Buddha resting on the western side. Almost a thousand years of building, falling, and rebuilding stand in front of you. As you walk away toward the Bayon or the palace, listen to your footsteps on the old stone. People have walked this path for centuries, and the temple is still standing for the next ones.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full raised causeway from the eastern gate.
Visit early morning before the heat and crowds build.
Wear shoes with grip for the steep upper stairs.
Cover shoulders and knees to climb the upper levels.
Pair your visit with the nearby Bayon and Royal Palace.
