The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere above you, a gibbon or a bird calls from the tall trees. Underfoot, the ground is a mix of packed red earth and worn sandstone slabs, uneven and warm. The light here falls in pieces. It drops through the leaves, lands on grey stone, and moves when the wind moves the branches. Welcome to Banteay Kdei. This is a temple you walk into slowly, because the doorways are low and the paths turn without warning.

The name is usually translated as the Citadel of Chambers, or the Citadel of Cells. Once you go inside, the name makes sense. The temple is a long chain of small rooms, corridors, and courtyards. They are linked by doorway after doorway, lined up along one central axis. Stand in one door, look straight ahead, and you can see a tunnel of frames stretching away from you, each one a little darker than the last.

This temple belongs to the reign of Jayavarman the Seventh. He was the Khmer king who ruled at the end of the twelfth century and into the early thirteenth. He was a Buddhist, which was unusual among Angkor's kings, most of whom built for the Hindu gods. His reign was the great building rush of Angkor. In those years, workers raised the Bayon, Ta Prohm, Preah Khan, and the walled city of Angkor Thom. Banteay Kdei comes from that same burst of energy.

That speed had a cost, and you can see it around you. Many scholars think the builders worked fast, and the sandstone here is often of poorer quality than at earlier temples. Some galleries have collapsed. Wooden props and steel braces hold up others. Piles of fallen blocks lie in the courtyards, some numbered by restorers. It is honest ruin. You are seeing a building that was pushed to its limits from the start.

Now look back at the entrance gates, the gopuras. A gopura is a monumental gateway tower. At the outer gates, large stone faces look out in four directions. They are serene, with closed lips and a faint smile. This is the same style you see at the Bayon. Who the faces represent is still debated. Some scholars link them to a compassionate Buddhist figure. Others think they also echo the king himself. Nobody can say for certain.

On the outer enclosure wall, watch the corners. Here and there, carved figures of Garuda appear. Garuda is the great bird-man from Hindu and Buddhist stories, a carrier of gods and an enemy of serpents. The outer walls are mostly laterite. Laterite is a rough, reddish stone full of small holes, a bit like dried sponge. It was cheap and strong. The fine carving was saved for sandstone, which is smoother and easier to cut.

As you move inward, you reach a building that many guides call the Hall of Dancers. It is a cross-shaped hall with open courtyards. Its pillars and walls carry carvings of dancing figures, frozen mid-step. Many visitors think it was used for ceremonies or performances, though its exact purpose is not known. Stand in the middle for a moment. Notice how the light comes from four sides at once and how the sound of footsteps changes.

Here is something many visitors miss. Look closely at the niches and panels where small seated Buddhas should be. In many places, the figure has been chiselled away. Only a rough outline or a flat scar remains. After Jayavarman the Seventh died, later rulers turned back toward Hinduism. A wave of defacement followed across Angkor, and Buddhist images were scraped from many walls. Some Buddhas were even recarved into other shapes, such as Hindu ascetics or lotus patterns.

And here is a second surprise. In 2001, a Japanese archaeological team from Sophia University was working at Banteay Kdei. They found a buried cache of Buddhist statues, more than two hundred pieces, many broken. The finding suggests that someone buried these images on purpose, perhaps during that same period of rejection. The sculptures were carefully studied and many are now in museum care. So the ground you are walking on has held secrets well into our own century.

Keep an eye on the walls between the doorways. Female figures called devatas stand in shallow niches. Devatas are divine guardian spirits. They wear tall headdresses, heavy jewellery, and patterned skirts. Their hands often hold a flower. Some are almost perfect. Others have lost their faces to rain, lichen, or time. Each one is a little different. The carvers did not use a single template, and you can spend a long time comparing them.

Compared with Ta Prohm, which sits not far away, this temple feels calmer. Trees grow close, but they do not swallow the stone in the same dramatic way. The crowds are usually smaller. That gives you room. You can sit on a fallen block, listen to the insects, and watch how the light shifts across a single carved panel. It is a place that rewards patience more than speed.

When you leave by the eastern gate, walk across the road. In front of you lies Srah Srang, a large rectangular reservoir. Its name is often translated as the royal bath. A sandstone landing terrace steps down to the water. Look for lions guarding it and balustrades shaped like the naga, a many-headed serpent from Khmer myth. The reservoir is older than the temple, but it was reworked in the time of Jayavarman the Seventh. Temple and water were planned to face each other.

Before you go, turn around once more. Look back through the gateway toward the long line of doorways. The stone faces above the gate still look east, toward the water, as they have for about eight hundred years. The insects are still calling. Somewhere a guard is sweeping leaves. As you walk on, notice the red dust on your shoes. It is the same earth the builders stood on.

Key facts

Look closer

What to look for

Four-faced stone heads crowning the outer entrance gates.
Garuda figures carved at corners of the enclosure wall.
Empty niches where seated Buddhas were scraped away.
Devatas with tall headdresses between the inner doorways.
Naga balustrades and stone lions at Srah Srang terrace.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon for softer light.
Combine with Srah Srang reservoir just across the road.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stones and fallen blocks.
Bring water; shade helps, but humidity is high.
Enter from the east to follow the temple's axis.
