The guide

The story

Listen first. On a still morning the wide plaza here carries the sound of footsteps on stone, the murmur of prayer from small groups, and the wind moving across the flat Greater Poland farmland. Then lift your eyes. A golden dome sits above cream and green walls, and a tall tower rises beside it. It looks like a city cathedral, but you are in a small village beside a lake.

This is the Basilica of Our Lady of Licheń, the largest church in Poland, and one of the largest in Europe. Many visitors do not expect that. Licheń Stary is a modest place near the town of Konin, surrounded by fields, woods and water. The contrast between the village and the building is the first surprise of this visit. The second surprise is how recently the basilica was built.

This is not a medieval cathedral. Construction began in the 1990s and was largely completed in the early 2000s. Pilgrims and donors paid for it, mostly through small gifts collected over many years. The architect was Barbara Bielecka, a Polish woman, which was unusual for a sacred building on this scale. The Marian Fathers, a Catholic religious order that cares for the sanctuary, led the project.

Why build something so large here? The answer is a small painting. Inside the basilica hangs an icon of the Virgin Mary, often called the Sorrowful Queen of Poland. It is modest in size, and it shows Mary with a white eagle on her chest. The white eagle is the national symbol of Poland. That single detail ties faith and nation together, and it explains much of the emotion you may see on the faces around you.

The story of the image belongs to the world of tradition and devotion. According to the pious account, a Polish soldier was badly wounded in the Napoleonic wars in the early nineteenth century. He prayed to Mary, and she is said to have appeared to him. Later he obtained or commissioned an image matching his vision. Years afterwards, the painting found its way to a forest shrine near Licheń.

The tradition continues with a shepherd from the area. In the middle of the nineteenth century, locals say, he saw Mary in the nearby forest. She warned of a coming disaster and called people to prayer. A deadly cholera epidemic did strike the region soon after. Many survivors came to believe the warning had been real. The painting was later moved to the parish church in Licheń, and pilgrims began to arrive in growing numbers.

The twentieth century tested that devotion hard. Poland lived through war, occupation, and then decades of communist rule. During those years, shrines like this one became places where people could gather openly under a shared identity. In 1967 the image was formally crowned by Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński, the leader of the Polish Church at that time. A crowning is a Catholic ceremony that honours an image as especially important to the faithful.

Now look again at the building itself. The style mixes many traditions. You will see classical columns, a large central dome, and a long colonnade, a row of columns that reaches out like open arms. Some visitors find it grand and joyful. Others find it too much. Polish critics have argued about its taste for years. You are free to form your own view as you walk.

The building also carries layers of symbolism. It is often said that the numbers of windows, doors and steps were planned to match the days of the year, the weeks, and the years of Christ's life. Take that as a popular explanation rather than a verified plan. Still, counting steps as you climb is a quiet way to slow down and look more closely.

Inside, the scale changes your sense of space. The nave, the long central hall of the church, feels wide and bright. Light falls from above, and the colours are warm, with gold and soft greens. The basilica also holds a very large pipe organ, among the biggest in Poland. If you are lucky enough to hear it during a service, the sound fills the whole interior and seems to come from every direction.

The basilica received a significant blessing before it was even finished. Pope John Paul II visited Licheń in 1999 and blessed the church. For many Poles, that visit gave the project a special seal of approval. The Polish pope remains deeply loved here, and you will likely find images and reminders of him around the sanctuary grounds.

The basilica is only one part of a larger complex. Behind the main church you can find an older parish church, chapels, gardens, and an artificial hill called Golgotha. Golgotha is the place where Christ was crucified, and this hill recreates the path of his suffering with grottos and figures. The grounds also hold a tall bell tower. Allow time to wander, because the quieter corners often feel more personal than the main nave.

This is still a working shrine, not a museum. On major feast days, especially in summer, large crowds arrive by coach and on foot. Families light candles, older pilgrims pray on their knees, and priests hear confessions in many booths. Please dress modestly and keep your voice low inside. If a Mass is underway, stand at the back and watch quietly rather than moving through the aisles.

Before you leave, walk out to the edge of the plaza and turn around once more. Notice how the dome catches the light, and how small the people look against the steps. Then look the other way, toward the fields and the trees around the village. A tiny painting brought all of this here. As you walk away, listen for the bells, and let the quiet of the countryside return.
