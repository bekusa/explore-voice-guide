The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the chatter of families and the crunch of footsteps on the paths, there are other sounds. A distant whoop from the trees. A low rumble that you feel more than hear. The wet slap of something large moving through water. Welcome to Chester Zoo, a place where the noises around you come from many corners of the planet at once.

You are standing a short distance north of the old walled city of Chester, in the village of Upton. It is easy to forget that, because the grounds feel like their own small country. Wide lawns open between clumps of trees. Paths split and curve away. Water runs through channels and pools. This is one of the largest zoos in Britain by area, and it rewards anyone who slows down.

The whole story begins with one man and one stubborn idea. His name was George Mottershead. He opened the zoo here in 1931, in the grounds of a country house called Oakfield. According to a story the family has long told, he visited a Victorian-style menagerie as a young boy. He saw animals pacing behind iron bars, and he promised himself he would one day build something better.

That promise shaped everything you will see today. Mottershead wanted animals kept without bars wherever possible. Instead, he used moats, ditches and natural barriers. The idea was simple — the animal and the visitor would both feel the space as open land. In the 1930s, that was an unusual way to think about a zoo. Today, look at almost any enclosure here, and you can still see the same principle at work.

The early years were not easy. Many local residents opposed the plan, and money was always short. The family did much of the work with their own hands. They dug ditches, built shelters and moved earth. Here is something many visitors never learn — the story of those first difficult years became a television drama in 2014, called Our Zoo, which brought the Mottershead family to a new audience.

Oakfield House itself still stands within the grounds. It is a reminder that this whole world grew out of a private garden and a family home. When you pass it, try to picture the scene in the early days. A handful of animals. A few enclosures built from salvaged materials. And a family who believed people would travel to see them. They were right.

Today the zoo is run by a conservation charity, the North of England Zoological Society. That detail matters more than it might seem. Money spent here goes back into the care of the animals, and into conservation work in the wild. Teams from Chester work with partners in many countries, from Africa to South East Asia. The zoo you walk through is only one part of a much larger effort.

One of the best places to understand that effort is the elephant area. Chester has kept Asian elephants for many years and has a long record of breeding them. Its scientists have also worked on research into a virus that is deadly to young elephants. Watch the herd for a while. Notice how the adults move around the calves, and how close the youngest stay to their mothers.

Now turn your attention to a different kind of space. The Islands zone, which opened in the mid-2010s, takes visitors on a journey through the landscapes of South East Asia and the Pacific. It is laid out like a series of island stops, with boats, wooden buildings and dense planting. Inside it sits Monsoon Forest, a huge indoor building filled with warm, humid air. Step through the doors and your glasses may fog up.

Here is the second surprise. In December 2018, a fire broke out inside Monsoon Forest. Staff worked to move animals to safety, and the building was badly damaged. It was rebuilt and reopened a couple of years later. When you walk through it today, the thick green canopy and the sound of running water give no clue to that night. It is a quiet story of recovery inside one roof.

As you continue, pay attention to how the paths guide you. Mottershead's family are often credited with a motto, Always Building, and the zoo has rarely stood still. New habitats appear. Old ones are changed or replaced. Some areas use glass walls so you can stand nose to nose with an animal. Others keep you at a distance and let you watch the animals behave as if no one is there.

The best way to enjoy a place this size is not to rush. Pick a few areas and give each one real time. Watch an otter dive and turn. Listen to the call of gibbons ring across the grounds. Sit on a bench and notice the birds that are not part of any exhibit at all — the wild robins, ducks and wagtails that have also made a home here.

Before you move on, take one more look at the space around you. Notice the ditch at the edge of an enclosure, the line of water, the low bank of grass. These are the quiet tools of an idea that began here almost a century ago. A boy saw bars and wanted something different. As you walk away, listen again. The calls, the rustle, the splash — they are still here, carried on the same open air he imagined.
