The guide

The story

Listen first. The air here is thick and warm, and it never goes fully quiet. Insects hum in a steady layer beneath everything. Somewhere in the canopy, a bird calls, and another answers from farther away. The ground under your feet is soft, damp, and dark with leaf litter. You are standing at the edge of one of the wettest and greenest corners of the Americas.

This is Los Katíos National Park, in the far northwest of Colombia. Most of the park lies in the department of Chocó, with a portion reaching into Antioquia, near the Gulf of Urabá. Its northern edge meets the border with Panama. On the other side of that line, the forest simply continues, into Panama's Darién National Park. Together, the two parks protect a large, unbroken stretch of tropical forest.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. This region is part of the Darién Gap — the one place where the Pan-American Highway stops. The road runs from Alaska almost to the tip of South America, with a single break. That break is here. Swamps, rivers, and steep forested hills made road-building difficult, and conservation concerns kept it that way. The absence of a road is one reason this forest still stands.

In 1994, UNESCO added Los Katíos to its World Heritage List. The reason was biodiversity. This corner of Colombia sits where two continents meet. For millions of years, plants and animals moved between North and South America through this narrow land bridge. Many of them stayed. As a result, species from both continents share the same forest, and some live nowhere else on Earth.

The landscape changes a lot within the park. In the east, the land is low and flat, shaped by the Atrato River — one of the largest rivers in Colombia by water flow. Here you find wide floodplains, slow channels, and swamps that local people call ciénagas. In the west, the ground rises into the Serranía del Darién, a range of forested hills along the Panamanian border. Between these two worlds, the forest shifts from flooded marsh to dense hillside jungle.

The name of the park comes from the Katío people. This is an older name for a group of the Emberá, an Indigenous people of the Pacific lowlands of Colombia and Panama. Emberá communities still live in this wider region today. Their knowledge of the rivers, plants, and seasons goes back many generations. When you travel here, you move through a homeland, not an empty wilderness.

Look up at the largest trees around you. One species to watch for is the cuipo tree. It has a tall, smooth, pale trunk that rises straight up before branching near the top. At the base, wide buttress roots spread out like low walls. These roots help hold the tree upright in soft, wet soil. Standing beside one, you get a real sense of scale. Your head reaches only a small part of the way up the trunk.

The animals are harder to see, but they are here. The park is home to howler monkeys, and you may hear them before you see them. Their deep, rolling roar can carry a long way through the trees, especially in the early morning. Large mammals such as the giant anteater and the Central American tapir also live in this region. And above the canopy, the harpy eagle hunts — one of the most powerful birds of prey in the world.

Birdwatchers come here for good reason. The mix of habitats — river, swamp, lowland forest, and hills — supports a very large number of bird species. Herons and other wading birds stand in the shallow water of the marshes. Toucans and parrots move through the higher branches. Even if you cannot name what you see, pay attention to colour and sound. Flashes of red, yellow, and green in the treetops are often the first sign that birds are nearby.

Water shapes almost everything in Los Katíos. Rain falls heavily here for much of the year. Streams run down from the hills and drop over rock ledges into clear pools. The best-known of these waterfalls is Tilupo, which falls in stages through the forest. Water also explains the colour of the rivers. Many of the lowland channels are brown with sediment, carried down from far upstream. The hill streams, by contrast, often run clear over stones.

The park has also faced serious pressure. Illegal logging, hunting, and armed conflict in the wider region affected the area for many years. For a period, UNESCO placed Los Katíos on its list of World Heritage in Danger. After improvements in protection and management, the park was removed from that list. That history is a reminder that a protected forest stays protected only through steady effort — by rangers, local communities, and visitors who follow the rules.

Getting here has always taken some effort. Traditionally, visitors have travelled by boat, across the Gulf of Urabá and up the Atrato River. The journey itself is part of the experience. You pass mangroves, river villages, and long stretches of green riverbank. Conditions and access rules can change, so travellers usually arrange visits through the national parks authority or authorised local guides. A local guide also helps you notice far more than you would on your own.

Now, before you move on, take one more moment where you stand. Notice the layers of green, from the low ferns to the high canopy. Feel the warmth and the moisture on your skin. Listen again for that steady insect hum, and for any call from the trees. This forest connects two continents, and it has done so for a very long time. As you walk away, keep listening. The forest will keep sounding long after you leave it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrange your visit through authorised guides or the parks authority.
Bring rain gear; heavy rainfall is common most of the year.
Start early in the morning for the best wildlife sounds.
Wear sturdy waterproof boots for muddy, uneven forest trails.
Pack insect repellent and light, long-sleeved clothing.
