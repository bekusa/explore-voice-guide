The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. There is the dry crunch of gravel under your boots, the smell of resin rising from the pines, and somewhere above you, a sharp, high whistle. That whistle is almost certainly a marmot, warning its family that you have arrived. In this valley, you are the visitor. Everything else here lives by its own rules.

You are standing in the Swiss National Park, in the far east of the Canton of the Grisons, close to the Italian border. The park opened in 1914. That makes it the oldest national park in the Alps, and one of the oldest in Europe. It sits in the Engadine, a long, high valley where the river Inn begins its journey towards the Danube, and where many locals still speak Romansh, the fourth national language of Switzerland.

Here is the idea that makes this place different. Most protected landscapes in Europe are managed. People cut the forests, mow the meadows and control the animals. Here, the founders chose almost the opposite. They decided that people would step back completely and let nature run its own course. No logging. No grazing. No hunting. Fallen trees stay where they fall. Avalanches clear the slopes, and nobody repairs the damage.

The park is protected under the strictest international category for nature reserves. In practice, this shapes your whole visit. You must stay on the marked trails. You may not pick flowers, collect stones or feed animals. Dogs are not allowed, even on a lead. There is no camping and no fire. These rules can feel strict at first. But look around, and you will see what they protect: a landscape that has been left alone for more than a century.

The story of the park begins in the early twentieth century. A group of Swiss naturalists worried that the Alps were losing their wild places, one valley at a time. They looked for an area where they could set nature aside and simply observe it. The remote side valleys around Zernez fit the plan. To help pay the communities for the land, supporters founded a national nature protection league in 1909. Today that organisation is known as Pro Natura, and it is still one of the largest nature groups in Switzerland.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The founders did not only want to protect animals. They wanted to run a scientific experiment that would last for centuries. What happens to a mountain landscape when humans stop touching it? Researchers have been measuring and mapping these valleys since the very beginning. Some of the oldest ecological study plots in the world are here, watched by generation after generation of scientists.

Now look at the trees around you. Much of the forest is mountain pine, a low, tough conifer that can grow on thin, rocky soil. Higher up, you may see larch, which turns gold in autumn, and the Swiss stone pine, with its dense, dark needles. Before the park existed, people cut large parts of these forests for timber and charcoal. Some of that wood fed fires for local mines and lime kilns. What you see today is a forest slowly rebuilding itself, without any help.

The animals are the reason many people come. Red deer graze in the open meadows, especially in the early morning and late evening. Chamois move across steep slopes with confident speed. Alpine ibex, the wild goats with long, curved horns, often rest on rocky ledges. Ibex had disappeared from Switzerland by the nineteenth century. Today they are back across the Alps, and this park is one of the best places to see them.

Look up as well. The bearded vulture, one of the largest birds in the Alps, once vanished from these mountains. People wrongly believed that it stole lambs, and even children, so they hunted it until it was gone. In the 1990s, conservationists began releasing young birds in this park. The bearded vulture eats mostly bones, which it drops onto rocks to break open. If you see a huge bird with a long, wedge-shaped tail, you may be watching the result of that project.

Here is a second surprise. Wild nature does not stop at national borders. In 2005, a brown bear crossed into this area from Italy. It was the first time anyone had seen a wild bear in Switzerland in roughly a century. Bears are still rare visitors, and you are very unlikely to meet one. But that moment showed how this park connects with a much larger mountain world.

Not every chapter here is perfectly wild. A road runs through the park, over the Ofen Pass, known in Romansh as Pass dal Fuorn. On the park's edge, the river Spöl is controlled by a dam that produces hydroelectric power. Over the years, conservationists and energy companies have argued about how much water should flow through the riverbed. The park, in other words, is also a story about compromise between wilderness and the needs of a modern country.

If you want to begin with context, the visitor centre in the village of Zernez is a good first stop. It explains the park's geology, wildlife and research in a clear, modern way. From there, trails lead into side valleys such as Val Trupchun, which is known for its deer and ibex. In autumn, the valley fills with the deep, rough roar of red deer stags during the rutting season. Many people say it is one of the most memorable sounds in the Alps.

Before you move on, pause where you are. Notice the silence between the birdcalls. Notice the grey limestone, the pale scree and the old dead trunks left lying across the slope. Nothing around you has been tidied or arranged. This is what a mountain valley looks like when people simply watch it. As you walk back along the trail, keep your eyes on the slopes. The marmot that whistled at your arrival may still be watching you leave.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the Zernez visitor centre for maps and context.
Bring binoculars to spot ibex and chamois on distant ledges.
Come at dawn or dusk for the best wildlife viewing.
Visit Val Trupchun in autumn to hear red deer rutting.
Leave dogs at home; they are not allowed anywhere inside.
