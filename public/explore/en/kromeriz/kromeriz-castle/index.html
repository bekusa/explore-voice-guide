The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel shifts under your shoes, and somewhere behind the long pale façade, the trees of the castle garden move in the wind. The air here often smells of cut grass and damp stone. Above you, a tall tower rises over the rooftops of a small Moravian town. This is Kroměříž Castle, and for centuries it was the summer home of some of the most powerful churchmen in Central Europe.

Kroměříž sits in the fertile lowland of Moravia, the eastern half of today's Czech Republic. The town is not large. Yet it holds a residence that feels built for an emperor. The reason is simple. This was the seat of the Bishops of Olomouc, a nearby cathedral city. Later, when Olomouc was raised to an archdiocese in the late eighteenth century, the castle became an archbishop's palace. Church leaders here held land, wealth and political weight, not only spiritual authority.

The building you see today is not the first one on this spot. An older residence stood here in the Middle Ages and grew through the Renaissance. Then came the Thirty Years' War, the long religious conflict that tore through Europe in the seventeenth century. Swedish troops captured Kroměříž in the 1640s and left the castle badly damaged. For a time, the bishops had a ruin instead of a palace. What happened next shaped everything around you.

A new bishop, Karl of Liechtenstein-Castelcorn, decided to rebuild on a grand scale. He brought in architects from the Italian-speaking world, including Filiberto Lucchese and Giovanni Pietro Tencalla. They gave the castle its calm, ordered Baroque shape. Baroque is the dramatic style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, built to impress with scale, symmetry and rich decoration. Look at the long lines of windows. Their steady rhythm was meant to show order, power and confidence after years of war.

Here is the first surprise. The interiors you will walk through owe much to a disaster. In the middle of the eighteenth century, a fire swept through the castle. Many rooms had to be rebuilt and decorated again. So the halls inside often feel lighter and more playful than the strict exterior. That lighter style is Rococo, a softer late form of Baroque, with pastel colours, curling gilded frames and painted ceilings full of sky.

The most famous of these rooms is the great Assembly Hall. It is long, tall and filled with light from rows of windows. Gold trim runs along the walls, and crystal chandeliers hang from a painted ceiling. The ceiling fresco is the work of the Austrian painter Franz Anton Maulbertsch, one of the leading artists of his age. Tilt your head back if you visit this hall. The painted figures seem to float far above the real room.

Now the second surprise. In 1848, revolutions spread across Europe, and the Austrian Empire was shaken. The imperial parliament left unsettled Vienna and moved to this quiet Moravian town. Its members met here, in this very castle. Through the winter, they worked on a new constitution for the empire, with ideas about the rights of citizens and the equality of nations. In early 1849, the emperor's government dissolved the parliament before the document could take effect. The Assembly Hall takes its name from these meetings.

The castle is also a treasure house of art. Its picture gallery holds one of the most important collections of old master paintings in the Czech lands. The best-known work is a late painting by Titian, the great Venetian master. It shows the ancient myth of Marsyas, a satyr who challenged the god Apollo to a music contest and lost. The scene is dark and unsettling. The paint is loose, almost rough, and many visitors stop in front of it for a long time.

Music and books matter here too. The bishops collected manuscripts, printed books and musical scores for generations. The castle library and music archive are known to scholars across Europe. Because the rooms still look so much like an eighteenth-century court, filmmakers have come here as well. Parts of Miloš Forman's film Amadeus, about the composer Mozart, were shot in Kroměříž. If you have seen the film, some of these halls may look familiar.

Step outside again and walk into the Castle Garden, which stretches behind the residence. Over time it changed from a formal Baroque layout into a romantic landscape park. Paths curve between old trees, ponds and small garden buildings. It feels less like a palace courtyard and more like a slow country walk. Early in the morning, the light falls low across the lawns, and the town noise fades behind the walls.

Kroměříž has a second garden, a short walk away through the town. This is the Flower Garden, a formal Baroque garden with clipped hedges, geometric flower beds and a long colonnade lined with statues. At its centre stands a round pavilion called the Rotunda. Inside, a Foucault pendulum swings slowly. This is a heavy weight on a long wire that shows the rotation of the Earth. Locals often call the Flower Garden one of the finest of its kind in Europe.

In 1998, UNESCO added the castle and both gardens to its World Heritage List. The reason was not only beauty. Together, they show how a Baroque prince-bishop's residence worked as a whole, with palace, park and pleasure garden planned as one landscape. Very few such ensembles survive so complete. That is why Kroměříž, a modest market town, sometimes appears in guidebooks as the Athens of Moravia, a nickname tied to its art, music and learning.

Before you move on, look once more at the castle. Notice the tower above the roofline, the pale walls, the windows of the Assembly Hall where an empire once argued about its future. Listen to the gravel under your feet and the birds in the garden trees. As you walk back into the town square, you are following the same path that bishops, painters and politicians once took... into the ordinary life of Kroměříž.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book the interior tour in advance during summer weekends.
Visit the Castle Garden early morning for quiet paths.
Walk through town to see the Flower Garden too.
Allow at least half a day for castle and gardens.
Wear comfortable shoes for long gravel garden paths.
