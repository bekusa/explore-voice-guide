The guide

The story

Cool air meets you at the entrance. It is damp and still, and it smells of wet limestone and old earth. Outside, the Ach valley is green and open. A few steps inside, the light drops away, sounds soften, and your footsteps begin to echo. You are walking into a cave that people have entered for tens of thousands of years. Some of the oldest art made by human hands came out of the ground beneath this floor.

The name Hohle Fels simply means hollow rock. It fits. From the outside, the cave is a modest opening at the foot of a limestone cliff near the small town of Schelklingen. Inside, the passage opens into one of the largest cave halls in the Swabian Jura. The Swabian Jura is the long limestone upland that crosses southern Germany. Its soft, porous rock is full of caves like this one, shaped by water over a very long time.

Take a moment to let your eyes adjust. The walls are pale and uneven, streaked in places with darker stains from water and minerals. The ceiling rises high above you. The floor has been lowered and levelled by excavation, so you are standing below the ground surface of earlier centuries. That missing earth is the whole story. It was removed carefully, layer by layer, because it held the traces of people who lived here during the last Ice Age.

Scientists first dug here in the nineteenth century. In those days, the main interest was the bones of cave bears and other Ice Age animals. Many of those early diggers did not yet understand what human tools and fragments could tell them. Modern excavations, led for many years by archaeologists from the University of Tübingen, worked far more slowly. They sieved the sediment and recorded every find in three dimensions. That patience changed what we know about early human culture.

In 2008, the team found a small figure carved from mammoth ivory. Today it is known as the Venus of Hohle Fels. It is only a few centimetres tall, small enough to close inside your hand. It shows a woman with very large breasts, a wide body and carefully marked details. And here is the first surprise... the figure has no head. In its place, the carver made a small loop. It was probably worn on a cord, as a pendant.

The figure is at least 35,000 years old, and possibly closer to 40,000. That makes it one of the oldest known sculptures of a human body anywhere in the world. Think about that for a moment. It was made long before farming, before writing, before cities. The people who made it were hunters and gatherers. They followed herds across a cold landscape of grass and open steppe, under skies we can barely imagine.

Archaeologists link these people to the Aurignacian culture. That is the name for some of the earliest modern humans in Europe, known for fine stone blades and ivory carving. In this valley and the valleys nearby, they carved animals, figures and ornaments. Mammoth ivory was hard to work with stone tools. Each piece would have taken many hours. These objects were not quick sketches. They were made with care, skill and intention.

Now the second surprise. In the same season of work, the excavators found pieces of a flute. It was made from the wing bone of a griffon vulture, a large bird with a wide wingspan. Several finger holes are still visible, and notches near the end show where the maker cut it. It counts among the oldest known musical instruments. So in this hall, so long ago, someone may have played music that echoed off the same walls around you.

Try this. Stand still and listen. Notice how sound behaves in the hall — how a voice carries and lingers. Nobody can know exactly what music sounded like here. But the space itself has not changed very much. The acoustics you hear now are close to what those early musicians heard. It is one of the rare moments where the past feels physical, not just imagined.

The cave is older than its famous finds. Deeper layers hold traces of Neanderthals, the earlier human species who lived in Europe before modern humans arrived. Later layers show visits by people after the Aurignacian period too. The cave was not one home for one group. It was a shelter people returned to, again and again, across a span of time so long that whole species came and went.

In 2017, this cave became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site called Caves and Ice Age Art in the Swabian Jura. It shares that status with several other caves in the Ach and Lone valleys. Together they tell one connected story about the birth of art and music in Europe. The original Venus and the flute are not displayed here. They are kept in the prehistory museum in nearby Blaubeuren, a short trip down the valley.

When you step back outside, look at the valley itself. The river Ach runs quietly along the meadows. Limestone cliffs rise on both sides, grey and white among the trees. During the Ice Age, this landscape was colder and far more open. Herds of reindeer, horses and mammoths moved through it. People sat at the mouth of this cave and watched them, the way you can watch walkers and cyclists along the valley road today.

Before you go, turn once more toward the entrance. It looks plain — a dark opening in a pale rock face. Nothing on the surface tells you what lay beneath the floor. That is worth keeping in mind as you walk on. The valley around you has many ordinary-looking rocks and slopes. Some of them may still hold small, patient traces of people who came here long before us, and who also stopped to listen to the echo.
