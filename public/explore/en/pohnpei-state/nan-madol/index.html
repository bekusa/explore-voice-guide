The guide

The story

Listen first. Water moves slowly through narrow channels around you, slapping against black stone. Mangrove roots creak in the tide. The air is warm and wet, and it smells of salt, mud, and green leaves. Under your feet, the ground is not quite land and not quite sea. You are standing on a reef flat off the southeast coast of Pohnpei, inside one of the strangest ruined cities on Earth.

This is Nan Madol. The name is usually translated as something like "the spaces between," and once you look around, it makes sense. The city is not one island. It is close to a hundred small artificial islets, built on the shallow reef beside Temwen Island. Between them run canals that fill and drain with the tide. Early visitors compared it to Venice. But there is nothing quite like it anywhere else.

Look closely at the walls. They are made of long, dark stone columns, each one with flat sides, like giant pencils. This is columnar basalt. It forms naturally when thick lava cools slowly and cracks into these shapes. The builders did not carve these columns. They found them, moved them, and stacked them. That alone took enormous effort, because many of the columns weigh several tonnes.

Now notice the pattern. The builders laid the columns in alternating layers. One layer runs lengthwise along the wall. The next layer runs crosswise, pointing inward. Then lengthwise again. It looks a little like a log cabin, and it locks the wall together without any mortar. The spaces inside were packed with chunks of coral rubble. Here is something many visitors miss — the walls often curve gently upward at the corners, which gives the buildings a quiet, lifted shape.

So who built all this? Archaeologists believe people were active on this reef by the first millennium of our era. The great stone building came later, mostly from around the twelfth and thirteenth centuries onward. The work is linked to the Saudeleur dynasty. The Saudeleur were rulers who, according to Pohnpeian history, brought the whole island under one central power for the first time. Nan Madol was their seat.

The city had two main parts. To the southwest was Madol Pah, the area connected with daily life, chiefs, and ritual activity. To the northeast was Madol Powe, the area connected with the dead. Here, priests lived and important people were buried. The division was deliberate. This was not a random cluster of islets. It was a planned landscape, where location carried meaning.

The largest and best known structure is Nandauwas, in the mortuary section. It is a royal tomb enclosure, with an outer wall and an inner wall, and a burial chamber in the centre. The outer walls rise well above head height, and in places they are many times taller than a person. When you stand inside, the noise of the sea drops away. The light becomes softer and greener. It feels closed and very still.

And now the great question — how did the stones get here? Researchers still debate exactly where every column was quarried and how it was moved. The basalt came from Pohnpei itself, but probably from several places, some of them far around the coast. One idea is that the columns were floated on rafts at high tide. Another is that they were dragged or levered. Nobody can say for certain.

Pohnpeians have their own answer. Legend has it that two brother sorcerers, Olisihpa and Olosohpa, came to the island to build a place of worship. According to the tale, they used magic to make the stones fly through the air and settle into place. Some versions add a flying dragon that helped them. It is a story, not a record. But it shows how impossible this work seemed, even to the people whose ancestors did it.

The Saudeleur did not rule forever. In Pohnpeian oral tradition, their power grew harsh, and people resented the demands of the rulers. A hero named Isokelekel, said to have come from the island of Kosrae to the east, led a revolt and defeated the last Saudeleur. After this, the island moved to a new system of chiefs. Nan Madol slowly lost its role, and over time it was largely abandoned.

For many Pohnpeians, this is still a place to treat with respect. Some people have long believed the site carries spiritual power, and there are traditional stories warning against disturbing it or staying after dark. The land also has customary owners. When you visit, you are a guest. Walk quietly, keep off the tops of the walls, and leave every stone exactly where you found it.

In 2016, Nan Madol was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List. In the same year, it was also placed on the list of World Heritage in Danger. That may surprise you. The threat is not mainly tourists. It is the water and the plants. Silt fills the canals, mangroves spread, and roots push between the stones. Slowly, the sea and the forest are taking the city back.

Pay attention to the tide while you are here. At high tide, the channels fill and the islets look like they are floating. At low tide, you can see mud, roots, and the bases of walls. Both views are worth seeing. Each one helps you understand how the builders lived with the water, rather than against it.

Before you leave, stop for a moment and look at one wall. Pick a single column. Think about the hands that lifted it, the canoe or raft that may have carried it, the people who walked these channels centuries ago. Then listen again. The water is still moving between the stones, just as it did when this was a living city. As you walk back, notice how the black basalt slowly disappears into the green.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check the tide schedule before you go.
Wear sturdy shoes that can get wet and muddy.
Bring drinking water and insect repellent for the mangroves.
Expect a customary landowner fee at the entrance.
Never climb or sit on top of the walls.
