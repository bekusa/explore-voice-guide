The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. Up here, on this low hill above the sea, it comes in off the water and moves through the gaps in the stone. Underfoot, the path is uneven, a mix of packed earth and old worn rock. Look east and the Aegean spreads out, pale blue and silver. Turn west and the dark slopes of Mount Olympus rise behind you. This is Platamon Castle. It stands on one of the narrowest strips of land in northern Greece, squeezed between the mountain and the sea.

That position explains almost everything about this place. For thousands of years, anyone travelling between Macedonia in the north and Thessaly in the south had to pass close to this hill. Armies, merchants, pilgrims, shepherds with their flocks. A short distance south, the valley of Tempe cuts through the mountains, and that gorge served as a natural gateway. Whoever held this hill could watch the road, the coast and the sea at the same time. Builders came back to this ridge again and again because the geography gave them no better choice.

Long before the castle, an ancient city stood here. Most scholars connect this site with a settlement known in antiquity as Herakleion, named after the hero Herakles, whom the Romans called Hercules. Ancient writers mention a town in this area, and excavations around the hill have turned up traces of earlier occupation. Very little of that classical town is visible today. What you see around you belongs mostly to the Middle Ages, but the ground beneath your feet carries layers that are much older than the walls.

By the Byzantine period, a fortified settlement already existed on this spot. The Byzantine Empire was the eastern, Greek-speaking continuation of the Roman Empire, ruled from Constantinople. The region was often threatened by raids from the sea and by invasions overland. So people gathered on high ground behind walls. The name Platamon itself is usually linked to this medieval world. Then, at the start of the thirteenth century, everything changed for this hill.

In 1204, the knights of the Fourth Crusade did something nobody expected. Instead of sailing on to the Holy Land, they captured Constantinople, the Christian capital of the Byzantine Empire. Western lords then divided Byzantine lands among themselves. A new Crusader state, the Kingdom of Thessalonica, was set up in this region. This area was granted to Lombard knights, nobles from northern Italy. They built the castle in its main form during those first years after the conquest. So here, in the shadow of Olympus, stands a fortress built by Italian Crusaders.

That surprises many visitors. They expect a Greek or Ottoman fort, and they find a story that began with knights from Lombardy. Crusader rule here did not last long, though. Within a few decades, the castle passed back into Greek hands, taken by the rulers of Epirus, a Greek state in the west. Over the following centuries the fortress changed owners several times. Byzantines, Venetians and finally the Ottoman Turks all held it. Each new garrison repaired the walls in its own way.

Now look at the shape of the defences around you. The outer wall does not form a neat rectangle or circle. It follows the edge of the hilltop, bending wherever the ground bends. Builders often did this in the Middle Ages. They used the natural slope as part of the defence, so attackers had to climb before they even reached the stone. Walk along the inside of the wall and notice how its line turns, again and again, to match the rock beneath it.

At the heart of the enclosure rises the keep, the strongest tower and the last place of refuge. It has eight sides, and its walls are thick and heavy. Look closely at the stonework. You can see rough local stone, set in mortar, with patches where the pattern changes. Those changes often mark repairs from different periods. The entrance to a keep like this was usually set high above the ground. Defenders could pull up a ladder or wooden stair and cut themselves off from the courtyard below.

Inside the walls, the open ground once held a small medieval town. Houses, storerooms, cisterns for collecting rainwater, and at least one church stood here. Today most of that has gone, and grass and low ruins fill the space. Archaeologists have uncovered foundations of buildings, including remains of a Byzantine church. Try to picture the courtyard full of people. There would be smoke from cooking fires, the sound of animals, voices in Greek and later in Turkish, and soldiers watching the road from the walls.

Under Ottoman rule, which lasted for centuries, the castle kept its military role. The fortress still guarded the coastal route. Its importance faded slowly as warfare changed and as cannon made medieval walls far less useful. Yet the hill never completely lost its strategic value. In the twentieth century, during the Second World War, soldiers fought here once again. In 1941, New Zealand troops held positions around Platamon against the advancing German army, before they withdrew south. Once again, the narrow passage between mountain and sea decided where the battle happened.

Today the castle has a gentler life. In summer, the courtyard often becomes an open-air stage. The regional Olympus Festival has long used the castle for concerts and theatre performances. Imagine music under the stars, with the walls lit from below and the sea dark beyond them. If you visit during the festival season, it is worth checking the programme. A performance inside these walls gives the place a completely different mood from a quiet morning visit.

Before you leave, walk to the seaward side and look down. Below the hill lies the modern village of Platamonas, with its beaches, cafes and small harbour. Further along the coast, the shoreline curves north toward Thessaloniki. Then turn and face the mountain. On a clear day, the high peaks of Olympus, home of the gods in Greek myth, can stand sharp against the sky. On other days, cloud hides them completely. Either way, you are standing exactly where travellers once looked for danger.

Take one more moment here. Run your hand along the stone if you can, and feel how rough and warm it is. Listen to the wind, and maybe to the waves far below. The knights who built these walls, the families who lived inside them, and the soldiers who watched from the towers all stood on this same hill. They looked at this same sea and this same mountain. As you walk back down the path, notice how the castle slowly disappears behind the slope... and how the road below still follows the old route between the mountain and the sea.
