The guide

The story

Listen for the traffic first. Just beyond the car park, the motorway hums without pause, a steady river of engines heading across southern Germany. Then look up. Above the roofline, two slender white aircraft point their noses at the sky, tilted as if frozen in the moment of take-off. Drivers on the road see them every day. Many swing off at the next exit just to find out what on earth they are looking at.

This is the Technik Museum Sinsheim, a museum of machines in a small town in the state of Baden-Württemberg. It opened in 1981, and it was not founded by a government or a university. It grew from a private association of enthusiasts who collected cars, engines and aircraft because they loved them. That origin still shows. The halls feel less like a polished gallery and more like the world's largest and most generous garage.

Let's start with those two aircraft on the roof, because they are the reason many people come. One is a Concorde, the Anglo-French supersonic airliner that once flew passengers between Europe and New York faster than the speed of sound. The other is a Tupolev Tu-144, its Soviet rival. The two jets look almost like twins. Here is the first surprise... this museum is known as the only place where you can see both supersonic airliners displayed together.

The story behind them is a Cold War story. In the 1960s, the West and the Soviet Union raced to build the first passenger jet that could fly faster than sound. The Soviet Tu-144 actually flew first, before Concorde. But its passenger career was short and troubled. Concorde flew paying passengers for decades. Today, the old rivals stand side by side on a German rooftop, wingtip to wingtip, competing for nothing but your attention.

And you do not only look at them. You can climb up and walk inside both. The stairways and walkways lead you into the cabins, and the floor slopes upward beneath your feet, because the aircraft are mounted at a steep angle. Notice how narrow the Concorde cabin feels. The seats are small, the windows are tiny, about the size of your palm. Speed, not space, was the luxury on board.

Back inside the halls, the scale changes completely. The air smells faintly of oil, metal and old leather. Rows of vintage cars stretch into the distance under bright lights. There are elegant pre-war limousines, gleaming sports cars and the great tail-finned American cruisers of the 1950s, all chrome and pastel paint. Many visitors simply wander, stopping wherever a shape or a colour catches their eye.

One collection deserves special attention. The museum holds a large permanent exhibition of Formula One cars, one of the biggest in Europe. Here you can follow decades of racing design in a single walk. Early cars look like cigars on wheels. Later ones grow wings, spoilers and low, sculpted bodies. Lean close to the cockpits. Many are astonishingly cramped, built around one driver's body like a tailored suit.

Now for the second surprise. Look for a strange, enormous vintage car called Brutus. Under its long bonnet sits not a car engine but a huge aircraft engine from the early twentieth century. Enthusiasts built it to see what would happen. When it runs at demonstration events, it is loud enough to feel in your chest, and flames can spit from the exhaust. It is a monument to a very simple question: why not?

The collection does not stop at cars and planes. You will find steam locomotives, their black iron wheels taller than a person. There are tractors, motorcycles, fire engines and military vehicles. There are mechanical music instruments too — giant fairground organs and orchestrions, machines that play music by themselves. In a museum full of engines, these are a reminder that technology was also built to entertain.

Aircraft fill the space overhead as well. Look up as you walk, because many planes hang from the ceiling or sit high on platforms. You will see propeller aircraft, early jets and military planes from different eras. Taken together, the halls tell one long story: how people learned, over little more than a century, to move faster on the ground and higher in the air.

The museum also has a large dome cinema with a giant screen that curves above your head. It is a separate experience from the halls, and many families plan their visit around one film. After hours of walking, sitting back under that wide screen is a welcome rest. Check the programme on arrival, because films and times change.

It helps to know that this place has a sibling. A second museum run by the same association stands in the city of Speyer, about an hour away. Speyer has its own large collection, including a Space Shuttle prototype and a Boeing jumbo jet. Some visitors combine the two over a weekend. If you only have one day, though, Sinsheim alone will easily fill it.

A final thought on the setting. Sinsheim is a modest town in rolling countryside, with farmland and small villages around it. The museum sits right beside the motorway, which feels fitting. This is a building about movement, placed at the edge of a road full of movement. The machines inside are the ancestors of the cars rushing past outside.

Before you leave, step back out into the open air and look up once more. The two supersonic jets still point at the sky, white against the clouds, their drooping noses lowered as if for landing. Neither will fly again. But listen to the motorway behind you, the endless rush of engines... and you will hear the same old human wish that built every machine in these halls — to go a little further, a little faster.

Practical tips

Visit the rooftop jets early before queues build up.
