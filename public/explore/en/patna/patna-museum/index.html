The guide

The story

Step out of the traffic on Buddha Marg and the noise seems to fall away. A wide lawn opens in front of you. Above it rises a long, pale building with small domes, arched windows and little rooftop pavilions. The air smells of dust, cut grass and, faintly, of the river city beyond. This is Patna Museum, and for more than a century people here have given it a simple local name... Jadughar, the house of magic.

That nickname tells you something important. For generations of families in Bihar, this building was the first place they saw ancient sculpture, strange fossils and objects from distant lands. Children came on school trips and left with stories. Even today, many older residents still call it Jadughar before they call it a museum. So as you walk in, try to imagine it the way they first did — as a doorway into something wonderful.

The museum began in 1917, during British colonial rule. A few years earlier, Bihar had been separated from the vast province of Bengal. The new province needed its own institutions, and a museum was part of that plan. At first the collection lived in temporary rooms. The building in front of you came later, finished in the late 1920s, and it was designed to give the province a proper home for its past.

Look closely at the architecture. The style is often called Indo-Saracenic. That is a colonial-era blend of British planning with Mughal and Rajput details from Indian palaces and tombs. You can see it in the domed kiosks on the roofline, called chhatris, and in the projecting windows that echo old Rajasthani balconies. The effect is part palace, part public office. It shows how colonial builders tried to borrow the language of Indian royalty.

To understand why this museum matters, you need to know where you are standing. Patna sits on the site of ancient Pataliputra. More than two thousand years ago, it was the capital of the kingdom of Magadha. Later it became the seat of the Mauryan Empire, whose most famous ruler, Ashoka, spread Buddhist teaching across much of South Asia. The ground under this city holds layers of that story, and much of what this museum collected came out of that soil.

Here is the first surprise. The museum's most famous object was found the same year the museum was founded. In 1917, on the banks of the Ganges at a place called Didarganj, people uncovered a life-size sandstone figure of a woman holding a fly-whisk. She became known as the Didarganj Yakshi. A yakshi is a female nature spirit, linked with fertility and abundance in old Indian belief.

What stunned scholars was her surface. The sandstone has a deep, mirror-like polish, a technique closely tied to the Mauryan period. Experts still debate exactly when she was carved. Some place her in the Mauryan era, others a little later. But almost everyone agrees she is one of the finest early sculptures from India. For decades, she was the heart of this building, and a symbol of Bihar itself.

Now the second surprise, and an honest one. In recent years, many of the museum's most celebrated pieces were moved to the newer Bihar Museum, a short distance away in the city. The Yakshi is among them. So the galleries here have changed, and some objects you may have read about may not be on display today. That is not a loss so much as a shift. This older building still carries a large and varied collection, and its own atmosphere.

As you walk the galleries, look for stone sculpture from across Bihar's long past. There are Buddhist and Hindu images, many carved in dark stone. Bihar was the land where the Buddha reached enlightenment, at Bodh Gaya, and where great monastic universities like Nalanda once stood. Many of the sculptures here come from that world. Notice the calm faces, the lowered eyes, and the fine detail of jewellery cut into hard stone.

Keep an eye out, too, for bronze figures from the Pala period. The Palas were a dynasty that ruled this region roughly from the eighth to the twelfth century, and they were great patrons of Buddhism. Bronzes from sites such as Kurkihar became famous for their grace and balance. Some have moved to the newer museum, but this house of magic was where many of them were first studied and shown to the public.

One of the museum's most unusual stories comes from the mountains, not the plains. The scholar and traveller Rahul Sankrityayan made journeys to Tibet in the twentieth century. He brought back Buddhist manuscripts and painted scrolls called thangkas. A thangka is a painting on cloth, often rolled up and carried by monks. Patna Museum became a home for many of these, linking Bihar, where Buddhism began, with Tibet, where it took deep root.

And then there is the object that children remember most. Somewhere in the building lies a very long fossilised tree trunk, turned to stone over an immense span of time. It looks like wood, with bark and grain, but it is rock. Standing beside it, you get a sense of scale far older than any empire. It is a quiet reminder that this museum was built to hold natural history as well as human history.

Before you leave, step back outside onto the lawn. Turn around and look at the building again. Notice how the light falls on the pale walls and catches the edges of the small domes. Listen to the city returning, the horns, the voices, the rickshaw bells on the road. Somewhere beneath all of that lies old Pataliputra... and in this building, a century of people trying to keep its memory close.
