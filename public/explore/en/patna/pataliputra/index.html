The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind the traffic of Patna there is a softer sound: wind moving through grass, birds in the trees, and somewhere beyond the rooftops, the slow presence of the Ganges. The ground under your feet is flat and ordinary. Yet beneath this ordinary ground lie the remains of one of the great capitals of the ancient world.

This is Pataliputra. Today the name belongs to memory and to archaeology, because the modern city of Patna has grown directly on top of it. There is no single gate to walk through, no skyline of ruins. Instead, the old city survives in pieces: in excavated sites like Kumhrar and Bulandi Bagh, in museum galleries, and in the words of travellers who saw it at its height.

The story begins with geography. This stretch of land sits close to where several rivers meet the Ganges, including the Son and the Gandak. For an ancient kingdom, that meant water, fertile soil, trade by boat, and natural protection. The kingdom here was Magadha, a powerful state in the eastern Gangetic plain. Its rulers understood that whoever held these rivers held the heart of northern India.

According to Buddhist texts, the settlement began as a small village called Pataligrama. In the fifth century before the Common Era, the Magadhan king Ajatashatru fortified the spot. Tradition says his successor moved the royal capital here. Buddhist scripture also tells a tale that the Buddha himself passed through while the fort was being built. He is said to have predicted that it would become a great city, threatened only by fire, flood, and quarrels. Whether prophecy or later storytelling, the city did grow.

It reached its peak under the Maurya dynasty. Chandragupta Maurya, who rose to power in the late fourth century before the Common Era, ruled from Pataliputra. So did his grandson Ashoka, one of the most famous rulers in Indian history. Ashoka's empire stretched across much of the subcontinent. Orders, officials, and ideas travelled outward from this riverbank to distant provinces, carved into rock and stone pillars far from here.

Here is the first surprise. Much of what we know about the city's appearance comes from a Greek. His name was Megasthenes, an envoy sent to the Mauryan court by Seleucus, a successor of Alexander the Great. His original book is lost. But later writers quoted him. Through them, we hear of a long city running beside the river, guarded by a moat and a massive wall of timber, pierced with many gates and topped with towers.

Timber, not stone. That detail sounds strange today, but archaeology supports it. At Bulandi Bagh, excavators found the remains of a wooden palisade, long lines of heavy timber set into the earth. Wood survived here because the waterlogged soil protected it from the air. Imagine the colour of the city then: not grey stone, but brown and gold, the smell of sawn timber and river mud, the sound of carts on packed earth.

Now to Kumhrar, the site most visitors come to see. In the twentieth century, archaeologists uncovered the remains of a vast pillared hall here, often called the Eighty-Pillared Hall. The pillars were made of polished sandstone. They once supported a roof, probably of wood. The scale suggests a royal or public building of real importance, although scholars still debate exactly how it was used.

The second surprise is the polish. Mauryan stone workers achieved a mirror-like shine on sandstone, a finish so smooth that people still argue about how it was done. You can see this quality on the surviving pillar fragment displayed at Kumhrar. Run your eyes along its surface. Even after more than two thousand years underground, the stone holds a soft glow in good light, almost like glazed pottery.

Why is so little visible? Water is the answer. The same high water table that preserved the wooden remains also made the excavated areas difficult to maintain. Much of the hall was later covered again for protection. So the park you walk through is partly a garden over buried history. The lawns and paths are calm, but the real monument lies quietly below them.

Pataliputra did not fade after the Mauryas. It remained an important city under later dynasties, including the Guptas, whose rule is often linked with a flowering of art, science, and literature. In the early fifth century of the Common Era, the Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Faxian visited. He wrote with wonder about the old palace, saying its stonework looked as if it had been made by spirits rather than people.

Later travellers found a different scene. When the pilgrim Xuanzang came through in the seventh century, he described much of the old city as ruins. Rivers shifted, power moved elsewhere, and Pataliputra slowly sank into the soil. Centuries later, a new city grew on the same ground. Under the Mughals and then the British it took the name Patna, and it is now the capital of the state of Bihar.

To see more of this ancient world, many visitors continue to the Bihar Museum. Among its treasures is the Didarganj Yakshi, a polished sandstone figure of a female spirit found near Patna. She carries a fly whisk and stands with calm confidence. Her surface shows that same remarkable Mauryan-style polish. Seeing her helps you picture the artisans who once worked in this city, shaping stone for kings and temples.

Before you leave, look around once more. Notice the ordinary life of Patna at the edges of the park: rickshaw bells, vendors calling, schoolchildren on the paths. This is the real continuity of Pataliputra. The palaces are gone, but people have lived, traded, and argued beside these rivers for more than two thousand years. As you walk back into the streets, remember that every step lands on the roof of an ancient capital.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Kumhrar early morning for softer light and cooler air.
Pair the site with a visit to the Bihar Museum.
Carry water and a hat for the open park grounds.
Wear comfortable shoes for walking the grassy excavation paths.
Allow extra travel time for heavy Patna traffic.
