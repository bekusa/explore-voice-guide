The guide

The story

Listen first. Far below, the Chenab River runs green and grey through the gorge. Wind moves across the slopes and carries the smell of pine and dry earth. Somewhere above, steel ticks and settles in the sun. Then your eye travels upward, and upward again, until it reaches a thin line of railway drawn across the sky. That line is a working track, and trains cross it every day.

This is the Chenab Rail Bridge, in the Reasi district of Jammu and Kashmir, in northern India. It holds the title of the highest railway arch bridge in the world. Its deck sits about 359 metres above the riverbed. To picture that, imagine the Eiffel Tower standing on the water below you. The top of the tower would still fall short of the track by a good margin. Many visitors only grasp this when a train appears and looks like a toy in the distance.

The bridge belongs to a much larger story. For generations, the Kashmir Valley had no railway link to the rest of India's network. Mountains, deep river gorges and fragile rock stood in the way. Road travel through the passes could close for days in winter snow or landslides. The answer was the Udhampur–Srinagar–Baramulla Rail Link, a line built through some of the hardest terrain any railway has crossed. This bridge is the crown of that line.

Look at the shape in front of you. The main structure is a single great steel arch that springs from one side of the gorge to the other. Above the arch, a row of tall columns carries the deck. On each side, long approach viaducts walk the track in across the slopes. Altogether the bridge runs for roughly 1.3 kilometres. The arch is not a solid curve. It is built from steel boxes and trusses, so light and wind pass straight through it.

Here is something most visitors miss. The engineers could not simply drop materials into the gorge. There were few roads at the bottom, and the slopes were steep and unstable. So builders first had to cut access roads into the mountains themselves. For the arch, they strung a huge cable crane between two tall pylons on opposite banks. Pieces of steel travelled across the valley on that cable. Then they were lowered into place, piece by piece, high above the river.

The two halves of the arch grew out from each bank, reaching toward each other over empty air. During this stage, each half had to be held back with heavy cables until the two could meet. That final joining happened in 2021. Workers on site described it as a moment of real emotion. Years of effort in heat, cold and wind came down to closing a gap far above the water.

The Himalayas are young mountains, and they are still moving. This region sits in an active earthquake zone. The bridge was therefore designed to handle strong seismic shaking. It was also designed for very high winds, which funnel hard through a gorge like this one. Engineers placed sensors on the structure to watch it in real time. They measure wind, movement and stress, and warn the operators when conditions become unsafe.

There is a second, quieter layer of protection. Because of the region's security situation, the bridge was built to resist blast damage. The steel was chosen to perform in cold temperatures without becoming brittle. Thick protective coatings guard against rust in the damp mountain air. The planners talk about this bridge in terms of more than a century of service. In other words, it was built for the grandchildren of the people who built it.

After the arch was finished, the deck, the track and the systems all had to follow. Test runs came first, with engineers watching every reading as trains rolled across. Then, in June 2025, the bridge was formally opened by India's Prime Minister. With that opening, passenger trains could finally run all the way from the Jammu side into the Kashmir Valley. For many families in the valley, it changed what a journey home could look like.

If you travel this line, you may notice something surprising. On a modern train, the crossing feels almost calm. There is no dramatic lurch, only a sudden openness at the windows. The ground falls away, and for a short time you see only sky and the far wall of the gorge. Passengers often go quiet, then reach for their phones all at once. Then the slope returns, and soon the track enters another tunnel.

That tunnel is a reminder of the whole route. This line is less a railway with tunnels than a chain of tunnels joined by bridges. Not far from here, also in Reasi district, stands the Anji Khad Bridge. It is India's first cable-stayed railway bridge, held up by a single tall pylon and fans of steel cables. Together, the two bridges show two very different ways to carry a train across a valley.

Now look at the land around you. The villages on these slopes are small, with terraced fields, goats on the paths and tea stalls by the roadside. For people here, the bridge years brought new roads, new work and a steady flow of visitors. You may hear Dogri, Hindi or Kashmiri spoken nearby. Ask a local what the gorge was like before the steel arrived. Many people remember the cranes, the noise, and the long wait.

Before you leave, take one more slow look. Follow the curve of the arch from one bank to the other. Notice how the river below keeps running, exactly as it did before anyone came here. Listen for the hum of a distant train, or simply the wind in the steel. The mountains are old and patient. This bridge is new. For now, the two of them share the same view... and so do you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the train across for the full sense of height.
Sit by a window and watch for the gorge opening.
Bring a warm layer; wind in the gorge is cold.
Visit in clear morning light for sharper mountain views.
Combine your trip with the nearby Anji Khad Bridge.
