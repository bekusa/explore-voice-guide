The guide

The story

Listen first. Water drips from the leaves above you. A bird calls somewhere in the green slope below. Under your feet the path is soft, often damp, and the air smells of wet earth and moss. Then, between the trees, a face appears. It is carved in grey stone. It has wide eyes, a broad nose, and a mouth full of fangs. It has stood here, in these southern Colombian mountains, for well over a thousand years. Welcome to San Agustín.

You are in the department of Huila, high in the Andes, close to the place where the Magdalena River begins. The Magdalena is Colombia's great river. It runs north for the length of the country to the Caribbean. Ancient people chose this headwater region for their dead and their gods. Around this park and the hills beyond, they left hundreds of stone figures. UNESCO describes the area as the largest group of religious monuments and megalithic sculptures in South America. A megalith is simply a monument made from very large stones.

Here is the first surprise. Nobody knows what these people called themselves. They left no writing that we can read. The Spanish name San Agustín comes from the colonial town, not from the sculptors. Archaeologists usually call them the San Agustín culture. Most of the great statues are thought to date from roughly the first to the eighth century of our era. That is long before the Inca, and in a different world entirely.

The statues are carved from volcanic rock. Look closely at the surface and you can see small grains and pores in the stone. The sculptors had no metal tools for this work, as far as we know. They shaped the rock with harder stones and abrasives, slowly, by hand. Some figures are small enough to sit beside a child. Others rise taller than a grown adult. Each one represents months of patient labour.

Most of the figures you will meet were not made to stand alone in a forest. They belonged to burial places. Across the park, earthen mounds covered stone tombs. Inside, flat slabs formed a kind of chamber, a little like a table of stone. At the entrance stood guardian figures, often flanked by pillars. Many are thought to have protected the dead on their journey. Over the centuries the earth wore away, and the stone guardians came back into the light.

These burial groups are called mesitas, which means small tables in Spanish. The name comes from the flattened, platform-like hills where the tombs sit. As you walk between them, notice how the landscape itself was shaped. The ground rises and falls in deliberate steps. People moved large amounts of earth here to create sacred spaces. The trees and grass that cover them today are recent. The design underneath is very old.

Now look at the faces again. Many have the fangs of a big cat, perhaps a jaguar. The jaguar was a powerful animal across the ancient Americas, linked with strength, the night, and the spirit world. On some statues a human body carries an animal face. On others, a second creature rides on the back or the head of the figure. Researchers call this the alter ego, or double self. It may show a shaman with his animal spirit. That reading is an interpretation, not a certainty.

Not every figure is fierce. Watch for statues that hold objects in their hands — a staff, a small animal, perhaps a child, perhaps a tool. Some show birds of prey with snakes in their beaks. Others show figures with simple, almost gentle expressions. And here is the second surprise. A few statues still carry faint traces of colour, in reds, yellows, and blacks. These figures were not always grey. Once, at least some of them were painted.

One of the most unusual places in the park is the Fountain of Lavapatas. Here the builders carved directly into the rock bed of a stream. Channels and small basins guide the water across the stone. Between them you can see carved reliefs of lizards, snakes, and human figures. Many experts think it was a place for ritual washing or ceremonies with water. Stand beside it for a moment. The sound of the running water is part of the design.

Above the fountain, the path climbs to the Alto de Lavapatas, one of the higher points in the park. The climb is steep, and on a rainy day it is slippery. At the top you find more tombs and guardian statues, and wide views across the green valleys. From here you begin to understand the setting. These people looked out over mountains, rivers, and clouds. The sacred sites sit on high ground, with long sight lines in every direction.

The story of outsiders at San Agustín is complicated. A Spanish friar described the statues in the eighteenth century. In the early twentieth century, the German ethnologist Konrad Theodor Preuss excavated here and studied the figures in detail. He also sent a number of statues to Berlin, where some remain in a museum. Many Colombians still feel that loss. The park today is a protected national site, and the work of study and protection continues.

Do not miss the Forest of Statues. This is a loop trail where dozens of sculptures from different parts of the region now stand along the path, under the trees. It is not their original setting. They were gathered here to protect them and to let visitors see them up close. The effect is quiet and a little strange. Around each bend in the trail, another stone face waits in the shade, wet with rain and dark with moss.

The small museum near the entrance is worth your time, ideally before you walk the trails. It shows pottery, tools, and smaller carved objects that give a human scale to the great statues. It helps you picture the people behind the stone. They farmed these slopes. They cooked, traded, buried their families, and honoured powers we can only guess at. The statues are the part that survived. Most of daily life has gone back into the earth.

Before you leave, stop at one figure, any figure, and simply look. Notice the weight of it, the marks of the tools, the green life growing in its cracks. Feel the cool, damp air of the mountains around you. Listen to the water still running toward the Magdalena. The sculptors stood in this same light and this same rain. Their names are gone. Their work is still here, facing you, watching the valley as it has for centuries.

What to look for

A second creature riding on a figure's back.

Faint traces of red or yellow paint on statues.
Stone pillars framing guardian figures at tomb entrances.
