The guide

The story

Tilt your head back. Then tilt it back a little further. The grey concrete cone in front of you narrows as it climbs, until it ends in a thin cross against the open Paraná sky. Close to the ground you may feel a breeze moving between the trees of the square. The surface of the tower is plain and smooth, and the sunlight slides along it. This is the Cathedral of Maringá, and it does not look like any church you were raised to expect.

Its full name is the Metropolitan Cathedral Basilica of Our Lady of Glory. It is the seat of the Archdiocese of Maringá, a city in the north of the Brazilian state of Paraná. The title of minor basilica is an honour that the Vatican gives to certain important churches. Locals usually skip the long name. They simply say the Cathedral, and everyone in the city knows which building they mean.

To understand this tower, it helps to understand the city around it. Maringá is young. It was founded in the late 1940s as a planned city, laid out by a colonisation company that was opening the rich red soil of northern Paraná to coffee farming. Its streets were drawn on paper before they were cut through the forest. Its parks, wide avenues and thousands of street trees were part of that plan. The cathedral was meant to crown the plan.

The driving force behind the project was the city's first bishop, Dom Jaime Luiz Coelho. He wanted a cathedral that would face the future rather than copy the past. The design is credited to the architect José Augusto Bellucci. Work began around the end of the 1950s and continued for well over a decade. The building was finally consecrated in the early 1970s. For a city that was then barely twenty-five years old, it was an enormous act of confidence.

Now the surprise that most visitors only learn later. With its cross, the tower rises more than one hundred metres above the square. It is often described as the tallest church in South America, and it is one of the tallest church buildings anywhere in the world. Many famous European cathedrals took centuries to build in stone. This one climbed in reinforced concrete — a modern material for a modern city.

Why a cone? There is a story that people in Maringá like to tell. The years of construction were also the first years of the space age. Soviet and American rockets were rising from their launch pads, and the whole world was watching the sky. According to this popular account, the shape draws on that energy — a building that points upward like a rocket. Whether or not the architect meant it, the comparison is easy to see from here.

There is also a gentler reading. A cone is one of the simplest shapes there is. It has no towers, buttresses or decorative spires that compete for attention. All of its lines lead the eye to a single point at the top. For a church, that idea is old even if the material is new. The building asks you to look up, and almost everyone standing in this square does exactly that.

Walk closer and step inside if the doors are open. The change of scale is immediate. The circular plan means there is no long nave stretching away from you. Instead, the space gathers around the altar at the centre. The walls lean inward as they rise, and the interior feels both tall and enclosed. Coloured light from the windows falls across the concrete and the floor, and it shifts as the sun moves through the day.

Listen for a moment. A round space handles sound differently from a long one. Footsteps, a cough or a quiet conversation can carry across the room in a way that might surprise you. During a Mass, music and voices fill the volume from all sides. If a service is in progress, it is worth standing still at the edge and letting the sound reach you. This is a working parish church, not a museum.

Here is a second detail that many visitors miss. The tower is not only something to look at. It can also be a place to look out from. Visitors have been able to climb to a viewing area high in the cone, reached partly by lift and partly on foot. From there the planned grid of Maringá spreads out below, green with trees. Access can change, so ask at the cathedral before you plan your visit around it.

Back at ground level, take in the square. The cathedral stands in an open plaza that acts as a meeting point for the city. Families cross it on their way to the shops and the avenues nearby. Students sit on the benches, and older residents walk slowly in the shade. On religious holidays the space fills with processions and crowds. On an ordinary afternoon it can be surprisingly calm, with birdsong coming from the trees.

If you want photographs, walk away from the building before you lift your camera. Up close, the tower is too tall to fit in a frame. From the far edge of the square or from the avenues that lead toward it, the full cone appears in one clean line. Late afternoon light warms the grey concrete. After dark, the lit tower stands out against the sky and becomes a landmark you can see across much of the city.

Before you go, look up one last time. Think about the young city that decided to build this, with fields and forest still close to its edges. It chose concrete over stone and a cone over a classical facade. As you walk out across the square, notice how often the cross at the top reappears between buildings and trees. In Maringá, the cathedral is the point the whole city keeps returning to.
