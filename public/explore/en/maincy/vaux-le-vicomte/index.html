The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel first. It crunches under every step as you walk the long avenue toward the house. Then the château comes into view: pale stone, a grey slate dome, and still water in the moat around the base. The air smells of cut grass and damp stone. For a moment it looks almost too calm, too perfect. But this house is the setting for one of the most dramatic falls in French history. It happened in a single summer, and it started with a party.

Welcome to Vaux-le-Vicomte, near the small village of Maincy, about an hour south-east of Paris. The house was built in the mid-seventeenth century for Nicolas Fouquet. Fouquet was superintendent of finances to the young King Louis the Fourteenth, which made him one of the most powerful men in the kingdom. He was rich and ambitious. He was also a generous patron of artists and writers. He wanted a country house that would show all of this at a glance.

To build it, Fouquet brought together three remarkable talents. Louis Le Vau designed the architecture. Charles Le Brun planned the paintings and the interior decoration. André Le Nôtre laid out the gardens. Work began in the late 1650s and moved at extraordinary speed. Whole villages were cleared to make room for the grounds. Thousands of workers dug, planted, carved and painted. The result was something France had not quite seen before: house, interiors and landscape all designed as one unified work.

Look up at the dome above the centre of the house. Beneath it sits the Grand Salon, an oval room that opens straight onto the gardens. The oval shape was unusual for a French country house at the time. Now here is a detail many visitors miss. The ceiling of this salon is plain. Le Brun had planned a great painted sky for it, full of allegory and celebration. That painting was never finished. The reason is the story of what happened next.

As you walk through the rooms, watch for a small animal. It is a squirrel, and it appears again and again in carvings and painted decoration. The squirrel was Fouquet's emblem. In the local dialect of his family's home region, the word for squirrel sounded like his name. His motto went with it, a Latin phrase that means roughly, to what heights will he not climb? It was meant as confidence. Today it reads more like a warning.

In August 1661, Fouquet held a great feast here in honour of the king. Louis the Fourteenth and his court came from nearby Fontainebleau. There was a lavish dinner, music, fountains playing across the gardens, and fireworks in the night sky. The playwright Molière presented a new comedy written for the occasion. Every guest saw the gilded rooms, the silver, the endless water. By every account, it was the most spectacular evening anyone could remember.

And that was the problem. The king was in his early twenties and had only recently taken personal control of government. He was already suspicious of Fouquet's finances. Seeing this display, in a house far grander than his own, seems to have hardened his decision. Less than three weeks later, Fouquet was arrested in the city of Nantes. The officer who carried out the arrest was d'Artagnan, the real musketeer who later inspired Alexandre Dumas's novels.

Fouquet never returned to Vaux. After a long trial, he spent the rest of his life imprisoned in a remote fortress in the Alps, and he died there in 1680. His house was sealed and much of its contents taken. Then the king did something telling. He hired Le Vau, Le Brun and Le Nôtre for his own grand project. That project grew into Versailles. In a real sense, the palace you may know so well began right here.

Now walk out through the salon onto the garden terrace. Le Nôtre designed this view to play with your eyes. From the house, the gardens look like one smooth, gently sloping plane. Pools, lawns and patterned beds seem to sit close together. As you walk forward, the ground surprises you. Hidden changes in level appear. Distances stretch. Pools that looked round turn out to be different shapes. The garden only shows itself slowly, step by step.

The biggest surprise comes toward the far end. From the terrace, you cannot see the long canal that crosses the garden. It lies lower, hidden in a dip in the ground. You only discover it when you reach the edge. Beyond it rises a grotto with carved figures, and above that, on the hillside, a large gilded statue of Hercules. That final statue is the point your eye travels to from the very beginning.

After Fouquet, the estate passed through several owners. Over time it fell into neglect, and parts of the gardens nearly disappeared. In the late nineteenth century, it was bought by Alfred Sommier, an industrialist who had made his fortune in sugar. He and his family began a long restoration of the house and grounds. His descendants still own and care for the estate today, which is rare for a French château of this size.

That private care shapes the visit. The rooms feel lived in, not frozen. You may find tables set, carriages in the stables, and small displays about the people who worked here. On some summer evenings, the house and gardens are lit by thousands of candles, a reminder of that famous August night. Check the estate's own programme if you would like to see it. The glow along the water is a sight that stays with people.

Before you leave, turn around once more and look back at the house from the garden. Notice how the dome sits exactly on the line of the main path. Notice the reflection of the stone in the still water of the moat. Listen to the gravel again under your feet. This is the view Fouquet imagined showing to his king. He had it for only one evening. You can stand in it for as long as you like.
