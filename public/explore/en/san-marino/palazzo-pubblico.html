The guide

The story

Start with the stone under your feet. The square here is paved and slightly uneven, worn smooth in places by many shoes. The air is often cooler than in the valley below, and a breeze moves across the open side of the piazza. In front of you rises a pale grey building with battlements along its roofline and a slim clock tower. This is the Palazzo Pubblico, the town hall of San Marino, and the working home of one of the oldest republics in the world.

The square has an official name, Piazza della Libertà, which means Liberty Square. Locals also call it the Pianello, the little flat place. That nickname makes sense once you look around. San Marino sits on the ridges of Monte Titano, a steep limestone mountain. Level ground is rare up here. So this small, flat terrace became the natural stage for public life, for announcements, ceremonies, and meetings of the community.

Look at the building itself. It looks medieval, with its crenellated top, its pointed arches, and its sturdy tower. But here is the first surprise. The palace you see today is not medieval at all. It was built in the late nineteenth century, between 1884 and 1894, in a revival style that architects call neo-Gothic. That means it borrows the look of the Middle Ages on purpose. The design came from the Roman architect Francesco Azzurri.

Why build a new palace that looks old? The answer is about identity. An older public building, known in records as the Domus Magna Comunis, the Great House of the Community, stood on this site before. By the late nineteenth century, much of Italy around San Marino was being united into one kingdom. San Marino stayed independent. A town hall that recalled the age of free medieval communes was a clear statement in stone. This small state wanted to show it had always governed itself.

Now take a closer look at the facade. Much of the stone comes from the local mountain, so the palace and the rock below it share the same pale colour. Above the entrance and across the front, you can spot carved coats of arms. One shows three towers on three peaks, the symbol of San Marino. Those three towers stand on the crests of Monte Titano, and on a clear day you can see some of them from the edges of town.

This is the second surprise for many visitors. The Palazzo Pubblico is not a museum piece. It is a working seat of government. Inside, the Great and General Council meets. This is the parliament of San Marino, with sixty members. The building is also the official place of the two Captains Regent. These two people serve together as heads of state. They hold the role for only six months at a time.

That short term is very old in spirit. The idea is simple. Power is shared between two people, and it changes hands often, so nobody holds it for too long. Each year, new Captains Regent are installed on the first of April and on the first of October. On those days, the square fills with officials in formal dress, guards in uniform, and music. If you visit on one of those dates, you will see the palace used for exactly the purpose it was built for.

Turn now to the statue in the middle of the piazza. It is a white marble figure of a woman, the Statue of Liberty of San Marino. It was carved by the sculptor Stefano Galletti and given to the Republic in 1876. The donor was a German noblewoman, Otilia Heyroth-Wagener. Look at the crown on the figure's head. It is shaped like three towers, the same symbol you saw on the facade. Liberty and the mountain are linked in one image.

The story of how San Marino began belongs to legend. According to tradition, a stonemason named Marinus came from the island of Rab, across the Adriatic Sea, around the year 301. He is said to have fled persecution of Christians and settled on this mountain with a small community. Locals say his last words left the people free from any other ruler. Historians cannot confirm these details, but the tale shapes how the Sammarinese see themselves. Saint Marinus appears again and again in art inside the palace.

Here is one fact that surprises many English speakers. In 1861, during the American Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln wrote a letter to the Captains Regent. San Marino had offered him honorary citizenship. In his reply, Lincoln praised the small republic for its long and stable government. It is a modest moment in history, but it shows how this tiny state was watched with interest by people far away who were trying to build republics of their own.

At the end of the twentieth century, the palace needed serious care. A major restoration took place in the 1990s, led by the Italian architect Gae Aulenti, who was also known for her work on museums in Paris and Italy. The work cleaned and repaired the stone and updated the interiors, while keeping the outward look of the building. Today, the palace looks sharp and well kept. In 2008, the historic centre of San Marino and Monte Titano were added to the UNESCO World Heritage list.

During the warmer months, watch for the changing of the guard in front of the palace. The guards come from the Guardia di Rocca, a military corps whose name means Guard of the Fortress. Their uniforms are bright, often green and red, and the ceremony is short and precise. Find a spot with a clear view of the main door a little before it begins. Then walk to the open edge of the square. On a clear day, the land drops away and you can look out over the hills toward the Adriatic coast.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look again at the battlements, the clock tower, and the white figure of Liberty. Listen to the voices in many languages, the footsteps on the paving, perhaps a bell from the tower. This square is small, but it has held the public life of a free community for a very long time. As you walk on along the steep lanes, notice how often you see those three towers again. They are carved, painted, and printed all over town.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit on April 1 or October 1 for installation ceremonies.
Arrive early to watch the summer changing of the guard.
Walk to the square's open edge for valley views.
Wear shoes with good grip for steep, uneven lanes.
Check ahead, since council sessions can limit interior access.
