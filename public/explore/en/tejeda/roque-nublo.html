The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. The path under your boots is loose and gritty, a mix of grey and rust-coloured volcanic stone that crunches with every step. The air up here is thin and dry, and it often smells of pine resin warmed by the sun. Then the trees open, the trail climbs a last rocky shoulder, and there it is. A single column of rock rises out of the mountaintop and stands against the sky.

This is Roque Nublo, in the high centre of Gran Canaria, above the village of Tejeda. The monolith rises roughly eighty metres from its base. Its summit sits at about 1,813 metres above sea level. That makes it one of the highest points on the island. From many places across the interior, you can pick out its shape on the skyline. It looks like a thumb, or a finger, pointing straight up.

The name is a small clue. In Spanish, the word nublo relates to clouds and cloudy weather. On many days, banks of cloud roll up from the north and wrap around the rock. One moment the column is sharp and clear. The next, it fades into white mist and the temperature drops. If you are lucky, you may watch this happen in real time. The cloud moves fast up here, and the light changes with it.

So how does a tower like this form? The answer lies deep in the island's volcanic past. Several million years ago, this part of Gran Canaria was a large volcano. Powerful eruptions and collapses left behind thick layers of rock fragments, cemented together into a rough, bumpy stone. Geologists call this kind of rock a breccia. Look closely at the surface and you can see it — chunks of different stones, set together like pieces in coarse concrete.

What you see today is what survived. Over enormous spans of time, wind, rain and frost wore away the softer material around it. The harder core stayed standing. In other words, nobody built Roque Nublo. It is a remnant, the last piece left after the land around it was carried away. That is the first surprise for many visitors. This tall column is not a growth on the mountain. It is what remains after everything else eroded.

Now turn your attention to its neighbours. Close by stands a smaller pillar known as El Fraile, which means the Friar. Many people say it looks like a monk in a hooded robe, perhaps praying, perhaps turned towards the larger rock. Nearby you may also hear locals point out La Rana, the Frog, a rounded shape that some see as a crouching animal. Spend a minute here. Shapes like these change as you walk around them.

Long before hikers arrived, this mountaintop mattered to the island's first people. The aboriginal Canarians lived on Gran Canaria before the Castilian conquest in the fifteenth century. Archaeologists have found that high places in this region held sacred meaning for them. Roque Nublo is widely understood to have been one of those places. Exactly which rituals took place here is not fully known. What is clear is that people looked up at this rock with respect for a very long time.

That sacred layer is part of why the wider landscape has international recognition. In 2019, the cultural landscape known as Risco Caído and the Sacred Mountains of Gran Canaria was added to UNESCO's World Heritage list. It protects caves, granaries and sacred sites in the mountains around Tejeda. The rock also stands inside a protected rural park. So the rules here are about care. Stay on the marked paths, and leave the stones where they lie.

Now look outward. From the plateau, the view drops into the great basin often called the Caldera de Tejeda, a wide, broken bowl of ridges and ravines. On the far side, you may see another lone rock, Roque Bentayga, which also held deep meaning for the aboriginal people. And on a clear day, look west across the sea. Floating above the clouds is the peak of Teide, the great volcano on the neighbouring island of Tenerife.

The Spanish writer Miguel de Unamuno visited this area in the early twentieth century. He described the landscape around Tejeda as a petrified storm. Stand here for a moment and the phrase makes sense. The ridges rise and fall like frozen waves. The rocks lean at strange angles. It feels as if a huge movement of earth stopped in the middle of its motion, and simply stayed that way.

The forest you walked through on the way up matters too. Much of it is Canary pine, a tree that grows only in the Canary Islands. Its long needles catch moisture from passing clouds, and drops of water fall to the soil below. The tree has thick bark that helps it survive wildfires. After a fire, the trunks can turn black, yet green needles often sprout again. Notice the soft carpet of fallen needles along the trail.

A few practical thoughts while you are here. The walk from the roadside parking area is fairly short, but it climbs, and the ground is uneven. Good shoes help a lot. The weather can change fast, so a warm layer is useful even on a hot day down on the coast. And the wind at the top can be strong. Keep a safe distance from the edges, especially with children.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the base of the column. Put your hand flat against the rough stone, if the path allows. Feel how it holds the warmth of the sun, or the cold of the cloud. Listen to the wind moving across the plateau. As you walk back down through the pines, glance over your shoulder. The rock will stay in view for a while... and then the trees will close behind you.
