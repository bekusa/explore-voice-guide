The guide

The story

Listen first. Below the walls, the river Gwendraeth slides toward the sea, and on a still day you can hear water moving over mud and stones. The air carries salt from the estuary. Underfoot, the grass gives way to worn grey stone, cool even in summer. Above you, round towers rise against the sky, their surfaces patched with lichen in pale greens and yellows. You are standing at Kidwelly Castle, a short drive west of Llanelli, in one of the best-preserved medieval fortresses in Wales.

The position is the first thing to understand. The castle sits on a steep bank above the river, close to the point where the Gwendraeth meets the estuary. In the Middle Ages, this meant control of a crossing, a route along the coast, and access by water. Ships could come up toward the town. Armies moving along south Wales had to pass this way. Whoever held this ridge held a key to the region.

The story begins with the Normans, early in the twelfth century. After the Norman conquest of England, Norman lords pushed steadily into south Wales. The first castle here was not stone at all. It was built of earth and timber, and it is linked to Roger, Bishop of Salisbury, a powerful royal official of that era. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The great curved bank that wraps around the castle today is the shape of that first defence. The stone walls you see were later fitted into an older Norman plan.

This is a border landscape, and the Welsh did not accept these new lords quietly. The most famous story from these years belongs to Gwenllian, a Welsh princess and the wife of the prince Gruffydd ap Rhys. In 1136, while her husband was away seeking allies, she led a force against the Norman garrison near Kidwelly. The attack failed. Gwenllian was killed in the fighting. A field north of the town still carries her name, Maes Gwenllian, which means Gwenllian's Field.

She is remembered in Wales as a symbol of resistance, and some writers describe her as one of the few medieval women known to have led troops into battle. Around her name, folklore has grown as well. Locals say her headless ghost walks near the castle, searching for her lost head. That tale is legend, not history. But it shows how deeply the memory of that day settled into this valley.

For more than a century, the castle changed hands several times. Welsh princes captured it, Norman and English lords took it back, and the timber defences were burned and rebuilt. The great transformation came in the later thirteenth century. In this period the castle was rebuilt in stone on a far more ambitious design. That design is the reason Kidwelly matters so much to historians of castles.

Look at the core of the castle, the inner ward. It is a square courtyard enclosed by high walls, with a round tower at each corner. Around it runs a second, outer wall, which follows the curve of the old Norman bank. This is called a concentric castle — a castle with one ring of walls inside another. An attacker who broke through the outer wall found himself in a narrow space, still facing a higher inner wall, with defenders shooting down from both. It is the same thinking you find in the great royal castles of north Wales.

But Kidwelly is unusual. A true concentric castle forms a full ring. Here the outer wall makes only a half circle, a D shape. The steep river bank on the east side served as the missing half of the defence. The builders saw no need to wall in a cliff. As you walk the outer ward, notice how the curtain wall simply stops where the ground falls away toward the water.

Now find the chapel tower, on the side facing the river. It juts out from the inner ward and drops down the slope. At its base, look for the massive sloping supports, called spur buttresses, which spread outward like the feet of a pyramid. They hold the tower firmly onto the hillside. Inside, the chapel was the spiritual heart of the household. Its position, high above the river, also kept it relatively safe from attack.

The last great addition is the one that greets you on arrival — the great gatehouse at the south end. It is the largest single structure in the castle, a tall block of stone flanked by towers, with an entrance passage designed to trap and slow anyone trying to force their way in. Building work on the gatehouse stretched into the early fifteenth century. Its completion was delayed partly by the rising of Owain Glyndŵr, the Welsh leader who rebelled against English rule around 1400. In 1403, his supporters besieged Kidwelly. The town suffered, but the castle held.

For much of its later life, Kidwelly belonged to the house of Lancaster, one of the most powerful noble families in medieval England. Through that family the castle passed to the English crown. As warfare changed and Wales became more settled, castles like this lost their military purpose. Kidwelly slowly shifted from fortress to administrative centre, and then to ruin. Yet the walls were never deliberately torn down. That is why so much still stands at full height today.

Here is one more surprise. Kidwelly has had a small career on film. The castle is often identified as a location used in the opening scenes of the 1975 comedy Monty Python and the Holy Grail. It is a strange afterlife for a place built for real war. When you walk the wall walks and climb the towers, though, it is easy to see why filmmakers came. Few castles in Britain keep their medieval outline so complete.

Before you leave, take time to look beyond the walls. From the towers you can see the little town of Kidwelly, with the tall spire of its medieval church of Saint Mary. Nearby stands a surviving gate from the old town defences. To the south, the land opens toward the estuary, flat and silver under the changing light. The castle and the town grew up together. The market, the church and the river trade all depended on these walls.

So pause here for a moment. Feel the stone under your hand, rough and cold, shaped by medieval masons. Listen again to the river below, still running its old course past the cliff. Think of the timber fort, the burned walls, Gwenllian's fight, and the long rebuilding in stone. As you walk back through the great gatehouse, look up at the dark passage overhead. Many people have walked through this gate before you, and you are now one of them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the corner towers for views over the Gwendraeth estuary.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone steps and wall walks.
Walk to Saint Mary's Church in town after your visit.
Visit Maes Gwenllian field to connect with Gwenllian's story.
Bring a layer, wind off the estuary can feel cold.
