The guide

The story

Stand still on the square for a moment and listen. Bicycle bells, the click of tyres on brick, students calling to each other. Then look down. Under your feet, the paving shows a pattern of darker stones. They trace the outline of something that is no longer here. You are standing inside a church that lost its middle more than three hundred years ago. Welcome to the Dom, the Cathedral of Saint Martin, the old heart of Utrecht.

To understand this place, start long before the cathedral. Roughly where you stand, the Romans built a small fort on the edge of their empire, beside a crossing of the Rhine. That fort is one of the reasons Utrecht exists at all. Centuries later, around the year 700, an English missionary named Willibrord arrived here. He became the first bishop of Utrecht, and he dedicated a church on this spot to Saint Martin of Tours.

Saint Martin is still everywhere in this city. He was a Roman soldier, later a bishop, remembered for cutting his cloak in half to share it with a beggar. You will find him on signs, on the city's emblems, and in the name of this cathedral. Several churches stood on this ground before the one you see now. Fire and rebuilding came again and again, as they did for most medieval churches.

The Gothic cathedral we know began to rise in the mid thirteenth century. Gothic, if the word is new to you, is the style of pointed arches, tall windows, and stone ribs that let walls reach upward and fill with light. The builders here took inspiration from the great cathedrals of France. Construction moved slowly. It took generations, and money often ran short, so the work paused and restarted many times.

Now turn to the tower. The Dom Tower rises about 112 metres, and it is the tallest church tower in the Netherlands. It was built in the fourteenth century, and it has dominated the flat skyline ever since. In a country with no mountains, a tower like this was a landmark for travellers across many kilometres of fields and water. Its shape is lighter at the top, a stone lantern above a solid square base.

Here is the first surprise. Look between the tower and the church behind it. There is a gap — a whole street and a square. They were never meant to be separate. A long nave once joined them. In the summer of 1674, a violent storm swept over Utrecht. The weak, unfinished nave collapsed. The tower survived. The choir and transept survived. The middle simply fell.

The city never rebuilt it. Money was scarce, and the rubble stayed for decades before it was finally cleared. Over time, the empty space became Domplein, the square where you may be standing now. That is why the paving marks matter. They show where the pillars of the lost nave once stood. If you walk along them, you are walking down an aisle with no roof and no walls.

Walk around to the end of the surviving church, where the nave once joined it. You will see a flat, plainer wall closing off the building. It was added after the collapse to seal the open wound. Above it, look for the flying buttresses — the stone arms that brace the walls from the outside. Here they stand a little exposed, like scaffolding that someone forgot to take down.

The second surprise waits inside. The cathedral was Catholic for most of its life. In the sixteenth century, the Reformation reached the Low Countries. In 1566, waves of image-breaking swept through churches, and statues were smashed or defaced. Not long after, the Dom became a Protestant church, and it still serves a Protestant congregation today. Inside, look for carved stone reliefs where the faces of saints were chipped away. The damage was left in place.

The interior feels spacious and calm, with pale stone and tall clear windows. Protestant worship placed the sermon at the centre, so much of the colour and ornament you might expect in a Catholic cathedral is gone. What remains is the architecture itself — the clustered columns, the high vaults, and the light. Some tombs and memorials survive along the walls. Take a slow walk around the choir and look upward.

On one side of the church, a door leads to the Pandhof, a cloister garden. A cloister is a covered walkway around a courtyard, once used by clergy for walking and reflection. Today the garden is planted with herbs and flowers, and it is one of the quietest spots in central Utrecht. Next to it stands a hall, now part of the university, where in 1579 the Union of Utrecht was signed. That treaty helped lay the foundation of the Dutch Republic.

If you have time, the tower can be climbed with a guided tour. The stairs are steep and narrow, and there are several hundred of them. On the way up, you pass the bells, some of them cast in the early sixteenth century and still rung on special occasions. From the top, on a clear day, the view runs across the city's canals and rooftops toward the green countryside beyond. Below the square, you can also explore the archaeological remains of the Roman fort and earlier churches.

Before you leave, step back to the edge of Domplein and take in all three pieces at once. The tower on one side. The church on the other. And between them, open air where a nave once stood. It is an unfinished sentence written in stone. Listen again — the bicycles, the voices, perhaps a bell overhead. Life simply moved into the gap the storm left behind.
