The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, traffic hums on a raised road. In front of you, a quiet row of dark brick houses runs down the street. Then, at the very end of the row, everything changes. Flat white planes. Thin grey walls. A slim red line, a yellow beam, a black window frame. It looks as if someone took the end wall of an ordinary terrace and pulled it apart into floating panels. This is the Rietveld Schröder House, and it is smaller than most people expect.

The house was built in 1924, here on the edge of Utrecht. At that time, this spot was where the city stopped. Beyond the garden there were open fields and wet Dutch meadows, flat to the horizon. The house turned its back on the brick street and faced that open land. Keep that picture in mind. The view the house was designed for no longer exists, and we will come back to why.

Two people made this house. The first was Gerrit Rietveld, a furniture maker from Utrecht. He was the son of a cabinetmaker and trained in his father's workshop. He was not a qualified architect when he designed this building. The second was Truus Schröder-Schräder, a young widow with three children. She wanted a home that broke with the heavy, formal interiors she had grown up with. She wanted light, openness, and freedom for her family.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. Truus Schröder was not simply the client. She worked closely with Rietveld on the design, and today she is widely recognised as a co-creator of the house. Many of its ideas about daily life came from her. How should children play? How should a family share space during the day and separate it at night? Those questions shaped the rooms as much as any theory of art did.

That theory had a name: De Stijl, which simply means The Style in Dutch. It was a group of Dutch artists and designers active in the years around and after the First World War. Painter Piet Mondrian is its best-known member. De Stijl artists used straight lines, right angles, and a strict palette. They chose the three primary colours — red, yellow, and blue — together with white, grey, and black. This house is often described as the clearest example of those ideas in a building.

Look at the facade again. No single wall closes the corner. Instead, separate planes slide past each other. A white slab sticks out beyond the window. A balcony seems to hang in the air. Thin lines of colour mark the steel beams and posts. The effect is deliberate. Rietveld wanted the house to look like a set of parts in balance, not a solid box. It is the same thinking he used in his furniture, where every board and rail stays visibly separate.

That furniture is worth a short detour in your mind. Around the end of the First World War, Rietveld designed a chair of flat boards and square rails, later painted red, blue, black, and yellow. It became known as the Red Blue Chair. Now look back at the house. You can see the same language, simply at a larger scale. Some people call this building a chair you can live in. It is not a bad description.

Here is the second surprise. The house looks modern and machine-made, so most visitors assume it is concrete. It is not, mostly. The walls are largely brick, finished with plaster to look smooth and uniform. Steel and timber carry much of the structure. The materials were quite traditional. The new idea was in how they were arranged and painted. Modern looks, ordinary methods... that tension runs through the whole building.

Inside, the two floors feel very different. The ground floor is closer to a normal house, with separate rooms. The upper floor is the real experiment. During the day, it can be one open living space full of light. At night, sliding and folding partitions divide it into separate rooms for sleeping. According to a well-known story, the upper floor was presented on the plans as an attic, which helped this unusual layout pass the local building rules.

Look up at the corner window on the upper floor, if you are standing where you can see it. When the windows are open, the corner simply disappears. There is no post at the angle. Inside and outside meet with nothing between them. Small details like this fill the house: built-in cupboards, fold-down surfaces, clever storage. Everything was designed so a family could live well in a compact space. A guided visit lets you see the partitions moving.

Truus Schröder lived here for most of her life, until her death in 1985. Rietveld also kept a studio in the house for a period. Later in his life, after his wife died, he lived here himself until he died in 1964. So this was never a showpiece. It was a working home for decades, and it changed with the people inside it. After Truus Schröder's death, the house was restored and opened to the public.

Now, about that missing view. In the 1960s, a road was built right beside the house, on a raised embankment. The open meadows that the house once faced were cut off. That is the traffic you heard at the start. It is a strange fate for a building designed around openness. In 2000, the house was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List. Today it is managed by the Centraal Museum in Utrecht.

Before you leave, take a few steps back down the street. Look at the brick terrace and the white house side by side. They were built close together in time, yet they belong to different worlds. One looks back to the nineteenth century. The other looks forward to everything that came after. Notice how small the house really is. Then notice how much attention it still holds. Walk slowly away, and turn around once more at the corner. The planes shift again as you move.
