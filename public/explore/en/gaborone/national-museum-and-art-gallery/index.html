The guide

The story

The building sits low and unassuming along Independence Avenue, its white facade catching the midday glare. Inside, the air shifts — cooler, quieter, the polished floors reflecting soft overhead light. This is Gaborone's National Museum and Art Gallery, the country's oldest public institution of its kind, opened in 1968 just two years after Botswana gained independence. The timing was no accident. The new nation needed a place to hold its story, to gather the fragments of a past that colonial rule had largely ignored.

The museum grew from modest beginnings. In the early years, the collection fit into a few rooms — ethnographic objects, archaeological finds from the Tsodilo Hills, photographs of early settlements. Over decades, it expanded to fill the current complex, adding galleries for contemporary art, natural history, and rotating exhibitions. The building itself has been extended several times, each addition reflecting a different era of national confidence. Walk through the sequence of spaces and you move through Botswana's own sense of itself, from cautious preservation to bold cultural assertion.

The ethnographic galleries anchor the permanent collection. Here you find the material culture of Botswana's various communities — Tswana, Kalanga, Herero, San, Yei, Mbukushu. Baskets woven from palm fronds, clay pots with geometric incisions, hunting tools, musical instruments, ceremonial dress. Each object is labeled with the community of origin, the materials used, the function it served. The displays avoid romanticizing or freezing these cultures in an imagined past. Many of the techniques shown are still practiced today, in villages and urban workshops alike.

One gallery focuses on the San peoples, whose ancestors lived in this region for tens of thousands of years. The collection includes bows, quivers, ostrich-shell beads, digging sticks, and reproductions of rock art from sites across the Kalahari. The San relationship with the land — reading animal tracks, finding water in dry riverbeds, using plant toxins for hunting — is presented not as folklore but as a sophisticated body of knowledge. The museum has worked with San communities to ensure the interpretation respects their own voices, though debates about representation and access continue.

Upstairs, the art galleries shift the mood. Botswana's contemporary art scene is small but vigorous, and the museum has championed it since the nineteen eighties. You will see bold canvases by artists like Thebe Phetogo and Philip Segola, sculptural work in wood and metal, mixed-media installations that wrestle with urbanization, identity, and the tension between tradition and modernity. The palette tends toward earth tones — ochres, umbers, burnt siennas — echoing the Kalahari landscape that dominates so much of the national imagination.

The natural history section occupies the ground floor's western wing. Dioramas show the wildlife of the Okavango Delta and the Chobe floodplains — elephants, hippos, lechwe, crocodiles — set against painted backdrops of papyrus and acacia. The taxidermy is competent if not spectacular, and some specimens date back to the museum's founding. What makes the section valuable is its focus on Botswana's ecosystems rather than generic African wildlife. The displays explain the delta's seasonal flood cycle, the adaptations of desert-dwelling animals, the delicate balance between cattle ranching and wildlife conservation.

A smaller gallery documents Botswana's colonial history under the British Protectorate of Bechuanaland, from 1885 to 1966. The tone is measured, neither celebratory nor bitter. You see photographs of early administrators, maps showing the borders negotiated with neighboring territories, documents from the independence negotiations. The museum does not shy from the extractive economics of the protectorate — cattle exports, labor migration to South African mines — but it also highlights the relative autonomy Tswana chiefs retained compared to colonized peoples elsewhere on the continent.

The archive and research wing, not always open to casual visitors, holds the museum's deeper treasures. Oral history recordings, archaeological reports from Iron Age sites, colonial-era correspondence, film reels from the independence era. Scholars from the University of Botswana and international institutions use the archive regularly. If you are serious about research, you can request access in advance, though expect bureaucratic patience to be required.

The museum hosts temporary exhibitions several times a year, often in partnership with regional institutions. Recent shows have featured contemporary photography from southern Africa, traditional pottery techniques, and a retrospective of Botswana's first generation of post-independence visual artists. The quality varies, but the ambition is genuine — this is a museum trying to be more than a static repository, trying to engage with living culture and current debates.

Outside, a small sculpture garden runs along the building's northern side. Works by local artists stand among indigenous trees — leadwood, marula, camelthorn. The pieces range from abstract forms in welded steel to figurative carvings in wood. It is a pleasant spot to pause between galleries, especially in the late afternoon when the harsh light softens and the shadows lengthen across the grass.

The museum's café, near the main entrance, serves light meals and cold drinks. The space is functional rather than charming, but it fills with students, artists, and the occasional diplomatic visitor. The walls display rotating works by emerging artists, for sale at modest prices. If you are looking to support Botswana's art scene directly, this is a straightforward way to do it.

The gift shop stocks books on Botswana's history and culture, reproductions of traditional crafts, and some contemporary design objects. The selection is curated with care — nothing feels mass-produced or generic. Proceeds support the museum's programs, which operate on a lean budget despite the institution's national importance.

As you step back outside into the Gaborone heat, the museum's quiet modesty stays with you. This is not a grand monument to imperial plunder or a slick temple of global art. It is a place built by a young country to hold its own memory, to argue with its past, to imagine its future. The stories it tells are still being written, and the objects on display are part of a conversation that continues in the streets and villages beyond these walls.
