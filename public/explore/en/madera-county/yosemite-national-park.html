The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere below you, water is moving over granite. It is a steady hush that never quite stops. The air carries pine resin, warm stone, and a cool edge that drifts down from the high country. Underfoot, the ground is a mix of needles, sand, and pale grey rock worn smooth by time. This is Yosemite. Before any story, it is simply a place of rock, water, and light.

Yosemite is large. It covers a wide stretch of the central Sierra Nevada, the long mountain range that runs down eastern California. The park spans several counties, and a slice of its wilder southern country lies within Madera County. Most visitors know only the famous valley. But the valley is a small part of the whole. Around it spread forests, meadows, granite domes, and alpine lakes that many people never see.

Start with the shape of the land. The famous valley is shaped like a broad letter U, not a narrow V. That shape tells a story. Rivers cut V-shaped canyons. Glaciers carve wide, flat-floored troughs. Over long ages, rivers of ice moved through here and ground the valley deeper and wider. When the ice melted, it left sheer walls behind. The Merced River now runs quietly across the floor that the glaciers left.

The rock itself is granite. It formed deep underground from slowly cooling molten rock, long before it ever saw daylight. Erosion stripped away the layers above it. Then the granite began to crack and peel in curved sheets, a little like the layers of an onion. Geologists call this exfoliation. It is one reason so many domes here look smooth and rounded. Look at any bare cliff, and you may see these curving slabs.

Two great rock forms anchor the valley. El Capitan is a single wall of granite that rises close to a kilometre above the valley floor. Climbers spend days on its face, sleeping on ledges and in hanging tents. At dusk you may spot their headlamps, tiny points of light high on the wall. Half Dome looks as if someone sliced a rounded mountain clean in two. Its flat face has become a symbol of the whole park.

Then there is the water. Yosemite Falls drops in stages down the valley's northern wall. It ranks among the tallest waterfalls in North America. Here is something many visitors miss. The falls depend almost entirely on melting snow. In late spring, the roar can shake the air and soak you with mist. By late summer, the falls often shrink to a thin thread. Sometimes they vanish completely, leaving only a dark streak on the rock.

Long before this was a park, it was a home. Native peoples lived in and around the valley for thousands of years. The people of the valley are often called the Ahwahneechee, after their name for the place, Ahwahnee. They gathered acorns from the black oaks, which provided a staple food. They also tended the meadows with fire to keep them open. The open meadows that visitors admire today were partly shaped by that careful human work.

That way of life changed sharply in the mid nineteenth century. The California Gold Rush brought miners and settlers into the Sierra foothills. Conflict followed. In 1851, an armed militia called the Mariposa Battalion entered the valley in pursuit of the people who lived there. Its members were among the first outsiders to describe the valley in writing. It is a painful beginning, and it is part of the true story of this place.

Now comes a surprise. Yosemite was protected before the United States had any national parks at all. In 1864, during the Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln signed a law that granted Yosemite Valley and the Mariposa Grove of giant sequoias to the state of California. The land was to be kept for public use and recreation. Historians often see this grant as an early seed of the national park idea.

One name is linked closely with Yosemite: John Muir. He was a Scottish-born naturalist who walked these mountains for years and wrote about them with deep feeling. He argued that the glaciers had carved the valley, an idea many experts doubted at the time. He also pushed for wider protection. In 1890, Congress created Yosemite National Park around the valley. Later, the valley itself became part of the national park.

Not every battle was won. North of the main valley lies Hetch Hetchy, a second glacier-carved valley that Muir loved. In the early twentieth century, the city of San Francisco wanted it as a reservoir. Muir fought hard against the plan. In 1913, Congress approved the dam, and the valley was later flooded. That fight helped shape the American conservation movement. People still debate whether the valley should one day be drained.

The trees offer their own quiet drama. Giant sequoias grow in several groves within the park. They rank among the largest living things on Earth by volume. Their thick, spongy, reddish bark helps them survive fire. In fact, fire helps them reproduce, because heat opens their cones and clears the ground for seedlings. Stand beside one and look straight up. Your neck tires long before your eyes reach the top branches.

The park changes greatly with the seasons. In winter, snow quiets the valley and closes many high roads. Spring brings full waterfalls and green meadows. Summer opens the high country, with its lakes and granite slopes above the trees. In February, on a few clear evenings, low sunlight can strike a small waterfall called Horsetail Fall and make it glow orange like fire. Crowds gather for it, hoping the weather cooperates.

So take a moment where you are. Notice the colour of the rock, grey or gold, depending on the hour. Listen for water, wind, or the call of a Steller's jay, a crested blue bird that lives here. Everything around you was shaped by ice, by fire, and by people who cared for it or fought over it. As you walk on, look up now and then. The cliffs will still be here, long after the light changes.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check whether a vehicle reservation is needed before you drive in.
Visit in late spring to see waterfalls at full force.
Store all food in the provided bear-proof lockers.
Bring layers, because mountain temperatures shift quickly.
Arrive early to find parking near the busiest trailheads.
