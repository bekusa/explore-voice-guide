The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here the wind moves through dry grass and over bare grey rock, and almost nothing else makes a sound. The stone under your feet is rough and crusted with pale lichen. It is old volcanic rock, broken into ledges and blocks. Look out across the hills of northeastern North Macedonia and the horizon is wide in every direction. That wide, clear horizon is the reason you have come.

This is Kokino. It takes its name from the small village below, in the municipality of Staro Nagoričane, not far from the town of Kumanovo. The rocky summit itself is known locally as Tatićev Kamen, which means something like the Father's Stone. It rises to roughly a thousand metres. Today it looks empty and quiet. But for a long period in prehistory, people climbed here with a clear purpose. They came to watch the sky.

The site was identified in 2001 by the archaeologist Jovica Stankovski, who worked with the museum in nearby Kumanovo. At first it looked like an ordinary hilltop scattered with pottery fragments. Then researchers began to look closely at the shape of the rock itself. Certain cuts and notches in the stone did not look natural. They seemed to line up with points on the eastern horizon. That observation changed how everyone understood this hill.

Here is the central idea. The sun does not rise in the same place every day. Across the year, its rising point slides along the horizon — north towards midsummer, south towards midwinter. If you sit in exactly the same spot every morning, you can mark these movements against fixed features on the skyline. Researchers who studied Kokino argue that its builders did just that. They used notches in the rock as sightlines for the solstices and the equinoxes.

The solstices are the longest and shortest days of the year. The equinoxes are the two days when day and night are roughly equal. For a farming community, knowing these turning points mattered a great deal. It told people when to sow, when to harvest, and when to hold festivals. A calendar written on the landscape needs no paper. It only needs a fixed seat, a clear horizon, and patience across many years.

That fixed seat is the first surprise. Look for the stone seats carved into the rock on the lower platform. People often call them thrones. They face east, towards the sunrise. The idea is that an observer — perhaps a priest, perhaps a respected elder — sat in one of these seats and watched the light appear through a particular notch. The seat fixed the eye in one exact position, and that made the observation repeatable.

The finds on this hill are mostly from the Bronze Age, a period when people across Europe first worked bronze, an alloy of copper and tin. The main activity here is usually dated to around the nineteenth century before Christ, so roughly four thousand years ago. Excavations recovered large amounts of broken pottery, along with stone tools and fragments of moulds for casting metal. Many of the vessels seem to have been placed in cracks in the rock deliberately.

That detail is the second surprise. Kokino was probably not only a place for counting days. It also appears to have been a sacred mountain. Archaeologists interpret the vessels pressed into the rock as offerings, perhaps of grain or other food, left at important moments in the year. So science and ritual were not separate here. Watching the sun and honouring the powers that people believed controlled it were part of the same act.

Some researchers have also suggested that Kokino tracked the moon, whose rising points shift across the horizon in a longer and more complicated cycle. This interpretation is more debated than the solar alignments. It is fair to say that specialists still discuss how precise the site really was, and how much was deliberate design. That debate is part of the story. You are standing in a place where questions are still open.

Kokino has attracted wide attention since its discovery. It has appeared in international discussions of the world's ancient observatories, alongside far better known sites. North Macedonia has also put it forward for recognition by UNESCO, the United Nations cultural organisation. For a quiet hilltop above a farming village, that is a remarkable journey. Two decades ago, very few people outside the region knew this rock had any meaning at all.

Now take a moment simply to look. Notice how the rock outcrops form natural walls and platforms. Notice how open the view is to the east, with no nearby peak blocking the horizon. That openness was not an accident of choice. A sky observatory needs a long, uninterrupted skyline. The people who chose this summit understood their landscape very well, and they read it with care.

If you can, think about the effort involved. Every person who came here walked up this same slope, without paths or signs. They carried pots, food, and tools. They came back at the same seasons, year after year, perhaps across many generations. Knowledge about where the sun would rise was likely passed from older people to younger ones by memory and by practice. The hill itself became a kind of shared memory.

Before you leave, turn once more towards the east and find the line where the hills meet the sky. Somewhere along that line, on a winter morning or a summer morning, the sun appears in a place that people here once marked in stone. The wind is still moving through the grass. The lichen is still slowly spreading across the rock. As you walk back down, you are leaving one of the oldest calendars in this part of Europe — and it is still keeping time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, rocky volcanic ground.
Bring water and sun protection; the summit has little shade.
Come near a solstice or equinox for the sunrise alignments.
Arrive at dawn to watch the sun clear the eastern hills.
Check weather first; wind on the summit can be strong.
