The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. It comes across the open plain and pushes against you on this pale hill, carrying the smell of dry grass and warm stone. Under your feet, the path turns from soil to rough white rock. Ahead, a long line of broken walls stretches across the ridge, grey against the sky. This is Spiš Castle, and it is one of the largest castle complexes in Central Europe.

Levoča is the old town most visitors use as a base, a short drive to the west. From the road, the ruin looks almost unreal. The walls follow the edge of the hill like a crown that has slipped sideways. Below sits the small town of Spišské Podhradie, whose name simply means "under the Spiš castle." For centuries, the town lived in the shadow of the fortress above it.

Now look down at the ground. The hill itself is made of travertine, a light, porous limestone formed by mineral springs. This is the first surprise most visitors miss. The rock is the castle's foundation and also its main building material. The builders cut stone from the very hill they were fortifying. In places, it is hard to tell where the natural rock ends and the human wall begins.

The story of the castle begins in the twelfth century. At that time, this region belonged to the Kingdom of Hungary. The first fortress stood on the highest point of the hill. It had a Romanesque palace and a strong central tower. Romanesque is the heavy, round-arched building style of medieval Europe before the pointed Gothic arches arrived. Small pieces of that early work still survive in the upper castle.

In 1241, the Mongol armies swept into Central Europe. Many towns and fortresses in Hungary fell or were burned. Spiš Castle held. That survival taught a hard lesson across the kingdom. Stone castles on high ground could resist the invaders, while wooden forts could not. After the invasion, the castle was strengthened, and new walls began to spread down the slopes.

Those walls are what you see stretching across the hill today. The complex grew in stages, layer by layer, over several centuries. The upper castle sits at the top. Below it are the middle courtyard and the large lower courtyard, sometimes called the outer bailey. The lower courtyard is enormous. In times of danger, people from the surrounding villages could shelter inside, with their animals.

In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the castle belonged to the powerful Zápolya family. They rebuilt parts of it in the Gothic style and added comfort to the old fortress. One member of the family, John Zápolya, later became King of Hungary. Local tradition says he was born here at the castle, although historians still discuss the details. Either way, his family left a strong mark on these walls.

Later, the castle passed to other noble families, including the Thurzó and later the Csáky family. But fashions changed. Nobles wanted elegant manor houses in the valleys, not cold rooms on a windy hill. Then, in 1780, a fire swept through the castle. After that, the owners did not rebuild it. The great fortress slowly became a ruin, and the roofs disappeared.

Here is the second surprise. For a long time, local people treated the ruin as a quarry. Cut stone was valuable, and the walls were close by. Some of the castle's stone almost certainly ended up in houses and farm buildings in the valley. Serious conservation work began in the second half of the twentieth century. Restorers stabilised the walls and partly rebuilt some rooms, so visitors could walk here safely.

In 1993, the castle and its surroundings joined the UNESCO World Heritage List. Later, the listing was extended to include Levoča's old town. Look across the valley and you will see the reason. On the opposite hill stands Spišská Kapitula, a walled church town with a cathedral. Together, the castle, the church town and the merchant town of Levoča tell one story: power, faith and trade in one medieval region.

As you climb toward the upper castle, notice how the path narrows. Each gate forced attackers into tighter spaces. At the top, the round tower gives the widest view of the whole Spiš region. On a clear day, you can see the High Tatras mountains to the north. Inside the restored rooms, a museum shows weapons, everyday objects and life inside a medieval fortress. The castle has also appeared in films, including Dragonheart in the 1990s.

Take a moment to listen. Up here the wind is constant, and it moves through gaps in the walls with a low hum. Swallows and jackdaws circle the towers. Below you, there is the quiet sound of the town, a dog barking, a car on the road. It is easy to imagine the guards on these walls, watching the same roads for riders, hour after hour.

Before you leave, look once more at the colour of the stone. In the afternoon, the travertine turns from grey to a soft gold. Touch a wall if you can reach one safely. It feels rough and full of tiny holes, like a sponge turned to rock. As you walk back down the hill, turn around now and then. With every step, the castle grows larger against the sky... and you see again why people chose this hill, so many centuries ago.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a windproof layer, even on warm sunny days.
Wear sturdy shoes for the rocky, uneven paths.
Climb the upper tower for views toward the Tatras.
Visit late afternoon when the stone turns golden.
Combine your visit with Spišská Kapitula across the valley.
