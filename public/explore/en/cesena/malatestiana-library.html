The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Outside, Cesena carries on with scooters, voices, and café cups set down on saucers. In here the sound drops away. The air is cool and dry, with a faint smell of old wood and stone. Ahead of you, two rows of pale columns run into soft light, like trees along an avenue. You are standing at the door of a reading room that has kept its shape for more than five and a half centuries.

This is the Malatestiana Library. It opened in 1454, and it is often described as the first civic library in Europe. That word, civic, is the key to everything here. Monasteries and princes had libraries long before this one. But this collection did not belong to a monastery or a single family. Its founder left it in the care of the town itself, the commune of Cesena, so that it would serve the community long after him.

The founder was Domenico Malatesta, known as Malatesta Novello, lord of Cesena in the fifteenth century. The Malatesta family ruled several towns in this part of Romagna, the region between the Apennine hills and the Adriatic coast. His brother, Sigismondo, ruled nearby Rimini and became famous for his ambition and his quarrels. Novello was quieter. He was in poor health for much of his life, and he chose to put his money and energy into books, learning, and a building to hold them.

The library sits inside the old Franciscan convent of the city. Novello worked with the friars, and the room was built above the cloister, on the upper floor. The architect was Matteo Nuti, from the coastal town of Fano. Look at how the space is organised. There are three aisles. The two side aisles hold the reading benches. The central aisle stays open, a corridor of light and air that leads your eye straight to the far wall.

Now look at the columns. They are made of white stone, slender and calm, set in two long rows. Their capitals, the carved tops of the columns, carry the symbols of the Malatesta family. Above the central aisle, a barrel vault curves overhead like the inside of a long tunnel. Over the side aisles, the ceiling breaks into smaller cross vaults. Many visitors notice that the plan looks like a church. That is no accident. Renaissance builders often gave knowledge the same dignity as worship.

Here is the first surprise. The benches in front of you are the original fifteenth century reading desks. They are called plutei. Each one is a sloping desk with a seat attached, and the books lie on them, not standing on shelves. Many of the manuscripts were fixed in place with iron chains. The chains were not a sign of distrust towards readers. Handwritten books were extremely valuable, and a chain allowed anyone to read a volume while making sure it stayed in the room.

The second surprise is even quieter. This library has survived almost untouched. The room, the furniture, and a large part of the original collection are still together, in the place they were made for. Wars, floods, changing tastes, and new rulers came and went across Italy. Libraries elsewhere were emptied, sold, or rebuilt. Here, the books stayed with their benches. In 2005, UNESCO added the library to its Memory of the World register, a list that protects documents of global importance.

Notice the light. Windows run along both side walls, and they are placed to fall across the reading desks. Before electric lighting, this mattered more than any decoration. A reader in the fifteenth century needed daylight to see small handwriting on parchment. So the room was designed around the eye. Morning and afternoon light enter from different sides, and the pale columns spread that light gently across the room.

Search for the elephant. It is the emblem of the Malatesta family, and it appears in this building. With it comes a Latin motto. In English it means roughly, the Indian elephant does not fear mosquitoes. The idea is simple. A great and strong creature does not worry about small, buzzing enemies. It is a proud message, and perhaps a slightly humorous one, carved into a place of quiet study.

What did Novello collect? The core of the library is a set of manuscripts, handwritten books made before printing spread across Europe. They include religious texts, classical Latin and Greek authors, works of medicine, philosophy, and science. Novello did not only buy books. He paid scribes and illuminators to copy and decorate new ones. Illuminators were the artists who painted coloured initials and borders on the pages, sometimes with gold. Some of this work was done here in Cesena.

Think about what this meant in its own time. In the middle of the fifteenth century, the printing press was only beginning in Germany. Books were rare, slow to make, and expensive. A library open to scholars of the town, and not only to a lord and his friends, was a bold idea. It fits the spirit of the Italian Renaissance, the movement that brought new interest in ancient learning and in human ability. Here, that spirit took the form of furniture, light, and a key held by the town.

Today the Malatestiana is more than this single room. The wider library complex also holds later collections and a modern public library used by students and residents every day. So the original idea is still working. People still come to this building to read and study. The historic reading room is protected, and visits are usually guided in small groups. You may view it from near the entrance, rather than walking among the benches. That distance is part of how it has lasted so long.

Before you leave, take one more slow look down the central aisle. Let your eyes travel along the columns, across the rows of wooden desks, to the books resting where they have rested for generations. Then step back into the cloister and the streets of Cesena. As the sounds of the town return, remember that the quiet behind you was built on purpose. It was a gift from one lord to his city, and the city has kept it.
