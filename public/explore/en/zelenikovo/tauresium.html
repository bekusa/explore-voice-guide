The guide

The story

Listen first. On this hillside the wind carries the dry rattle of grass and, now and then, the bark of a dog from the village below. The ground underfoot is uneven. Loose stones shift under your shoes, and pale chunks of old masonry break through the soil. It is a quiet place. Few people come here by accident. Yet this modest ridge is linked to the birth of one of the most powerful rulers the Roman world ever produced.

You are standing at Tauresium, an archaeological site near the village of Taor, in the Zelenikovo Municipality of North Macedonia. The capital, Skopje, lies a short drive to the northwest. The Vardar, the great river of this country, runs through the valley nearby. Long before modern borders existed, this land belonged to the Roman Empire. Later it belonged to its eastern half, the empire historians now call Byzantine, which was ruled from Constantinople.

The name Tauresium comes to us from a sixth century writer called Procopius. He served the imperial court and wrote a book about the emperor's building projects. In it he says that the Emperor Justinian was born in a small place called Taurisium. He also says that Justinian later honoured his birthplace. According to Procopius, the emperor built a wall with four towers around the village, and founded a new city close by.

So who was Justinian? He ruled the eastern Roman Empire from 527 until his death in 565. That is close to four decades on the throne. His reign rebuilt the great church of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople, the domed building that still stands in Istanbul today. His lawyers gathered centuries of Roman law into one organised collection. That legal work later shaped the law codes of much of Europe. Many legal systems in use today still trace part of their structure back to it.

Here is the first surprise. Justinian was not born into the imperial family. He came from a rural, Latin-speaking family in these Balkan hills. His rise began with his uncle, Justin. Justin left this region as a young man, travelled to Constantinople, and joined the army. He climbed through the ranks of the palace guard. In 518, to the surprise of many, he became emperor himself. He brought his talented nephew to the capital, educated him, and prepared him to rule.

Now, an honest word about this place. Historians agree that Procopius named a village called Taurisium as Justinian's birthplace. But scholars still debate exactly where that village stood. Several sites across the central Balkans have been proposed. The link with Taor rests partly on the similarity of the names, and partly on the Roman remains found on this hillside. Many local historians accept the identification. Others remain cautious. You are visiting a strong candidate, not a settled certainty.

The new city that Procopius described, Justiniana Prima, is a separate question. Many archaeologists now connect it with a ruined city called Caričin Grad, in southern Serbia. That site lies some distance to the north. If that identification is right, Justinian's birthplace and his showcase city were not neighbours in the way the old text suggests. The debate continues. And that uncertainty is part of what makes standing here interesting. History, in this valley, is still being argued over.

Look around at the remains themselves. Excavations here uncovered stretches of fortification wall, built from rough stone set in mortar. Some sections have been partly restored, so you may notice a difference between older, weathered stone and cleaner reconstructed courses. The walls follow the shape of the ridge. This was typical for late Roman builders. They used the natural slope as part of the defence, so attackers had to climb before they could even reach the stone.

Why build walls at all? In Justinian's time, the Balkan provinces faced repeated raids from groups moving across the Danube frontier to the north. Small rural communities were exposed. Across the region, emperors paid for walls, towers and hilltop refuges where villagers could shelter. A fortified village like the one Procopius describes was a practical defence, but also a statement. The emperor was telling his home region that it had not been forgotten.

Here is the second surprise. Justinian probably spoke Latin as his first language, not Greek. In his day, the Balkans were divided by language. The south looked to Greek, while much of the interior, including this area, used Latin. Justinian was one of the last emperors in Constantinople for whom Latin was the language of childhood. After him, the eastern empire became more and more Greek in its speech and in its government.

For people in North Macedonia, Justinian's possible birthplace carries real meaning. You may see his name used across the country, on streets and in the names of schools and institutions. The village of Taor itself is small and agricultural. Fields, orchards and low houses sit on the slopes below the site. Life here moves at the pace of the seasons. It is easy to imagine that a boy growing up here, centuries ago, saw a similar rhythm of planting and harvest.

Take a moment to look out across the valley. The Vardar corridor has always been a route of movement. Armies, traders and travellers have passed along it for thousands of years, linking the Aegean coast with the lands to the north. Today a motorway and a railway follow much of the same path. From this height you can understand why a settlement here made sense. It watched the road, and the road brought the wider world to its door.

The experience here is simple, and that is its value. There is no grand museum, and no crowd. Instead, there is the wall line, the open sky, and the chance to think about how far a single life can travel. A boy from a farming family on a Balkan hillside may have grown up to rule from the shores of the Bosphorus. He would rebuild its greatest church and reshape the law of half a continent.

Before you leave, rest your hand on the stone for a moment. Feel how rough it is, and how warm or cold the sun has left it today. Listen again to the wind in the grass and the faint sounds from the village. Whether or not Justinian took his first steps on this exact ridge, people lived, worked and sheltered here in his age. As you walk back down the slope, notice the valley opening in front of you, the same view they saw.
