The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the great square and listen for a moment. Horse-drawn carriages clatter over the paving. Water moves in the long central pool. Somewhere a shopkeeper calls out from the arcades. Now turn toward the west side of the square and look up. A tall, narrow building rises above the arcade line, topped by a broad wooden veranda on slender columns. This is Ali Qapu, and for a royal dynasty it was the place where the king looked out at his city.

The square in front of you is known today as Naqsh-e Jahan, and also as Imam Square. Its name is often translated as the image of the world. It is one of the largest city squares on Earth, and UNESCO listed it as a World Heritage Site in 1979. Ali Qapu is one of four great buildings set into its edges. Each one faces another across the open space, and together they form a single plan.

The builder behind this plan was Shah Abbas the First, the most celebrated ruler of the Safavid dynasty. The Safavids ruled Iran from the early sixteenth century, and they made Shia Islam the faith of the state. Shah Abbas moved his capital to Isfahan in 1598. He did not simply settle into an old city. He set out to build a new royal centre, and this square, with its palace gate, was the heart of that project.

The name Ali Qapu is usually understood as the lofty gate or the exalted gate. The first word comes from Arabic and means high or noble. The second comes from Turkic and means gate. That mix of languages is a small clue to the Safavid court itself, where Persian culture, Turkic military traditions and Arabic religious learning all lived side by side. The name also tells you what the building was for. It was an entrance.

Behind Ali Qapu lay the royal precinct, a large area of gardens, pavilions and private palaces. Ordinary people did not walk through it freely. This tower worked as the formal threshold between the public square and the private world of the king. Ambassadors, officials and guests passed through here. One of the palaces from that precinct, Chehel Sotoun, still stands a short walk to the west, and it helps you imagine how much of this garden city once spread behind the gate.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Ali Qapu did not appear in its final form all at once. Historians believe it began as a more modest gatehouse and grew in stages. Floors were added upward, and the great veranda was built out at the front. What you see today is the result of several building phases under the Safavids. That is why the structure looks a little unusual — tall and stacked, rather than wide and symmetrical.

Now look again at that veranda, called a talar in Persian. A talar is a raised, open hall with a flat roof held up by columns. Here the columns are tall, thin and made of wood. Above them, the ceiling is covered in panels of inlaid and painted woodwork. From this platform the shah could sit in the shade, high above the crowd, and watch everything happening below. It was a royal box seat, built for an entire city.

And what did he watch? The square was not only a market and a meeting place. It was also a field for polo, a sport with deep roots in Persian court life. Look toward the north and south ends of the square. You can still find old stone goalposts standing there. On festival days the shah also watched parades, military displays and receptions for foreign envoys. The talar turned public spectacle into a stage for royal power.

Across the square, directly opposite Ali Qapu, stands the Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, with its pale, cream-coloured dome. It is a small mosque with no courtyard and no minarets. It is often described as a private place of prayer for the royal household. The two buildings face each other across the open ground — the palace gate on one side, the mosque on the other. To the south rises the larger Shah Mosque, now called the Imam Mosque, and to the north stands the gateway to the old bazaar.

Inside Ali Qapu, the experience changes completely. The stairways are narrow and steep, and they twist upward through the building. Rooms open off them on different floors. Many walls and ceilings carry paintings of flowers, birds, animals and graceful human figures. These works come from the circle of Safavid court painters. Some are traditionally linked to the school of Reza Abbasi, a leading artist of the period, though not every panel can be firmly attributed.

The most surprising space waits near the top. It is usually called the music room. Here the walls and ceiling are covered with plaster cut into hundreds of shapes — the outlines of jugs, vases, bottles and cups. Each shape is a hollow niche set into the stucco. It is often said that these openings shaped the sound for court musicians, softening echoes in the room. Whether that was the main purpose or not, the effect on the eye is remarkable. It looks like lace made of plaster.

The building has also lived through hard centuries. After the Safavid dynasty fell in the eighteenth century, Isfahan lost its role as capital. Many royal structures decayed or disappeared. Ali Qapu survived, though its paintings and woodwork suffered damage over time. In the twentieth century, Iranian and international specialists carried out long programmes of restoration. As you climb, you may notice areas where old paint is faded and areas where conservators have carefully stabilised what remains.

If you reach the talar, take your time. From up here the scale of the square becomes clear. The pool, the gardens, the long two-storey arcades and the domes all line up in front of you. You are looking at the city from the same height, and roughly the same angle, that a Safavid king once chose for himself. The noise of the square softens a little at this height, and the view does most of the talking.

When you come back down to the paving, pause one more time. Look up at the wooden columns against the sky and imagine the shah's shaded seat above the crowd. Then look across at the mosque dome, and along the arcades where shops still sell copperware, carpets and painted tiles. Ali Qapu was built to be seen as much as to see from. As you walk away, turn back once. The gate is still watching the square.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb slowly; the interior stairways are narrow and steep.
Visit the talar late afternoon for soft light over the square.
Pair the visit with Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque directly opposite.
Walk to Chehel Sotoun to see another royal palace nearby.
Wear comfortable shoes for uneven steps and many floors.
