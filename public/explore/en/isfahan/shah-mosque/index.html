The guide

The story

Stand for a moment at the edge of the great square and look south. The tiles ahead are blue. Not one blue, but dozens of them: deep cobalt, pale turquoise, a soft blue that is almost grey in the shade. As the light shifts, the colours change with it. Pigeons cross the open sky. Somewhere behind you, a horse carriage clicks over the paving. In front of you rises a tall gateway, covered from top to bottom in glazed tile.

This is the Shah Mosque, known today in Iran as the Imam Mosque. It stands on the southern side of Naqsh-e Jahan Square, one of the largest city squares in the world. The square and the buildings around it were part of a single royal vision. Together they are listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. To understand the mosque, you first need to understand the man who wanted it built.

In the late sixteenth century, Shah Abbas the First moved the capital of the Safavid empire to Isfahan. The Safavids were the dynasty that made Shia Islam the state religion of Persia. Shah Abbas wanted his new capital to show power, faith, and wealth in one place. Around this vast square he placed a palace, a bazaar, a smaller private mosque, and finally this great congregational mosque. Work on it began in 1611, and the main building was finished in the years around his death.

Now look closely at the entrance portal in front of you. It is tall and deep, and its upper part is filled with muqarnas. These are small, stacked niches that look a little like honeycomb or hanging stalactites. They catch light and shadow in layers. Across the portal runs a long band of white calligraphy on a blue ground. This inscription is attributed to Ali Reza Abbasi, one of the most respected calligraphers of the Safavid court.

Here is the first surprise. The portal faces the square, which points roughly north. But a mosque must face Mecca, which from Isfahan lies to the southwest. The builders solved this with a quiet trick. When you walk through the gateway, the passage turns. You will hardly feel it. Yet by the time you reach the courtyard, the whole building has rotated by about forty-five degrees. Look back at the square from inside, and you will see the angle clearly.

The courtyard opens up in front of you. It is calm after the open square. In the centre lies a wide rectangular pool, its water reflecting the tiled walls. On each of the four sides stands a great arched hall called an iwan. This four-iwan plan is a classic Persian design. The largest iwan, on the side facing Mecca, leads to the main prayer hall under the great dome. Take a moment here. Notice how quiet it becomes, and how the walls frame the sky.

Look at the tiles again, more slowly this time. There are two main kinds in this mosque. The first is mosaic tile, where small pieces of coloured glazed tile are cut and fitted together by hand, like a jigsaw. This is slow and expensive work, and you can see it on the main portal. The second kind is called haft rangi, which means seven colours. Here, the full pattern is painted onto square tiles and fired at once.

Why use two methods? The answer is time. Painted seven-colour tiles were much faster to make and to fit. They allowed the builders to cover enormous surfaces in a few years rather than many decades. Stories often describe Shah Abbas as impatient to see his mosque complete. Some historians think this is why so much of the building uses the faster technique. Look closely and you may see that the painted tiles look a little flatter, while the mosaic sections shine with more depth.

The name most often linked to the design is Ali Akbar Isfahani, the master builder of the project. There is also a popular legend about him. According to one tale, he disappeared for years after laying the foundations, and the Shah was furious. When he returned, he explained that the foundations needed time to settle before the heavy dome could rise. It is a good story, but treat it as folklore, not documented history. Many great buildings across the region carry similar tales.

Now walk into the main prayer hall, under the great dome. This is where the second surprise waits. Stand on the dark stone marked in the floor near the centre, and clap your hands or speak a single word. Listen. The sound comes back to you, again and again. Locals often say the echo repeats seven times. Whatever number you count, the effect is real. Yet a person standing a few steps away hears very little of it.

This effect comes partly from the shape of the dome. It is built with a double shell. That means there is an inner dome you see from below, and a taller outer dome you see from the square. A gap separates the two. This design let the builders give the dome a high outline on the skyline while keeping a balanced space inside. From the courtyard, look up at the outer dome. Its turquoise surface carries curling patterns of vines and flowers in yellow and white.

On either side of the main hall are smaller courtyards. These were once religious schools, called madrasas, where students studied and lived. They are quieter than the main courtyard. Trees grow there, and the light falls softer through the leaves. Visitors often miss them, but they show another side of the mosque. This was not only a place for grand prayer. It was also a place of daily study, conversation, and learning.

Before you leave, walk back toward the gateway and turn around one last time. Notice how the dome and the two slender minarets line up above the portal from the square, even though the building behind them is turned. Listen to the water in the pool, the soft steps on the stone, the voices that echo gently under the arches. As you step out into the square, look at how the blue of the tiles changes again in the open light.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in early morning for softer light on the tiles.
Women should bring a headscarf to cover their hair.
Speak softly under the dome to test the echo.
Look back at the square from inside the courtyard.
Visit the side madrasa courtyards for quieter moments.
