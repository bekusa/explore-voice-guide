The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere in the plane trees above you, birds are calling, and below them water moves softly through a stone channel. The light here falls in patches, green and gold, across a path of packed earth. Ahead stands a low pavilion with tall, slender wooden columns. It looks light and open, as if a breeze could pass straight through it. That feeling is intentional. You are standing in the Garden of the Nightingales, in front of Hasht Behesht.

The name means Eight Paradises. In Persian tradition, the number eight connects to the idea of the eight gardens of heaven. The name also describes the building itself. The pavilion follows a plan with rooms grouped around a central domed hall, arranged in a pattern of eight. Persian architects used this layout for garden palaces, and the plan itself is often called a hasht behesht. So this building carries the name of a whole architectural type.

The pavilion dates to 1669, during the reign of Shah Suleiman, a ruler of the Safavid dynasty. The Safavids governed Persia from the early sixteenth century to the early eighteenth century. Under them, Isfahan became the capital and grew into one of the great cities of the Islamic world. Hasht Behesht came late in that story. It was built roughly seventy years after Shah Abbas the First moved his court here and redesigned the city.

Think about what this place was for. It was not a fortress or an office of government. It was a pleasure pavilion, a private residence set inside a garden. The court came here to rest, to receive guests in comfort, and to enjoy the shade and the water. Many accounts describe it as a place for the ruler's household. Imagine silk cushions, music, and the sound of fountains... a world built for leisure.

Now look at the building as a whole. It is octagonal, with its corners cut at angles. It rises two storeys, and the facades open outward in deep porches. Persians call this kind of columned porch a talar. The tall wooden columns hold up a flat, decorated ceiling. Notice how the porch frames the garden. From inside, every opening becomes a picture of trees and sky. The building was designed to look outward as much as to be looked at.

Step closer to the walls and study the tiles. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. These panels are not only geometric patterns. They show animals and birds — foxes, deer, peacocks, and many others — along with figures and hunting scenes. Some tiles show mythical creatures. This type of figurative tilework is less common on religious buildings, which tend to favour calligraphy and pattern. On a palace, the artists had more freedom to show the living world.

Take your time with the colours. The tiles mix deep blue, turquoise, yellow, and white, with touches of green and other tones. Some panels are bright and well preserved. Others are faded or replaced. Look carefully and you may see differences in style between sections. That variation comes from the building's long life. Over the centuries, many hands repaired, altered, and restored the decoration, and the walls carry traces of each period.

Now walk inside, beneath the central dome. This is the heart of the pavilion. Look up. The ceiling is covered in muqarnas, a form of Islamic decoration made of many small, stacked niches. From below, it looks like a honeycomb or a cascade of carved cells. Here you may also catch flashes of mirror work catching the light. Stand still for a moment, and turn slowly. The space changes as the light shifts through the openings.

Here is the second surprise. The building you see today is not exactly the Safavid original. In the nineteenth century, the Qajar dynasty ruled Persia. Qajar rulers and governors altered Hasht Behesht, adding painted decoration and changing some interiors. Some earlier work was covered or replaced. Later restorations in the twentieth century tried to recover and protect what remained. So when you look around, you are seeing several centuries layered together on one structure.

The pavilion also once had a fountain and water features at its centre. Water was essential to the Persian garden. It cooled the air in the dry summer heat, and its sound created a sense of calm. The Persian garden, often divided by water channels into ordered sections, became a symbol of paradise on earth. This idea is old, and the word paradise itself comes from an ancient Persian term for an enclosed garden.

Look back toward the garden itself. Today it is a public park, and you will often see families walking, children playing, and older residents sitting on benches in the shade. This is part of the experience. Hasht Behesht is not separated from daily life in Isfahan. It sits close to Chahar Bagh, the long avenue that once formed the grand promenade of the Safavid city. Locals still use this garden as a place to breathe, just as the court once did.

European travellers who visited Isfahan in past centuries often wrote about the city's palaces and gardens with great admiration. Many of the Safavid pavilions they described have since disappeared. That makes Hasht Behesht precious. It is one of only a few royal palace buildings from this period that still stand in Isfahan. When you walk around it, you are seeing a rare survivor of the court culture that once filled this city with gardens.

Before you leave, walk once more around the outside of the pavilion. Notice how the view changes on each side, and how each porch frames a different part of the garden. Listen again to the birds in the trees, the voices of the people around you, the scrape of shoes on the path. The court is long gone. But the shade, the water, and the quiet pleasure of this place are still here — and for a moment, they are yours.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk a full circle around the pavilion before entering.
Visit in the morning for softer light and cooler air.
Combine your visit with a stroll along Chahar Bagh.
Bring water, especially during Isfahan's hot summer months.
Sit on a garden bench to watch local daily life.
