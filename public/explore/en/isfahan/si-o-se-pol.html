The guide

The story

Put your hand on the brick beside you. It is warm, a little rough, and the colour of dry sand. Look along the bridge and the arches repeat, one after another, like a long breath held in stone. Somewhere under your feet, people are talking. Somewhere further along, someone may be singing. This is Si-o-se-pol, the long bridge of Isfahan, and it was built as a place to walk slowly.

The name is simple once you hear it. In Persian, si-o-se means thirty-three, and pol means bridge. So this is the Bridge of Thirty-Three, named for the arches that carry it across the river. Count them if you like. Many visitors try, and many lose track halfway. Officially, though, the bridge carries another name — the Allahverdi Khan Bridge, after the man who oversaw its construction.

That man has a surprising story. Allahverdi Khan was a commander in the service of Shah Abbas the First, the ruler who made Isfahan his capital in the late sixteenth century. Allahverdi Khan was of Georgian origin. He was brought into Persian service from the Caucasus, rose through the court, and became one of the most powerful generals of his age. So a Georgian-born general gave his name to one of the best-loved structures in Iran.

The bridge belongs to the Safavid era. The Safavids were the dynasty that ruled Persia from the early sixteenth century, and they turned Isfahan into a planned imperial city. Shah Abbas laid out a long, tree-lined avenue called Chahar Bagh, which means four gardens. Si-o-se-pol was built around the turn of the seventeenth century to carry that avenue south across the river. The bridge was not only a crossing. It was part of a grand city design.

Now look at the shape of the structure. It has two levels. The upper level is the main walkway, with a wide path in the centre and covered arcades along both sides. Below, at the level of the water, there is a second, lower set of passages and openings. The design lets people move across the river, but it also invites them to stop. You can sit in an archway, out of the sun, and watch the city pass.

The river below is the Zayandeh Rud. Its name is often translated as the life-giving river. For centuries it fed the gardens and fields of Isfahan. Here is something many visitors do not expect. In recent years the riverbed has often been dry for long periods, because of drought and heavy upstream water use. You may see water today. You may see only sand and stones. Both are part of the bridge's present story.

When the river is dry, people walk right under the arches on the riverbed. It is an odd and quiet experience. You look up at the bridge from below, and the stone piers rise around you like the legs of a great table. When the water returns, usually for short periods, the mood changes completely. Families come down to the banks, children dip their feet, and the sound of moving water fills the lower passages again.

Listen carefully under the arches, especially in the evening. The curved brick ceilings carry sound in a particular way. For many years, men have gathered in these lower archways to sing. Often it is old Persian poetry set to traditional melodies, sometimes a single voice, sometimes a small group. Nobody organises it. People simply arrive, someone begins, and others listen. It is one of the most human things you can witness in Isfahan.

The materials here are modest — brick and stone. The Safavid builders used them with great care. Notice how the arches are not all the same scale. The rhythm of large and small openings keeps the eye moving along the bridge. Notice too the thickness of the piers below. They had to stand against floods when the river ran high in spring. The bridge has carried people for more than four centuries, so that care clearly worked.

Light changes everything on this bridge. In the middle of the day, the sun bleaches the brick to a pale gold, and the arcades fall into deep shade. In the late afternoon, the colour warms toward honey and copper. After dark, lamps light the arches from within, and the whole bridge glows against the night sky. If the river holds water, the reflection doubles every arch. That is the image of Isfahan many Iranians carry in their minds.

Si-o-se-pol is not the only historic bridge on this river. Further downstream stands the Khaju Bridge, built later in the Safavid period. It is shorter, more decorated, and has its own pavilions. Some people prefer it. Others say Si-o-se-pol is the purer design, long and calm and repeated. If you have time, walk along the riverbank between them. The path is shaded by trees and is a favourite place for locals.

This bridge is also a meeting place, in the most ordinary sense. For generations, people in Isfahan have said, meet me at Si-o-se-pol. Students come here after class. Couples walk slowly along the arcades. Older men sit on the low ledges and talk for hours. Tea and snacks are often sold near the ends of the bridge. You are standing in a living space, not a museum gallery.

As you stand here, think about all the feet that have crossed these bricks. Merchants with goods for the bazaar. Soldiers of the shah. Pilgrims, poets, traders from distant lands. The stone under you is worn smooth in places, polished by centuries of walking. The bridge has seen floods, empty riverbeds, empires rising and falling. And still, each evening, people come back to sit in its arches.

Before you leave, choose one archway and stay a moment. Look out through it, at the river or the riverbed, at the trees and the domes beyond. Listen for voices, for footsteps, maybe for a song. When you walk away, turn back once. The arches will be lining up behind you — thirty-three of them, give or take the ones you lost count of — patient and warm in the light.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come at sunset to see the brick turn warm gold.
Return after dark when lamps light the arches.
Visit the lower arches in the evening for singing.
Walk the riverbank downstream to reach Khaju Bridge.
Carry water and a hat for the midday sun.
