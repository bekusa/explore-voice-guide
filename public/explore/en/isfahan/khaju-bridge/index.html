The guide

The story

Run your hand along the stone steps at the water's edge. They are smooth, worn into gentle hollows by centuries of people sitting exactly where you are. Above you, arches open in a long row, each one framing a slice of sky. On a quiet evening the air under these vaults carries a low echo. Footsteps, a laugh, a snatch of song all return to you a moment later.

This is Khaju Bridge, which crosses the Zayandeh Rud, the river that runs through the heart of Isfahan. The bridge dates from the middle of the seventeenth century. It was built during the reign of Shah Abbas the Second, a ruler of the Safavid dynasty. The Safavids were the Persian royal house that made Isfahan their capital and filled it with mosques, palaces and gardens. This bridge is one of their most graceful works.

At first glance it looks like a simple crossing. Look longer, and you will see that it does several jobs at once. It is a road, yes. But it is also a dam, a public promenade, and a royal viewing platform. The Safavid builders did not see these as separate tasks. They folded them all into one long structure of brick and stone.

Start with the lower level, the part closest to the river. Here the bridge rests on a series of broad stone platforms, cut into steps that descend toward the riverbed. Between them run narrow channels. In the past, gates in these channels could be opened or closed to control the flow of water. When they closed, the river rose behind the bridge and formed a wide, still pool upstream. In effect, the bridge worked as a weir — a low dam that managed the water.

That engineering detail is the first thing most visitors miss. People come for the arches and the tiles. They rarely realise that the steps under their feet were part of a water system. The raised level upstream helped supply the gardens and fields that depended on this river. In a dry land like central Iran, water was wealth, and this bridge helped to manage it.

Now climb to the upper level. The central lane once carried horses, carts and travellers between the two banks of the city. On either side, covered walkways run beneath their own arcades. These sheltered passages let people walk in shade, away from the traffic in the middle. Stand in one of them and look along its length. The repeated arches pull your eye toward a vanishing point, and the effect is almost hypnotic.

In the middle of the bridge, the structure widens into a pavilion. This was a space reserved for the shah and his court. From here the ruler could look out over the river, the pool, and the festivities that were sometimes held on the water. Look up at the walls and ceilings. You will find traces of painted decoration, and in places the colours have faded into soft ochres and blues.

The outer faces of the bridge carry glazed tilework, set into the brick in geometric and floral patterns. Turquoise, yellow and deep blue appear against the warm tan of the masonry. This contrast is typical of Safavid Isfahan. The same palette covers the great domes elsewhere in the city. Here it appears on a smaller, more human scale, close enough to study piece by piece.

Before you leave, walk to the ends of the bridge. Look for the stone lions that sit near the approaches. They are weathered now, their features softened by time and countless curious hands. Children often climb on them, and many visitors pause for a photograph. They are a small, quiet detail, and easy to walk past without noticing.

Here is the second surprise. The vaulted arches under the bridge do something remarkable with sound. In the evenings, local men often gather in the passages beneath the arches and sing. Sometimes the songs are traditional Persian melodies, sometimes poetry set to a tune. The curved brick carries their voices outward, so a single singer can fill the whole space. If you are lucky, you will hear it — an informal concert that nobody planned and nobody sells tickets for.

The river itself has changed in recent decades. Upstream diversion and long periods of drought have left the Zayandeh Rud dry for much of the year. Depending on when you visit, you may see water rushing through the channels, or you may see a wide bed of sand and pebbles. Both views tell you something true about this city and its long relationship with water.

When the river is dry, people walk across the riverbed itself and look up at the bridge from below. From down there, the full height of the platforms becomes clear, along with the size of the stones. When the river runs, families picnic on the steps and dip their feet in the current. Either way, this is a living public space. Students, couples, grandparents and street vendors all share it.

Isfahan has several historic bridges across this river. Upstream stands Si-o-se-pol, the long bridge of thirty-three arches, which is older and plainer in form. Many visitors walk the riverside path between the two. The comparison is worth making. Khaju is shorter, but it is richer in decoration and more complex in its design.

So take a moment before you go. Sit on one of the worn stone steps. Listen for the echo under the arches, and watch the light shift across the tiles as the sun drops lower. People have sat here in the evening for nearly four hundred years, doing much the same thing. As you walk away along the riverbank, turn back once. Notice how the arches glow against the darkening sky.

Practical tips
