The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment in the courtyard. The brick under your feet is the colour of dry earth. The walls around you are plain, almost severe, and the air is quiet after the traffic of the streets outside. Then look toward the low wooden doorway of the church. Behind it, you will soon see every surface covered in gold, deep blue, and painted figures. This contrast, plain outside and full of colour inside, is the first thing to understand about Vank Cathedral.

You are in New Julfa, the Armenian quarter of Isfahan, on the south bank of the Zayandeh River. The cathedral is also known as the Holy Saviour Cathedral. The word Vank simply means monastery or convent in Armenian. For around four centuries, this courtyard has been the centre of Armenian church life in the city. Many Armenian families in Iran still look to this church as their spiritual home.

The neighbourhood exists because of a forced journey. In the early 1600s, the Safavid ruler Shah Abbas the First moved thousands of Armenians from the town of Julfa, on the Aras River in the Caucasus, deep into Persia. His empire was at war with the Ottomans, and the border region was being emptied. The Shah also wanted something specific. He wanted the skills and trade networks of Julfa's merchants, who were famous for the silk trade.

So the Armenians were settled here, close to his new capital, and the district was named New Julfa after the home they had lost. The Shah gave them a degree of protection and religious freedom. Several churches rose in the quarter during the seventeenth century. This cathedral, completed around the middle of that century, became the most important of them. Its story is a story of loss, survival, and a community that built something beautiful in exile.

Now look up at the dome from the courtyard. If you did not know this was a church, you might guess it was a small mosque. The rounded dome, the brick, and the simple, solid shape all follow Persian building traditions. The builders worked with local methods and local materials. A cross on top is one of the few outside signs of what this building is. That blending of two worlds is the second thing to notice here.

When you step inside, give your eyes a moment to adjust. The walls and ceiling are covered with painted scenes, many framed with gold. Low on the walls, you will see bands of glazed tilework in the Persian style, with flowers and patterns. Above the tiles, the paintings begin. The style mixes Persian decoration with figures that look European. Historians connect this to the European prints and books that Armenian merchants brought back from their travels.

Take some time with the painted stories. Scenes from the Book of Genesis run along the walls, including the creation of the world and Adam and Eve in the garden. There are scenes from the life of Christ. Look especially at the large painting of the Last Judgement. Heaven is shown above, and below it there is a vivid picture of the punishments of hell. Many visitors walk past it quickly, but the small details reward a closer look.

There is a harder series of paintings too. Some panels show the martyrdom of Saint Gregory the Illuminator, the figure Armenians honour for bringing Christianity to their land. Armenia is often described as the first kingdom to adopt Christianity as its state religion, in the early fourth century. These painted tortures can be uncomfortable to look at. For the people of New Julfa, though, they told a story of faith that survived suffering, a story that was close to their own experience.

Now raise your eyes to the inner dome. Its painted ceiling is filled with deep blue and gold, with patterns that some visitors find closer to a Persian palace than a European church. Stand under the centre and turn slowly. The light comes in from high windows, and the gold changes as you move. On a bright day, the colours seem warm. On a cloudy day, the blues become darker and calmer.

Back outside, walk across the courtyard to the bell tower. It stands apart from the main church, which is common in Armenian church design. Around the courtyard you will also find gravestones set into the ground, some carved with Armenian letters. Please step carefully. You will also see a memorial to the Armenian Genocide of 1915. Every year on the 24th of April, members of the community gather here to remember the victims.

The museum on the cathedral grounds is easy to miss, and many visitors only stop at the church. Inside, you can see illuminated Armenian manuscripts, religious objects, textiles, and royal decrees issued by Safavid rulers to the Armenian community. One small exhibit often surprises people. It is a single human hair with tiny writing inscribed on it. You need a magnifying lens to see the letters at all.

The museum also tells the story of printing in this courtyard. In the seventeenth century, the monks of Vank set up a printing press here, and it is often described as the first printing press in Iran. Some early printed books are kept in the collection. Think about this for a moment. A community that had been forcibly moved from its home became a place where books were printed. That says a lot about New Julfa.

Before you leave, stand once more in the courtyard and look back at the plain brick walls. It is easy to forget what is behind them. As you walk out into the streets of New Julfa, notice the cafes, the small shops, and the other church domes rising above the roofs. Listen for the mix of Persian and Armenian voices. The people who were brought here four centuries ago did not only survive. They stayed, and their quarter is still alive around you.
