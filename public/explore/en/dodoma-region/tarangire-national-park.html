The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves through the grass, and somewhere close a branch cracks under real weight. The air smells of dust and crushed leaves. Then you see them. Grey shapes stand in the shade of an enormous tree, ears moving slowly, trunks lifting sand onto their backs. This is Tarangire National Park. Here the elephants often notice you long before you notice them.

Tarangire lies in northern Tanzania, on the dry country south of the safari town of Arusha. It covers roughly 2,850 square kilometres of savanna, woodland and seasonal swamp. It became a national park in 1970. Many travellers treat it as a first stop on the northern safari circuit. That circuit also includes Lake Manyara, the Ngorongoro Crater and the Serengeti. People often come here for one afternoon and later wish they had stayed longer.

The park takes its name from the Tarangire River. Everything here depends on it. The river runs from south to north through the length of the park. In the wet months, water lies across the wider landscape and the animals spread out. When the rains stop, pools dry up and grass turns the colour of straw. The river then becomes one of the few reliable sources of water for a very large area.

This is the first surprise for many visitors. Tarangire changes completely with the seasons. In the dry months, roughly the middle of the year into the autumn, animals travel back toward the river from the surrounding plains. Zebra, wildebeest, buffalo and gazelle gather along its banks. Elephants arrive in large family groups. At this time of year, the park can hold some of the densest concentrations of wildlife in northern Tanzania.

The elephants are the main story here. Tarangire is known for its large herds, and researchers have followed families here for decades. Watch how a group moves. An older female usually leads, and the young calves walk close to their mothers' legs. In the dry season, elephants dig into the sandy riverbed with their feet and trunks. They reach water below the surface, and other animals later drink from the holes they leave.

Now look at the trees. The baobab is the other great character of Tarangire. These trees have thick, swollen trunks, smooth grey bark, and short branches that spread like roots in the air. Locals across East Africa sometimes call it the upside-down tree. One popular tale says the gods planted it the wrong way round as a punishment. That is folklore, of course. But when you look at a bare baobab against the sky, the story makes sense.

Baobabs can live for a very long time, and some of the largest trees here are likely many centuries old. Their trunks store water in the soft, fibrous wood. In hard dry seasons, elephants strip the bark and chew the wet wood inside. Look closely at a large trunk, and you may see deep grooves and scars. Remarkably, a baobab can often regrow its bark after this damage. Some trees carry the marks of many years of elephant visits.

Between the trees, you will notice tall mounds of red earth. Termites build these. Some rise higher than a person and have a hard, baked surface. Termites are rarely seen, but their mounds shape the landscape. Abandoned mounds become homes for other animals. Small dwarf mongooses often live in them. If you stop near one, watch the openings. A small brown head may appear, look around carefully, and then disappear again.

Tarangire is also a park for bird lovers. More than five hundred bird species have been recorded here. Listen for sharp, high calls in the acacia trees. Look for flashes of green from the yellow-collared lovebird, a small parrot found only in Tanzania. Large birds are easier to spot. Ostriches walk across open ground. Kori bustards, one of the heaviest flying birds in Africa, move slowly through the grass. Vultures and eagles circle overhead on the warm air.

The swamps add another layer. In the southern part of the park, wide wetlands stay green after the plains around them have turned brown. Buffalo stand deep in the grass there, and birds hunt along the edges. Predators follow the herds. Lions rest in the shade during the heat of the day. Leopards are harder to find, but they live here too. Patient guides scan the branches of large trees, where a leopard may lie with its tail hanging down.

As the wet season returns, the story reverses. Fresh rain brings new grass to the open plains outside the park. Many of the zebra and wildebeest leave Tarangire and spread across these wider lands. Local herding communities, including Maasai families, share much of this land with their cattle. The park therefore depends on the country around it. Wildlife corridors and grazing lands outside its borders matter as much as the land inside the park.

Take a moment to notice the human texture of the place too. Your guide reads the land in small signs. A fresh footprint in the sand. A fallen branch with new leaves still green. Alarm calls from a group of monkeys. Many guides on the northern circuit grew up nearby and learned these signs over years. Ask them what they see. Often the most interesting part of a game drive is the story behind a single track in the dust.

Before you move on, stay still for a minute. Turn off the engine if your guide allows it. Listen to the wind in the grass, the birds in the acacias, and perhaps the low rumble of an elephant nearby. Look at the shape of the baobabs against the light. As you leave, watch the riverbed and the shade under the trees. Tarangire often shows its best moments at the end of the day, quietly, on the road back.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in the dry season for the largest animal gatherings.
Plan a full day rather than a short afternoon.
Bring binoculars for birds and leopards in treetops.
Ask your guide to stop near the river at dusk.
Carry a scarf against dust on dry-season roads.
