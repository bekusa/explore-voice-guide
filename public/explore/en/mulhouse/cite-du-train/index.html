The guide

The story

Listen first. Inside this long hall, the air smells faintly of oil, steel and old varnish. Your footsteps echo off concrete, and somewhere a child is calling out the name of a locomotive. Around you stand machines that once weighed on the rails of France, now perfectly still. Black boilers, red buffer beams, green and blue carriages, polished brass. Welcome to the Cité du Train in Mulhouse, often described as the largest railway museum in Europe.

Before we walk the tracks, consider where you are. Mulhouse grew rich on industry. In the nineteenth century, its textile mills and engineering works earned it the nickname of the French Manchester. Factories needed coal, cotton and fast connections to markets. So railways arrived early in this corner of Alsace, close to the Swiss and German borders. A city built on machines is a fitting home for a museum of machines.

The museum began in the 1970s as the French Railway Museum. Its collection was gathered with the help of the national railway company, the SNCF. The building on this site in the Dornach district opened in the mid-1970s. In 2005, after a major expansion, it took the name you see today. The idea behind the name is simple. This is not only a museum. It is meant to feel like a small city of trains, with streets of rails to wander.

Start with the oldest engines, and look at their scale. The earliest steam locomotives here look almost delicate. Tall chimneys, large single driving wheels, open platforms where the driver stood with little protection from rain and smoke. One of the treasures is the Saint-Pierre, a locomotive from the 1840s that worked on one of the first main lines out of Paris. Stand beside it and imagine a crew standing in the open air, at speeds no human had travelled before.

Now walk forward through the decades. You will notice the machines grow heavier and lower. Boilers become fatter. Wheels multiply. Cabs close in around the crew. This is the story of steam in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Engineers wanted more power, more speed and more efficiency from every tonne of coal. Each locomotive here is a solution to that problem. Some are elegant. Some are simply massive.

Here is a detail many visitors walk past. Look into the cab of a steam locomotive, where the crew worked. There is no steering wheel. A train cannot steer. The driver controlled only the steam, the brakes and the whistle. The fireman fed coal into the firebox, shovel after shovel, for hours. It was hot, loud and exhausting work. Those small levers and gauges were the whole world of two people, mile after mile.

Part of the museum is laid out as a staged journey, with lighting, sound and scenery. Trains appear in settings that suggest their era and their purpose. Some scenes are cheerful, like holidays by the sea and the age of family travel. Others are serious. The railway also carried soldiers, and in the twentieth century it was used during the darkest chapters of European history. The museum does not hide that. Take a moment there, quietly.

Then comes luxury. Railways were not only for freight and commuters. They carried emperors, presidents and wealthy travellers. Among the carriages here you can find rolling stock linked to the imperial train of Napoleon the Third, the nephew of Napoleon Bonaparte, who ruled France in the mid-nineteenth century. Look at the upholstery, the wood panelling, the small decorative touches. These carriages were moving drawing rooms, designed to impress anyone who stepped inside.

Now for the second surprise. Find the electric locomotive numbered BB 9004. It looks fairly ordinary — a boxy machine in a practical livery. But in 1955, on a straight line in southwest France, this locomotive reached about 331 kilometres per hour. That was a world record for rail at the time. Engineers learned a lot from that test, including how fiercely the overhead wire and the rails suffered at such speeds. The path to today's high-speed trains runs partly through this unremarkable-looking machine.

Notice the shift from steam to electric and diesel as you move through the collection. The shapes change completely. Smoke and fire disappear. Smooth bodies and flat fronts take their place. The driver now sits in a closed cab with windows, looking ahead rather than leaning out. In a few metres of floor, you cross a revolution in how people worked and how countries moved. It is one of the quietest and clearest lessons in the building.

The museum also covers the smaller world of the railway. You may find signals, station equipment, uniforms, tools and models. These objects are easy to overlook beside the giant engines. But they show the human system around the trains. Signal operators, track workers, conductors and station staff kept the network safe. A railway was never just a locomotive. It was thousands of people working to a timetable, every day, in every season.

If you have time, step outside. Parts of the collection and some extra activities sit in the open air, depending on the season. From there you feel the industrial edge of Mulhouse more strongly. This is a working city, not a postcard town. And if your interest in machines is not satisfied, Mulhouse has another large museum devoted to automobiles. Many visitors combine the two, making a full day of wheels, engines and engineering.

Before you leave, choose one locomotive and stand in front of it for a minute. Look at the rivets, the patched paint, the worn steps where boots climbed up thousands of times. Each of these machines once had a crew, a route, a home depot and a final journey here. They no longer move, but they still carry the marks of their working lives. As you walk back toward the exit, listen again. The echo of footsteps. The low murmur of voices. Maybe a child, still naming engines. Outside, somewhere in Mulhouse, a modern train is pulling out of a station right now. It runs on the lessons stored in this hall.
