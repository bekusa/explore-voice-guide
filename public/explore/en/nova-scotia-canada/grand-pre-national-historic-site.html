The guide

The story

Start with the grass under your feet. It is soft, a little damp, and very flat. Beyond the gardens the land runs out toward a wide, pale horizon, and on most days a steady wind comes in off the water. That wind carries salt. You are standing on ground that was once sea marsh, ground the tides covered twice a day, until people decided to keep the sea out by hand.

The name Grand-Pré is French. It means the great meadow, and the meadow is the reason this place exists. In the late seventeenth century, Acadian families came here from settlements further down the coast. The Acadians were descendants of French colonists who had made a home in this part of North America. They did not clear forest for their farms. Instead, they looked at the tidal marshes of the Minas Basin and saw rich soil waiting under the salt water.

To understand what they did, you need to know about the tides. This basin is part of the Bay of Fundy, and the Bay of Fundy has some of the highest tides on Earth. Twice a day, enormous volumes of water push in and drain away again. The water leaves thick red-brown silt behind. That silt is extremely fertile... but only if you can stop the sea from returning to soak it in salt.

The Acadian answer was the dyke. Working with shovels, oxen and their own backs, families built long earthen walls along the edge of the marsh. Into these walls they set the aboiteau — a wooden sluice with a hinged flap. At low tide, fresh water drained out through the flap. At high tide, the sea pressed the flap shut. It is a simple idea, and it worked so well that farmers here still depend on dykes today.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. The farmland you see beyond the park is not a museum display. It is living, working land, and much of it still sits below the level of the highest tides. The dyke system has been repaired and extended over the centuries, but the basic pattern began with those Acadian families. This continuity is a big part of why the wider Landscape of Grand Pré was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List.

The Acadians did not arrive in an empty land. This region is part of Mi'kma'ki, the ancestral territory of the Mi'kmaq people. Historians describe generally close relations between Acadians and Mi'kmaq in this period, including trade, alliance and some intermarriage. As you walk, keep in mind that the story of this meadow is older than any French name for it.

For several generations, Grand-Pré grew into one of the largest Acadian communities. Farms spread across the uplands, orchards were planted, and a parish church stood near the heart of the settlement. But this was contested ground. The territory changed hands between France and Britain, and the Acadians tried to stay neutral. Britain wanted an unconditional oath of loyalty. Many Acadians refused to sign an oath that might force them to take up arms against France or their Mi'kmaq neighbours.

In 1755, that tension ended in catastrophe. British colonial authorities decided to remove the Acadians from the region. At Grand-Pré, the men and boys were summoned to the parish church. There, in early September, the British officer in command read the deportation order aloud. The church had become a prison. In the weeks that followed, families were loaded onto ships and scattered along the Atlantic coast and beyond. Homes and barns were burned so that no one could return.

Acadians call this the Grand Dérangement — the great upheaval. The deportations from Nova Scotia continued for years. Families were separated, and many people died at sea or from disease. Some eventually found their way to Louisiana, where their descendants became known as Cajuns. Others returned to the Maritime provinces, but rarely to their old farms. New settlers from New England took over the dyked lands here.

Now look toward the small stone church in the gardens. It is easy to assume it is the original. It is not. It is a memorial church, built in the twentieth century on the site traditionally believed to be the old parish church. Inside, you will find paintings and exhibits that retell the deportation. It was built as a place of memory, partly with money raised by Acadians themselves.

Nearby stands a bronze woman with her head turned back over her shoulder. This is Evangeline. Here is the second surprise. She never lived. She is a fictional heroine from a long poem by the American writer Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, published in the mid-nineteenth century. In the poem, Evangeline is separated from her fiancé during the deportation and searches for him for the rest of her life. The poem became hugely popular, and it brought the Acadian story to readers around the world.

That is why Grand-Pré feels like two places at once. One is the documented history of farms, dykes and a forced removal. The other is the landscape of a poem, with its willows, its well and its grieving heroine. Look for the old willow trees and the well in the park. Locals say some willows descend from trees the Acadians planted, but treat that as tradition, not record. For many Acadians today, this site is a place of pilgrimage, reunion and pride.

Before you leave, walk to the edge of the gardens and look out across the meadow. On a clear day, the dark headland of Cape Blomidon rises across the water. The fields in front of you are still divided by drainage ditches and protected by dykes. If you come back in a few hours, the tide in the basin will have moved a long way. Stand still for a moment. Feel the wind again. You are looking at land that people made, lost, and still remember.
