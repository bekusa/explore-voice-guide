The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through tall birches, and their white trunks catch the light like pale columns. Somewhere a crow calls. Underfoot, the path is soft earth and fallen leaves. This is the road Leo Tolstoy walked almost every day of his long life, here on his family estate, Yasnaya Polyana.

The name means something like "bright glade" or "clear clearing", and it fits. The estate lies in gently rolling country south of the old arms-making city of Tula, about a day's journey from Moscow in Tolstoy's time. It is a mix of woodland, orchards, meadows and ponds. There is no grand palace. What you will find is a working country estate, kept close to the way its most famous owner left it.

You probably entered between two small white towers at the gate. From there, a long avenue of birches runs toward the house. The family called it the "preshpekt", a homely country version of the word "prospect", or avenue. Tolstoy walked and rode along it countless times. Visitors from across the world walked it too, coming to see the writer in his final decades. Writers, students, pilgrims and curious strangers all arrived on this same strip of earth.

Tolstoy was born here in 1828. His mother came from the Volkonsky family, an old line of Russian princes, and the estate passed down through her side. She died when he was still a small child. Later, in War and Peace, he gave the character of Princess Marya some features that many readers connect with the mother he barely remembered. In a real sense, this landscape is where his imagination began.

Now, here is the first surprise. The house where Tolstoy was born is not here anymore. As a young man, he lost heavily at cards, and the main house was sold to pay his debts. It was taken apart and carried away to another estate. The house you see today was once a side wing of the old manor. Tolstoy expanded it over the years as his family grew. Look closely, and you can see that it was built in stages rather than all at once.

And the family did grow. Tolstoy married Sophia Behrs in 1862, and they had a large household of children. Sophia was far more than a hostess. She ran the estate, managed the money and copied her husband's manuscripts by hand. His handwriting was famously difficult to read, and he revised again and again. Sophia copied draft after draft of his great novels, often late into the night, by lamplight.

It was in this house that Tolstoy wrote War and Peace and Anna Karenina. Inside, the rooms keep the plain furniture, the family portraits and the writer's study. His library is still here, with many thousands of books in several languages. Tolstoy read widely, from ancient philosophy to religious texts from many traditions. Notice how simple the rooms feel. In later life, he rejected luxury, and the house shows that change of mind.

Near the house stands an old tree that the family called the Tree of the Poor. Local peasants and travellers came here to wait for Tolstoy, to ask for help, advice or money. A bell once hung in its branches. It was rung to call the household to meals. The tree is a quiet reminder that this estate was never only a writer's retreat. It was a meeting point between a count and the villagers who lived around him.

Tolstoy took those villagers seriously. As a young man, he opened a school for peasant children here. He wrote his own teaching materials, including simple reading books and short stories for children. His ideas were unusual for the time. He believed children learned best through curiosity, not fear. Many visitors are surprised to discover that the author of enormous novels also wrote primers for village children.

In his later years, Tolstoy's beliefs grew more radical. He preached nonviolence, simple living and a personal form of Christianity, and he wanted to give away his property. This caused deep conflict at home, especially with Sophia. In 1910, at the age of eighty-two, he left Yasnaya Polyana secretly, before dawn. He fell ill on the train and died days later at a small railway station called Astapovo. His body was brought back here to be buried.

The walk to his grave is the heart of any visit. Follow the path into the forest called the Old Zakaz. There, at the edge of a ravine, you will find a low, grass-covered mound. There is no cross. There is no headstone and no inscription. This is the second surprise for many visitors. One of the most famous writers in the world chose to rest under nothing but grass and trees.

The place was not chosen at random. Tolstoy told a story from his childhood. His older brother Nikolai said he had buried a little green stick in this spot. On it, he claimed, was written a secret that would make all people happy and end all hatred. The boys never found it. Tolstoy asked to be buried where the green stick lay. Most visitors stand here in silence. Many people simply stop, breathe, and listen to the trees.

After Tolstoy's death, the estate became a museum, and it has remained one ever since. During the Second World War, the region was occupied for a short time, and the house was damaged. Many treasures had already been sent away for safety, and the estate was restored after the war. Today, descendants of the Tolstoy family have played a role in caring for this place. The orchards still bear apples, and horses still graze nearby.

As you walk back toward the gate, slow down. Look at the ponds, the orchards and the long line of birches. These are the same views that Tolstoy saw when he worked out the lives of his characters. Notice the sound of your steps on the path, and the shifting light through the leaves. Somewhere in this ordinary, beautiful countryside, some of the greatest novels ever written took shape.

Practical tips

Allow a separate unhurried walk to the forest grave.

Come early to walk the birch avenue in quiet.
