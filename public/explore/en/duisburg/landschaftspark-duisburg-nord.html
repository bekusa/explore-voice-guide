The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere above you a loose sheet of metal ticks in the wind. The air smells of damp rust and wild birch. Rising ahead is a tangle of pipes, ladders and towers, all stained the colour of strong tea. This is not a ruin left to rot, and it is not a museum behind glass. It is a former ironworks that people now walk through, climb, picnic in and play in. Welcome to Landschaftspark Duisburg-Nord.

For most of the twentieth century, this ground belonged to the Meiderich ironworks, part of the Thyssen industrial empire. The blast furnaces here began producing iron in the early twentieth century. Day and night, coal and iron ore went in at the top, and molten metal poured out at the bottom. The Ruhr region around you was the industrial engine of Germany. Towns grew up around mines and furnaces like this one, and whole families worked shifts in their heat and noise.

Then the world changed. Steel production moved to larger, newer plants, and the furnaces of Meiderich fell silent in 1985. Many sites like this across Europe were simply torn down. The land was cleared, the soil was buried, and the memory went with it. Here, something different happened. Planners, architects and local residents asked a bold question — what if the ironworks could stay, and the landscape could grow back around it?

That question became part of a large regional programme called the IBA Emscher Park. It was an international building exhibition, which in Germany means a long-term project to renew a whole region through architecture and landscape. The design for this park came from the landscape architect Peter Latz and his studio. Their idea was simple and radical. Do not hide the industry. Keep the furnaces, the bunkers and the rail tracks, and let nature and people move in. The park opened to the public in the 1990s.

Look around and you will see that idea everywhere. Birch trees grow out of cracks in the concrete. Mosses cover old railway sleepers. Wildflowers bloom along the edges of slag heaps. Many of these plants arrived on their own, some carried as seeds in shipments of ore from distant countries. So the botany here quietly reflects the trade routes of the old ironworks. Ecologists have found a surprising range of species in this mix of steel and soil.

Now turn your attention to the blast furnaces themselves. These towering structures are the heart of the site. Each one is a column of riveted steel, wrapped in walkways, pipes and staircases. Hot air was blown into the base, and the furnace reached temperatures far beyond anything a home oven could manage. One of the furnaces is open for visitors to climb. The stairs are steep and metal, and you can feel the wind grow stronger with every level.

The climb is worth the effort. From the top platform, the Ruhr spreads out in every direction. You can see church spires, motorways, power stations, wind turbines and the green line of distant woodland. On a clear day, the view shows how tightly industry and daily life are woven together in this region. It also gives you a sense of scale. From up here, the people walking along the paths below look very small beside the old machinery.

Here is one detail many visitors miss. Down near the ground, look for the old ore bunkers. These deep concrete chambers once stored raw materials before they went into the furnaces. Today, some of them hold quiet, enclosed gardens, sheltered from the wind. Others have been turned into climbing walls, run with the German Alpine Club. On a busy afternoon you may see climbers roped up against the concrete, practising routes on surfaces that once held tonnes of iron ore.

A second surprise waits inside the old gasometer. This large round tank once stored gas from the ironworks. Now it is filled with water and used as a diving centre. Divers descend into the dark interior to explore sunken objects placed there for training and fun. It is a strange thought — scuba divers practising in a steel drum, in the middle of an industrial park, many kilometres from the sea.

Next, find the open square of heavy iron plates known as the Piazza Metallica. The plates were taken from the casting areas of the old works, where molten metal once flowed. Walk across them and notice the different textures and patterns of wear. Elsewhere, the former power station building now hosts concerts, festivals, theatre and corporate events. The cultural festival of the Ruhr region, the Ruhrtriennale, has used spaces here as stages.

Water runs through this story too. A channel follows the line of the old Emscher river, which for decades served as an open sewer for the region's industry. Today, rainwater is collected from roofs and paved surfaces and guided through the park. Part of the restoration work was about cleaning water and soil, not only about saving old buildings. Stand beside the channel and you will often hear frogs or see ducks where there was once polluted runoff.

If you stay until after dark on certain evenings, the park takes on a completely different character. A light installation by the British lighting artist Jonathan Park washes the furnaces and pipes in shades of red, blue and green. The colours slowly shift across the metal. The effect is calm rather than flashy. Families stroll with torches, photographers set up tripods, and the old ironworks glows against the night sky like a sleeping machine.

As you walk on, take a last look at the furnace nearest to you. Notice the rivets, the flaking paint, the young trees pushing through the gravel at its base. This place was built to make iron, and it now makes something else — space for people to breathe, play and remember. Listen again to that ticking metal in the wind, and to the voices drifting along the paths. The work here never really stopped. It simply changed shape.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for steep metal stairs and gravel paths.
Climb the open blast furnace for wide views across the Ruhr.
Stay after dark on lighting evenings for the coloured furnaces.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed furnace platforms.
Check the events calendar for concerts in the power station.
