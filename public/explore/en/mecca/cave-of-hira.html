The guide

The story

Stop for a moment and feel the rock under your feet. It is dark, rough and warm, and it gives a little grit under each step. The air is dry. Somewhere below, the sound of the city comes up the slope as a soft, steady hum. Above you, the path keeps rising in steps of stone and concrete. This is Jabal al-Nour, the Mountain of Light, and near its summit is a small opening in the rock: the Cave of Hira.

For Muslims around the world, this small space holds one of the most important moments in history. According to Islamic tradition, this is where the Prophet Muhammad received the first revelation of the Quran. That event is usually dated to around 610 of the Common Era, during the month of Ramadan. Before that night, he was a merchant of Mecca, known among his people as trustworthy. After it, everything changed — for him, for this city, and eventually for much of the world.

The mountain rises on the northeastern edge of Mecca. From the upper path, you can look back toward the city centre and the towers that surround the Grand Mosque. Today, roads, homes and traffic reach right up to the foot of the slope. But in the seventh century, this was open desert land outside the town. To come here, a person had to walk a long way across dry valleys. That distance was part of the point. This was a place to be alone.

The early sources describe how the Prophet came to this cave for periods of quiet reflection. He would stay for several nights at a time. He brought simple food and water with him, and then he went home to refill them. Tradition also remembers his wife, Khadijah, as his strongest support in these years. Some accounts say that she sometimes brought him provisions herself. Picture that climb without paved steps or handrails... and with a basket of food.

Then came the night that Muslims remember above all others. According to the accounts collected in the classical hadith literature, the Angel Gabriel — Jibril in Arabic — appeared to the Prophet in the cave. The angel gave him a single command: Read. The Prophet answered that he could not read. The command came again, and again. Then the first words of the Quran were spoken. In one common translation, they begin: Read in the name of your Lord who created.

Those verses open the ninety-sixth chapter of the Quran, known as al-Alaq. It is a surprise for many visitors. The first word of a scripture that would shape a civilisation was a call to read. Islam would later place enormous value on learning, writing and scholarship. Many Muslims see a deep connection between that tradition and this first command, spoken in a small, dark room of rock.

The moment did not feel like a triumph. The sources describe the Prophet coming down the mountain shaken and afraid. He went home to Khadijah and asked her to cover him with a cloak. She comforted him and told him that God would not abandon a man who was kind to his family and generous to the poor. She then took him to her elderly cousin, Waraqah ibn Nawfal, a Christian who knew the earlier scriptures. Waraqah told him that he had received a message like those given to earlier prophets.

Now look at the climb itself. The path is long and steep, with a great many steps cut into the slope. Many people take around an hour to reach the top, and some take longer. You will pass people of every age and nationality. Some recite prayers quietly as they climb. Others stop on the rocks to catch their breath and look out over the city. Small stalls and resting points sometimes appear along the way, set up by local people.

Near the top, the trail narrows. To reach the cave, you usually squeeze through a gap between large boulders. The cave itself is very small. It has room for only a few people at once, and a grown man can barely stand up straight inside. The walls are bare, dark granite. There is no decoration, no inscription carved by any builder. Many visitors find that simplicity more powerful than any monument could be.

Here is a detail that many people miss. Locals often point out that from the area of the cave, a person faces in the general direction of the Kaaba, the sacred cube at the centre of the Grand Mosque. Whether or not you can see it clearly through the haze and towers today, the idea is meaningful to many pilgrims. Even in solitude, the cave was not cut off from the heart of the city below.

It is also worth knowing what the visit is — and what it is not. Climbing to the Cave of Hira is not part of the rituals of Hajj or Umrah. Islamic scholars generally teach that there is no special act of worship tied to the cave itself. Most people come to remember, to reflect, and to stand in the place where the story began. In recent years, a cultural district has also opened near the foot of the mountain, with exhibitions about the revelation.

As you stand here, pay attention to the human texture around you. Notice the many languages: Urdu, Malay, Turkish, Arabic, French, and more. Notice the scratched names and messages on some of the rocks, left by visitors over many years. Notice the pigeons on the ledges and the way people share water with strangers. The mountain is crowded now. Yet the small cave still keeps a feeling of quiet, if you give it a moment.

So before you begin the long walk down, take one more breath of this dry mountain air. Look at the dark rock around the opening, and at the city spread out below. Think about a man sitting here alone in the night, far from the noise of the town. As you descend, listen for the moment when the hum of traffic grows louder again... and remember how quiet this place once was.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start the climb at dawn to avoid the worst heat.
Carry plenty of water for the long, steep ascent.
Wear sturdy shoes with good grip on dusty stone.
Expect queues at the cave entrance during busy seasons.
Mecca is open only to Muslims, so plan accordingly.
