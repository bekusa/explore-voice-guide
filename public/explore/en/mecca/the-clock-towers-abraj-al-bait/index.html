The guide

The story

Stand in the wide marble plaza and tilt your head back. The stone underfoot is cool, even in the heat. Around you is the soft rush of thousands of footsteps, the murmur of prayer, and the rustle of white cloth. Then your eyes keep climbing, past hotel windows and pale stone, until they reach a clock face so large that it seems closer than it is. Above it, a golden crescent sits against the sky.

You are looking at the Abraj Al-Bait, a complex of seven towers that rises directly beside the Masjid al-Haram, the Great Mosque of Mecca. The name means roughly the Towers of the House. The House is the Kaaba, the black-draped cube at the heart of the mosque. Muslims around the world turn toward it in prayer. So this enormous building stands on the most important ground in Islam, and it was designed to face that ground.

The tallest of the seven is the Makkah Royal Clock Tower. It reaches roughly six hundred metres, and when it was finished in the early 2010s, it was among the tallest buildings on Earth. Only a few skyscrapers in the world rise higher today. Yet many visitors notice something unusual. The tower does not feel like a business district skyscraper. Its lower floors are wide and solid, and the shaft above them is heavy and squared, with a tall spire at the top.

This shape is deliberate. The designers looked to the great clock towers of Europe, such as the tower that holds Big Ben in London, and then multiplied the scale many times over. The result is part modern hotel and part civic monument. From many streets in Mecca, you can see the clock above the rooftops. It works as a landmark in the old sense. It helps pilgrims find their way back toward the mosque.

Now look at the clock itself. Each of the four faces is about forty-three metres across. That makes them some of the largest clock faces ever built. A single minute hand is longer than a city bus. The faces carry Arabic script and decorative patterns, picked out in green and gold. At night, thousands of small lights shine through the faces. The clock glows white and green over the city, and it can be seen from far away.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The tower is not only a clock. It also works as a call to prayer you can see. At the five daily prayer times, lights near the top of the spire flash. The flashing helps people far from the mosque know that the prayer has begun. Loudspeakers carry the call to prayer across the plaza, so the moment arrives through both sound and light.

The golden crescent at the very top is a structure in its own right. It is large enough to hold rooms inside, and it is used for observing the moon. This matters more than it may seem. The Islamic calendar is a lunar calendar. Each month begins with the sighting of the new crescent moon. So a tower that tells the time of day also plays a small part in the much older practice of watching the sky.

The ground beneath the towers carries a difficult story. Until the early 2000s, a hill near the mosque held the Ajyad Fortress. The Ottomans built it in the late eighteenth century to protect the holy sites. The Ottomans were the Turkish dynasty that ruled much of the Middle East for centuries. Saudi authorities demolished the fortress to make room for this complex. The decision caused protest in Turkey, and it is still debated today.

That debate is part of a wider conversation about Mecca. Each year, millions of pilgrims come for the Hajj, the great pilgrimage, and for Umrah, the smaller pilgrimage that can happen at any time. The city has expanded hard to hold them. Supporters say towers like these give pilgrims rooms, food, and services within walking distance of the mosque. Critics say much of the old city has been lost. Both views shape how people talk about the building.

Inside, the complex is a small city of its own. There are hotels on the upper floors, with rooms that look straight down into the mosque. There are prayer halls, so that guests can join the congregation when the plaza is full. Lower down, a large shopping mall sells clothing, perfumes, dates, and prayer beads. Food courts serve meals from across the Muslim world. The building is made to support people during pilgrimage, from waking to sleeping.

The tower also holds a museum near the top, focused on the clock, on astronomy, and on timekeeping in Islamic tradition. Muslim scholars studied the stars for centuries, partly to calculate prayer times and the direction of Mecca. The museum connects the huge machine in front of you to that long history. Access arrangements change, so ask at the hotel or information desks about current visits.

One practical note matters before anything else. Mecca is open only to Muslims, and entry to the city is controlled. Most visitors here are pilgrims, many of them dressed in the simple white garments of ihram, the state of ritual purity for pilgrimage. The atmosphere is devotional, not touristic. People pause and pray in the plaza itself. Move gently, keep your voice low, and give space to those in prayer.

If you want the best view of the clock, step back from the base. Up close, the tower is almost too large to read. From farther across the plaza, the whole structure comes into view at once, with the mosque's minarets in front and the clock above. In the evening, when the faces light up and the plaza fills, the contrast between the old shrine and the new tower is at its clearest.

Before you walk on, look up one last time. The hands are moving, slowly, over a city that has welcomed pilgrims for well over a thousand years. Below, people still walk around the Kaaba, as pilgrims have done for generations. Listen to the footsteps on the marble. Feel the evening air. The clock tells the hour, but the rhythm of this place still comes from the prayers.
