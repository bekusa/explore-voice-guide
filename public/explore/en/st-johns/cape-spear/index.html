The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind comes in off the Atlantic without anything to slow it down. It pushes the grass flat against the rock, and it carries salt you can taste on your lips. Below you, the sea breaks white against dark cliffs. Somewhere out there, beyond the grey line of the horizon, the next land is Europe. You are standing at Cape Spear, on the island of Newfoundland, and for a moment there is nothing between you and the open ocean but air.

This headland is the most easterly point of land in North America, if you leave Greenland aside. That simple fact shapes everything here. When the sun rises over the continent, it reaches these rocks before it reaches anywhere else. On clear summer mornings, people drive out from Saint John's in the dark just to stand here and watch it happen. It is a quiet kind of pilgrimage... coffee in hand, jackets zipped tight, everyone facing the same direction.

The name itself carries a small story. Early Portuguese sailors who mapped this coast are said to have called it Cabo da Esperança, which means Cape of Hope. French mariners later turned that into Cap d'Espoir. Over time, English speakers wore the words down into something shorter, and Cape of Hope became Cape Spear. So the name you see on the signs is really a hope that has been passed from language to language, and slowly worn smooth.

Now turn toward the hill and look for the old lighthouse. It does not look like the tall, slender towers you may expect. Instead, you see a square, two-storey wooden house, painted white, with a light tower rising from its centre. The keeper and his family lived in the rooms built around that tower. First lit in the 1830s, it is the oldest surviving lighthouse in Newfoundland. Today it has been restored to look much as it did in the early nineteenth century.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The first lighting apparatus inside this tower was not new. It came secondhand from a lighthouse in Scotland. It was a system of reflectors and lamps that burned oil, and it was shipped across the ocean to warn sailors off these rocks. In those days, the keeper climbed the stairs every evening, trimmed the wicks, polished the reflectors, and kept the flame steady through the long night.

For much of the lighthouse's working life, that keeper came from one family — the Cantwells. Generation after generation, Cantwell men and their families tended the light, for well over a century. Think about what that meant. Children grew up here, with the wind against the walls and the fog rolling in from the sea. They learned the work by watching their parents. The light was not just a job for them. It was the family home, and it was the family's duty.

If you look nearby, you will see a second, more modern lighthouse — a plain concrete tower. It was built in the 1950s, and the working light moved there. The old wooden station was retired from service and later restored as a museum. Together, the two towers show how lighthouse keeping changed. The first needed a family living under the lamp. The second needed far less human hand. Today, the old station is part of a National Historic Site, protected for visitors like you.

Now walk a little way down the slope, toward the water, and the story changes completely. Set into the rock are concrete gun emplacements, bunkers, and tunnels. These belong to a coastal defence battery from the Second World War. During that war, German submarines hunted ships in the North Atlantic, and Saint John's was an important harbour for convoys. Large guns were placed here to guard the approach to the harbour. You can still walk into some of the passages, where the air turns cold and damp.

Standing inside those bunkers, it is easy to forget the view outside. But the soldiers who served here lived with it every day. They watched the same grey water for the shape of a periscope or the shadow of a ship. The cold was constant. The fog could hide everything within minutes. It is a strange contrast — the peaceful lighthouse on the hill, built to save ships, and the guns below, built to stop them.

Now look back out to sea, because the ocean here is never empty for long. In late spring and early summer, icebergs can drift past this coast. They break off glaciers in Greenland and float south on the currents, along a stretch locals call Iceberg Alley. Some are the size of a house. Some are far larger. In summer, humpback whales come close to shore to feed. Watch for a sudden spout of mist, or a dark curved back rising from the waves.

The ground beneath you also deserves attention. The rock is old, hard, and folded, with patches of low shrubs, mosses, and wild berries growing in the cracks. Nothing grows tall here, because the wind does not allow it. Seabirds wheel along the cliffs and ride the air currents just above the water. A long-distance hiking path, the East Coast Trail, also passes through this area, so you may meet walkers with muddy boots who have come along the cliffs on foot.

A word of care, too. The cliffs at Cape Spear are steep, and the rocks near the water can be wet and slippery. Large waves sometimes surge higher than they look from a distance. Stay on the marked paths and behind the barriers. The weather can also change quickly. Bright sunshine can turn to thick fog in the time it takes to walk from the lighthouse to the bunkers, so an extra layer is always a good idea.

Before you leave, find a spot where you can stand still and face east. Feel the wind on your face. Listen to the waves, the gulls, and perhaps a foghorn somewhere along the coast. Behind you lies a whole continent — cities, forests, and thousands of kilometres of land. In front of you lies only the ocean. Take one more look at the white lighthouse on the hill. Then, as you walk away, notice how quickly the sound of the sea follows you, all the way back to the road.

Practical tips

Arrive before dawn to see North America's first sunrise.
Bring a windproof layer even on warm summer days.
Stay on marked paths; waves can surge onto low rocks.
Bring binoculars to scan for whales and passing icebergs.
Wear sturdy shoes for the bunkers and uneven trails.
