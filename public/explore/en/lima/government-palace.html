The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the Plaza Mayor and listen. Pigeons scatter across the paving stones. A shoeshine brush taps against a wooden box. Somewhere a vendor calls out. Then the sound that does not belong to any ordinary square: the sharp ring of boots on stone behind tall iron railings. Ahead of you, a long pale facade stretches across the whole northern side of the plaza. This is the Government Palace of Peru, the official seat of the country's president. Peruvians often call it by an older name, the House of Pizarro.

That name points straight to the beginning of colonial Lima. In 1535, the Spanish conquistador Francisco Pizarro founded the city here, near the Rímac River. He chose this spot for his own residence, facing the new main square. Long before the Spanish arrived, the land belonged to the people of the Rímac valley. Local tradition places the home of a native chief, Taulichusco, on this same ground. So the palace stands on a layer of power that is older than Lima itself.

Pizarro did not live here long. In 1541, a group of his rivals, supporters of his former partner Diego de Almagro, forced their way into the house. Pizarro was killed inside his own residence. It is a dark first chapter for a building that would go on to hold power for centuries. Each president who works here today sits on the same site where the city's founder died.

After Pizarro, the house became the residence of the viceroys. A viceroy was the Spanish king's direct representative, and Lima was the capital of a vast colonial territory in South America. For roughly three centuries, decisions made in this place reached far beyond modern Peru. Then, in the nineteenth century, independence arrived. The viceroys left, and the building passed to the new republic. Its role changed. Its address did not.

Now look closely at the facade. What you see is not colonial at all... and this surprises many visitors. Earthquakes, fires and repeated rebuilding wore away the older structures over the centuries. The present palace is largely a work of the twentieth century. Its main front was designed by the architect Ricardo de Jaxa Malachowski, and the building was completed in the late 1930s. The style borrows from French and Spanish baroque traditions — grand, symmetrical and formal.

Notice how the stone-coloured front contrasts with the rest of the square. To your right, if you face the palace, rises the cathedral, with its twin towers. Beside it stands the Archbishop's Palace, famous for its dark carved wooden balconies. Those enclosed balconies are a Lima signature. They allowed people to watch the street without being seen. The Government Palace has a different character: open, ordered and official, with a long line of windows and heavy gates.

The railings themselves are worth a moment. Tall black iron bars separate the public square from the palace forecourt. Behind them, guards stand in uniforms that recall the soldiers of the independence era. Their helmets, plumes and polished boots look almost theatrical. Yet these are serving soldiers of the presidential guard. The changing of the guard is a formal ceremony with music and marching, and crowds gather along the railings to watch it.

Here is a detail that many people miss. During the ceremony, the military band does not only play marches. Visitors have often heard Peruvian popular tunes woven into the performance, including well-known waltzes and folk melodies. It is a reminder that this is not only a stage for the state. It is also a stage for national identity. Ask a local about the music, and you may get a story about a favourite song.

Inside the palace, beyond the reach of most visitors, are formal salons, courtyards and reception halls. One courtyard holds an old fig tree. Legend has it that Pizarro himself planted this tree in the sixteenth century. Locals point to it with affection, but the story is tradition rather than proven history. Either way, a living tree inside a seat of government is a quiet, human touch.

The inside can sometimes be visited on organised tours. These normally require a reservation made well in advance, and identity documents are usually checked. The rules change from time to time, so it is wise to ask at your hotel or a tourist office. If you cannot go in, do not worry. Much of what makes this place memorable happens out here, in the square, in full public view.

Take a moment to think about the sky above the palace. For much of the year, Lima sits under a soft grey ceiling of cloud. A fine mist, called garúa locally, drifts in from the Pacific during the cooler months. That light flattens shadows and turns the facade a gentle, even tone. On rarer sunny days, the stone glows warmer and the ironwork casts sharp lines on the ground. Same building, very different moods.

The palace also turns its back on something important. Behind it runs the Rímac River, the reason the Spanish chose this valley at all. Next to the palace, close to the river, stands an old railway station building that now hosts a centre for Peruvian literature. If you walk around the side, you can see how the government, the river and the old city connect. The plaza is not an island. It is the knot that ties the historic centre together.

This square has also seen protests, celebrations and national ceremonies. When Peruvians want to be heard by those in power, many of them come here. Police barriers sometimes appear along the edges, and access can change at short notice. That too is part of the experience. You are not visiting a museum. You are standing in front of a working centre of government, where the country's politics play out in real time.

Before you move on, turn slowly in a full circle. See the cathedral towers, the wooden balconies, the bronze fountain in the centre of the plaza, and the long pale front of the palace. Listen again for those boots behind the railings. Nearly five centuries of decisions began on this patch of ground. And as you walk away into the streets of old Lima, notice how many of them still lead back here, to the square and the house that stands above it.
