The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, on the northern edge of the old city, the wind does most of the talking. It moves across the sand and around the broken stone with a low, dry rush. The ground underfoot is gritty and uneven, scattered with pale limestone fragments that turn honey-coloured as the sun climbs. Look around you. This patch of desert once held one of the best-preserved temples in all of Palmyra. Today it holds something quieter, and in some ways more powerful: what is left after a temple has been destroyed.

This was the Temple of Baalshamin. The name means something like Lord of the Heavens. Baalshamin was a sky god, worshipped across the Semitic-speaking world of the ancient Near East. He was a god of rain, storms and fertile seasons. For a desert caravan city, that mattered enormously. Rain decided whether crops grew and whether animals survived. Here at Palmyra, Baalshamin was often linked with the eagle. The eagle was a bird of the open sky, and it fitted a god who ruled from above.

To understand why a temple like this stood here, picture the city at its height. Palmyra grew rich around an oasis, roughly halfway between the Mediterranean coast and the river Euphrates. Caravans carrying goods from the East passed through. Merchants, camel drivers and traders filled the streets. The city spoke Aramaic, a Semitic language of the region. Its elite also used Greek, and the city sat under the power of Rome. That mix of worlds shows up in almost every building you can still find here.

The temple you are standing near was not built in a single moment. Worship of Baalshamin on this spot seems to go back to at least the first century. Over time, courtyards and colonnades grew up around the sacred area. The main shrine, the inner chamber that archaeologists call the cella, was dedicated in the early second century. An inscription records that dedication. It also names the man who paid for much of it — a wealthy Palmyrene citizen called Malé Agrippa.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. That same Malé Agrippa is also remembered in an inscription for hosting the Roman emperor Hadrian. Hadrian visited Palmyra around 129, during his long journeys through the eastern provinces. The temple's dedication came only a short time later. So this small sanctuary is tied, quite directly, to one of the most famous imperial visits in the city's history. A local benefactor, an emperor passing through, and a god of the sky — all in one building.

Now, imagine how the temple looked before its destruction. It was compact rather than grand. A porch of six columns stood at the front, topped with leafy Corinthian capitals, the ornate style of carved acanthus leaves. Behind the porch sat the stone chamber of the god. The walls were lined with flat pilasters, columns carved into the surface of the wall. The overall look was classical, the kind of temple you might see in Greece or Italy. Yet the god inside, and the people who prayed to him, were local.

The second surprise is on the columns themselves. Many Palmyrene columns had small stone shelves, called consoles, projecting about halfway up the shaft. Statues of important citizens once stood on them. Beneath each shelf, an inscription explained who was being honoured and why. These texts were often written in two languages, Greek and Palmyrene Aramaic. It was a way of saying thank you in public — and of making sure the whole city knew who had paid for what.

The temple's story did not end with the Romans. In the Byzantine period, when Christianity became dominant, the building was turned into a church. This kind of reuse happened across the region. It is one reason the temple survived so well for so long. A building that is still in use is usually repaired, roofed and protected. Many temples that were abandoned were stripped for stone. Baalshamin's shrine stayed largely whole for well over a thousand years.

In the middle of the twentieth century, a Swiss archaeological mission excavated this part of the site. Their work during the 1950s cleared the courtyards and studied the inscriptions. It gave scholars much of what they know about the temple's phases of building. For decades afterwards, travellers came here and found a small, handsome temple standing almost complete under the desert sky. Palmyra was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1980, and this temple was one of its quieter treasures.

Then came 2015. During the Syrian civil war, fighters of the so-called Islamic State group took control of Palmyra. In August of that year, they packed the temple with explosives and blew it up. Satellite images confirmed the destruction soon afterwards. The same summer, the group killed Khaled al-Asaad, a respected scholar who had cared for Palmyra's antiquities for decades. The Temple of Bel, the city's great sanctuary to the south, was destroyed in the same period.

So what are you looking at today? Mostly fragments. Blocks of cut stone lie where the force of the blast threw them. Pieces of carved molding, column drums and broken capitals rest in the sand. Look closely and you may still see traces of decoration on individual stones — a curl of acanthus leaf, the edge of a cornice, a line of carved beading. Each fragment was once part of a whole. Specialists have studied the debris carefully, because many pieces can be identified and recorded.

It is worth taking a slow walk around the edge of the area, rather than climbing on the stone. The wider setting helps you understand the temple's place in the city. The great colonnaded street lies to the south. On the hills beyond, you can see the tower tombs where Palmyrene families buried their dead. And behind you, the desert opens out toward the horizon. The temple once sat inside this whole web of streets, homes and markets. Now the open space around it feels much larger.

There is a strange double feeling here. On one side, there is loss — a building that survived nearly two thousand years did not survive a single summer. On the other side, there is evidence of care. The inscriptions were copied and photographed long ago. The plans were drawn. The excavation records exist. Scholars, local and foreign, still argue over how best to protect and perhaps one day restore what remains. The temple lives on in archives as well as in stone.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Feel the heat coming off the limestone and the grit under your shoes. Notice how the light changes the colour of the broken blocks, from pale grey to gold. Somewhere in this scatter of stone are the words of people who stood here long ago and wanted to be remembered. As you walk away, look back once. The god of the open sky is gone from his house — but the sky above it is exactly the same.
