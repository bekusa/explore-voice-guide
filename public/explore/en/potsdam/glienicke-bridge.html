The guide

The story

Stand at the railing for a moment and listen. Tyres hum across the deck behind you. Below, the Havel moves slowly, dark green in the shade and silver where the light catches it. A tram bell rings somewhere on the Potsdam side. The steel under your hand is cool and painted green, layer over layer. Everything feels calm. Yet for almost three decades, this was one of the most closely watched places in Europe.

This is Glienicke Bridge. It crosses the Havel at a narrow point where the river runs between wide lakes. On one side lies Potsdam, the old royal residence city of the Prussian kings. On the other side lies Berlin, in its leafy south-western edge near Wannsee. The bridge is short. You can walk across it in a few minutes. But those few minutes once separated two political worlds.

People have crossed the water here for centuries. The first crossing was a simple wooden bridge, built for the rulers of Brandenburg as they travelled between Berlin and their hunting grounds and palaces around Potsdam. Over time the traffic grew, and the wooden structures were replaced. In the nineteenth century, the great Prussian architect Karl Friedrich Schinkel designed a brick bridge for this spot. Schinkel shaped much of the look of Berlin and Potsdam, so his work here fitted a wider royal landscape.

The steel bridge you see today dates from 1907. Engineers chose a long, low span so that boats could pass easily underneath. Look at the shape. The main girders rise in gentle curves above the roadway, and the riveted steel follows clean, regular lines. It is a practical design from the early age of motor traffic. It was built for carriages, cyclists, and the first automobiles, long before anyone imagined soldiers guarding its centre.

The Second World War brought damage here too. In the last days of fighting in the spring of 1945, the bridge was blown up and partly collapsed into the river. After the war, Potsdam fell inside the Soviet zone, and the western part of Berlin across the water came under American control. The bridge was rebuilt and reopened in 1949. The new East German state gave it a hopeful name — the Bridge of Unity. Few names would soon sound so strange.

When the Berlin Wall went up in 1961, the border ran right through the middle of this bridge. A line was marked across the deck. East German guards stood on the Potsdam side. Ordinary citizens could no longer cross. For years, only Allied military personnel and a few diplomats were allowed to pass. Imagine the quiet here then — no traffic, no strolling couples, just watchtowers, barriers, and the river moving underneath.

And then the bridge found its most famous role. It became a place where East and West exchanged prisoners. On a cold morning in February 1962, the American pilot Francis Gary Powers walked across from the east. His U-2 spy plane had been shot down over the Soviet Union. At the same time, the Soviet intelligence officer known as Rudolf Abel walked the other way. The two men passed each other on the open deck.

That exchange gave this crossing a nickname that has stayed ever since — the Bridge of Spies. It was not the only swap. In 1985, a larger exchange took place here, trading a group of people held in the East for several agents held in the West. In February 1986, the Soviet dissident Anatoly Shcharansky crossed to freedom at this bridge. He later became known in Israel as Natan Sharansky. Each exchange was negotiated in secret and carried out in public view.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look closely at the paint. During the division, each side looked after its own half of the bridge. For a long time the two halves were painted in slightly different shades of green. It was a small, almost comic sign of a serious divide. Even the colour of the steel depended on which system you belonged to.

The story turned again in November 1989. The Berlin Wall opened on the night of the ninth. The next evening, on the tenth, the barriers at Glienicke Bridge were lifted too. Crowds poured across from Potsdam into West Berlin. People cheered, hugged strangers, and walked over a line that had stopped them for twenty-eight years. Today a small plaque and the open road are the main reminders of that change.

Popular culture has kept the bridge alive in memory. Spy novels used it as a setting, and in 2015 the film Bridge of Spies, directed by Steven Spielberg, told the story of the 1962 exchange. Scenes were filmed here on the real bridge. So some visitors arrive knowing the film first, and the history second. That is fine. The real events were tense enough without any Hollywood help.

Look around at the setting. On the Berlin side, the parkland of Glienicke Palace spreads along the shore, with paths, trees, and classical buildings. On the Potsdam side stands Villa Schöningen, a nineteenth-century villa that now hosts an exhibition about the bridge and its divided past. Further along the water, the parks of Babelsberg rise on the hills. This is a landscape of palaces and gardens, designed for royal pleasure — and the Cold War cut straight through it.

Before you move on, walk to the middle of the deck. Stop where the border once ran. Notice how ordinary it feels. Cars pass. Cyclists ring their bells. A tour boat may slide under your feet, its passengers waving up. Look east toward Potsdam, then west toward Berlin. There is no line now, only one bridge, one river, and the soft green steel above you... as quiet as the water below.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk to the middle of the deck to stand on the old border.
Visit Villa Schöningen for an exhibition on the bridge.
Combine your visit with a walk through Glienicke Park.
Come in the early morning for quieter views over the river.
Watch Bridge of Spies beforehand to recognise the filming spots.
