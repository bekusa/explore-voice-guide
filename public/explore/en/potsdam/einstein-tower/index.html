The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Pine needles under your shoes, the creak of tall trees, birdsong, and very little traffic. You are on a wooded hill above Potsdam. Through the trunks a pale, curved shape appears. It looks less like a building and more like something poured, or shaped by hand from soft clay. This is the Einstein Tower, and it was built to check a prediction about light.

The hill is called the Telegrafenberg, the Telegraph Hill. The name comes from the nineteenth century, when an optical telegraph station stood here. It was one link in a chain of signal towers. Their wooden arms passed coded messages across Prussia, from station to station, long before electric wires. Later the hill became a home for science. Observatories and research institutes were built among the trees, and many of them still work here today.

Now look at the tower itself. There are almost no straight lines. The walls swell and curve. The windows sit in deep, rounded openings, like eyes under heavy brows. At the top, a small dome sits on the body of the tower. Below it, the base spreads out low and wide, as if the building is gripping the ground. The architect was Erich Mendelsohn, a young German architect. This tower made his name around the world.

Mendelsohn worked in a style called Expressionism. In architecture, this meant buildings that try to show feeling and movement, not only function. He made many quick sketches of this tower. They show a shape that seems to lean forward and rise up at the same time. He wanted the building to look as if it carried energy inside it. For a laboratory built to study the physics of light, that idea fits well.

So why build it at all? In 1915, Albert Einstein published his general theory of relativity. One of its predictions was strange. Light leaving a very strong gravitational field, like the field of the Sun, should shift slightly toward the red end of the spectrum. Scientists call this gravitational redshift. A young astronomer, Erwin Finlay-Freundlich, wanted to measure it. For that, he needed a special telescope built only for studying sunlight.

Here is the first surprise. The tower is not a hollow sculpture. It is a working instrument. At the very top, under the dome, mirrors follow the Sun as it moves across the sky. They send the sunlight straight down through the tower, through a vertical shaft. At the bottom, in a cool underground room, the light is split into its colours by a spectrograph. The whole building is the body of a telescope, standing upright.

This is why the base is so long and low. The underground laboratory needed a steady, even temperature. Small changes in heat could spoil delicate measurements of light. So the working rooms were set into the ground, where the earth keeps conditions calm. The curved shape above is the face the public sees. But much of the science happened below your feet, in the dark, while the sunlight came down from above.

The second surprise is about the material. Mendelsohn imagined the tower in concrete, a material that can be poured into flowing shapes. But this was Germany just after the First World War. Materials were scarce, and shaping curved concrete was difficult. In the end, much of the tower was built in brick, then covered with a smooth layer of render, a kind of plaster. So the building only looks poured. The flowing skin hides a more ordinary structure underneath.

That choice brought problems. Water got into the walls. Cracks appeared. Damp spread through the brick. The tower has needed repair again and again over its life. It was also damaged during the Second World War. Major restorations followed in later decades, including careful work in recent years. Each time, conservators tried to keep the original shape and colour. When you see the smooth, clean surface today, you are looking at the result of a long fight against water.

There is a well-known story about Einstein and this building. Locals and guidebooks often repeat it. According to the tale, Mendelsohn showed Einstein around, waiting nervously for his opinion. Einstein said nothing for a long time. Then he gave a single word of praise: organic. Nobody can say for certain how the moment really went. But the word fits. The tower does seem to grow out of the hill, not stand on it.

Einstein's connection to the tower was real but complicated. He supported the project, and the building carried his name. But he was not the astronomer working inside. And the measurement it was built for proved very hard. Sunlight is full of other effects that also shift its colours, so the tiny redshift was difficult to separate. Clear confirmation of gravitational redshift came later, through other methods. Still, the tower helped start a long tradition of solar research on this hill.

That tradition continues. The tower is still used for science today, operated by the Leibniz Institute for Astrophysics Potsdam. Around you, on the same hill, there are other research buildings. Some are about the stars. Others study the Earth, its climate, and its magnetic field. This is not a museum park. It is a place where people come to work each morning. Please treat it with the quiet you would show in any laboratory.

Before you leave, walk slowly around the tower. Watch how the curves change as you move. From one side it looks low and heavy. From another it seems to lift toward the dome. Notice how the light falls on the pale walls, and how the shadows soften in the rounded window openings. Then listen again to the trees and the birds. A century ago, someone built this strange, soft shape to catch the light of the Sun. It is still doing that work, right in front of you.
