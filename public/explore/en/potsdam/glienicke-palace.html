The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel shifts under your shoes, and somewhere beyond the trees, water laps against a shore. The light here has a soft, washed quality, because the River Havel is never far away. Pale walls glow between the dark green of old trees. A gilded lion catches the sun. For a few seconds, you could believe you are standing in a villa garden outside Rome. You are not. You are on the edge of Potsdam, at Glienicke Palace.

This is the first surprise. Although the palace belongs to the Potsdam landscape, it actually sits just across the line on the Berlin side, in the district of Wannsee. The river and the lakes here once formed a soft border between royal estates. Today the palace is part of the UNESCO World Heritage site of the palaces and parks of Potsdam and Berlin. It is one piece of a much larger design, stitched together across water and woodland.

The story of the house really begins in the 1820s. Before that, the estate passed through several owners, including Prince Karl August von Hardenberg, the Prussian state chancellor. Then, in 1824, the estate came to Prince Carl of Prussia, a son of King Frederick William the Third. Carl was young, wealthy, and in love with Italy. He had travelled south, and he came home wanting to bring the Mediterranean with him.

To do that, he turned to Karl Friedrich Schinkel, the most important Prussian architect of his age. Schinkel worked in the neoclassical style, which means he took the calm, ordered forms of ancient Greece and Rome as his model. He did not tear down the old manor house. Instead, he reshaped it. He gave it clean lines, low roofs, and a simple, almost quiet elegance. Look at the facade in front of you. Nothing shouts. Everything is in proportion.

Now turn your attention to the fountain with the golden lions. This is the Lion Fountain, and it sits on the axis of the palace like a front door made of water. The lions are modelled on a famous pair of ancient lions in Rome, at the Villa Medici. Here they gleam in gold against the green lawn. It is a small piece of theatre. It tells every visitor, before they reach the house, that this prince dreamed of the south.

The gardens around you were shaped by Peter Joseph Lenné, the great Prussian landscape gardener. Lenné did not like rigid flower beds and straight hedges. He preferred the English style, with sweeping lawns, loose groups of trees, and long views that open suddenly toward the water. As you walk, notice how the paths curve. They are designed to hide a view and then give it to you, a few steps later, as a small reward.

Here is something most visitors walk straight past. Prince Carl was a passionate collector of ancient fragments. Pieces of carved stone, old reliefs, and bits of columns from Italy and further afield were set into the walls of the buildings on the estate. Look closely at the walls in the courtyards and around the outbuildings. You may spot a carved face, a fragment of lettering, or a piece of marble that is clearly far older than the wall around it.

This habit of building old stones into new walls was more than decoration. For Carl, it was a way to live inside history. The most striking example is the Cloister Court, a small enclosed space added later in the century. It was made to look like the courtyard of a medieval Italian monastery. Its walls carry many of these collected fragments. It feels less like a museum and more like a private conversation with the past.

Down by the water stands another building by Schinkel, known as the Casino. In this case, the word does not mean a place for gambling. It comes from the Italian for a small house, a garden pavilion for leisure. The Casino has long pergolas, wooden frames for climbing plants, stretching out on either side. From here, the prince and his guests could look across the Havel. It is one of the calmest spots on the whole estate.

On the edge of the park, near the main road, you will find two round, open pavilions. They carry delightful names: the Great Curiosity and the Little Curiosity. They were built as viewing points. From them, people could watch the traffic on the road and the boats on the river. The name says it all. These were places for looking, for watching the world go by, a royal version of sitting at a café window.

That road leads to the Glienicke Bridge, just a short walk away. During the Cold War, this bridge stood on the border between West Berlin and East Germany. It became known around the world as the Bridge of Spies, because East and West exchanged captured agents here. So the quiet park around you sits beside one of the tensest crossings of the twentieth century. Romantic Italy on one side, the Iron Curtain on the other.

Today the palace interior is open as a museum, and it also houses an exhibition about the court gardeners. These were the skilled men who planted and cared for the royal gardens of Prussia. It is a fitting subject for this house. The park is not wild nature. Every tree you see was chosen, placed, and tended. The gardeners were the quiet authors of the landscape you are walking through right now.

Before you leave, stand still for one last moment. Look from the pale walls of the palace to the gold of the lions, and then out toward the trees and the water. A young prince once looked at this same view and imagined Rome. Listen again for the gravel and the river. As you walk on, notice the old stones in the walls, the curve of the paths, and the way the light keeps changing over the Havel.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk down to the Casino for calm views across the Havel.
Combine your visit with a stroll to Glienicke Bridge.
Wear comfortable shoes for the long gravel park paths.
Visit the court gardeners exhibition inside the palace.
Come in the morning for softer light and quieter paths.
