The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel shifts under your shoes, and somewhere below the slope, water laps against a reed-lined shore. Through the trees, pale towers rise, with crenellated tops like a castle from an English novel. You are in Potsdam, in eastern Germany, yet this building looks as if it came from the Thames valley. That surprise is the whole point. Babelsberg Palace was built to feel like somewhere else, and like someone else's idea of romance.

The palace was a summer home for Prince William of Prussia and his wife, Augusta. At the time, William was a younger son. Nobody expected him to become king. Much later, he became King of Prussia and then the first German Emperor. So the man who spent his summers here would one day stand at the centre of a new German state. In the 1830s, though, he simply wanted a quiet retreat by the water.

The first designs came from Karl Friedrich Schinkel, the leading Prussian architect of his day. Schinkel shaped much of Berlin and Potsdam, usually in a calm, classical style of columns and clean lines. Here he worked in a very different language. The style is Gothic Revival, a nineteenth-century fashion that copied the pointed arches and battlements of medieval buildings. Construction began in the 1830s. After Schinkel died, other architects, including Ludwig Persius, continued and expanded the work.

Why English Gothic? Augusta had strong opinions, and she admired English country houses and castles. Windsor Castle is often named as one inspiration. Look at the towers, the octagonal shapes, the tall windows grouped under pointed frames. The building does not sit as one neat block. Instead, it steps and turns, with wings and turrets added over the years. From different angles it looks almost like a small town of towers, each with its own outline against the sky.

Now turn your attention to the setting, because the landscape matters as much as the stone. The palace stands on a hillside above the Havel, a river that widens here into a lake. The first plans for the park came from Peter Joseph Lenné, the great garden designer of Prussia. Later, Prince Hermann von Pückler-Muskau took over. He was a famous and eccentric landscape artist, and he gave the park much of its present shape.

Pückler's idea was the English landscape garden. This style avoids straight lines and clipped hedges. Instead, paths curve, lawns roll down to the water, and trees frame carefully chosen views. Every few steps, something new appears — a glimpse of the lake, a distant tower, a church across the water. None of this is accidental. The views were planned like paintings. As you walk, try stopping wherever a gap opens in the trees. Often, you are standing exactly where a designer wanted you to stand.

Here is the first surprise most visitors miss. This park is tied to one of the turning points of German history. In 1862, King William faced a crisis with the Prussian parliament over the army budget. He even considered giving up the throne. According to the well-known account, he met Otto von Bismarck here at Babelsberg. After that conversation, the king appointed Bismarck as his chief minister. Bismarck would go on to lead the unification of Germany.

So picture these quiet paths in a different mood. A worried king, an ambitious politician, and a decision that would reshape Europe. Historians still discuss exactly what was said. But the meeting itself is a firm part of the record. It is strange to think that a garden built for summer leisure became the backdrop for such a serious moment. The lawns and the lake look the same. The weight of the place is entirely in what you know.

The park holds more than the palace. On higher ground stands the Flatow Tower, a tall brick tower in the same medieval spirit. Elsewhere you may find an unusual structure called the Gerichtslaube. It is a medieval court arcade that once belonged to Berlin's old town hall. When that building was replaced in the nineteenth century, the arcade was taken apart and rebuilt here. So a real piece of medieval Berlin now stands among imitations of the Middle Ages.

Down by the water, look for a pumping station built in a playful, Moorish-inspired style. Machines inside it once raised water from the river to feed fountains and irrigate the gardens. It is a reminder that this romantic landscape depended on new technology. The nineteenth century loved this mix — medieval dreams on the outside, steam power hidden within. The fantasy of the past was kept alive by the engineering of the present.

The second surprise belongs to the twentieth century. After the Second World War, Potsdam lay in East Germany. When the Berlin Wall went up, the border ran along this shoreline, because West Berlin sat just across the water. Parts of the park became a guarded border zone. Paths were cut off, and the gardens suffered for decades. Not far away, the Glienicke Bridge became famous during the Cold War as a place where East and West exchanged spies.

After German reunification, the long work of repair began. The palace and park are now part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site that covers the palaces and parks of Potsdam and Berlin. Restoration has been slow and careful, and the palace has spent long periods closed while experts work on it. If the interior is open when you visit, take the chance. If not, the exterior and the gardens still tell much of the story.

Before you leave, pause where you can see both the palace and the water together. Notice how the pale walls take on the colour of the light — warmer in the evening, cooler under cloud. Listen again for the gravel, the birds, the soft movement of the lake. A prince built this place to escape. A king made a fateful choice here. A border later cut across it. And now, it is simply quiet again, waiting for your next step.
