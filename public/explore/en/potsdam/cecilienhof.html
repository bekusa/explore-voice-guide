The guide

The story

Listen first to the gravel under your feet, and to the quiet of the trees. Then look up at the roofline. Dark timber beams cross pale plaster. Red brick fills the gaps. Above it all rise chimneys, many of them, each one shaped a little differently. At first glance you might think you have wandered into the English countryside. You have not. You are in Potsdam, on the edge of the New Garden, and this is Cecilienhof.

The house looks gentle, almost cosy. That is part of its surprise. In the summer of 1945, the leaders of the three most powerful nations on Earth sat inside these walls. Together they decided much of what Europe would look like for the next half century. A country manor built for a royal family became the stage for the end of the Second World War in Europe. Keep that contrast in mind as we walk.

Let us begin with the family. Cecilienhof was built during the years of the First World War, from 1913 to 1917. It was made for Crown Prince Wilhelm, the eldest son of Emperor Wilhelm the Second, and for his wife, Crown Princess Cecilie. She came from the ducal house of Mecklenburg-Schwerin, a small state in northern Germany. The palace carries her name. It was the last palace the Hohenzollern dynasty ever built. Within months of its completion, the monarchy itself was gone.

The architect was Paul Schultze-Naumburg. He chose the style of an English Tudor country house. Tudor refers to the English royal era of the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, with its half-timbered walls and tall clustered chimneys. Why English, in the middle of a war against Britain? The style was fashionable among wealthy Germans before the war. The family also had deep ties to the British royal house. Emperor Wilhelm the Second was a grandson of Queen Victoria.

Walk around the outside and notice how the building spreads out rather than up. It has no grand central block and no sweeping staircase facing the park. Instead, the wings fold around several inner courtyards. The palace has well over one hundred rooms, but it never feels like a single monument. It feels like a cluster of houses. This was deliberate. It was meant to feel like a family home, not a seat of power.

Now look again at those chimneys. Count them if you like... it will take a while. Many are purely decorative and were never connected to any fireplace. They are there to shape the skyline and to give the roof its picturesque rhythm. Some are twisted, some are patterned in brick. This is one of the details most visitors walk past without a second thought, and it is worth a slow look.

After the end of the monarchy in 1918, the crown prince's family was allowed to keep living here. They stayed through the 1920s and 1930s, and through most of the Second World War. Only in early 1945, as the Red Army approached from the east, did Cecilie leave. She took little with her. Many of the family's belongings remained behind. Within a few months, the empty house would receive very different guests.

Berlin, just to the northeast, lay in ruins. The Allies needed a place to meet that was intact, secure, and close to the defeated capital. Potsdam offered it. From the seventeenth of July to the second of August, 1945, Cecilienhof hosted what history calls the Potsdam Conference. The three delegations came from the United States, the Soviet Union, and Great Britain. The Soviet forces, who controlled this area, prepared the building for the meeting.

The men at the table are famous. Harry Truman had become American president only that April, after the death of Franklin Roosevelt. Joseph Stalin led the Soviet Union. Winston Churchill arrived for Britain. Then came a second surprise. Midway through the conference, the results of the British general election were announced. Churchill had lost. He flew home, and the new prime minister, Clement Attlee, returned to Potsdam in his place. The conference simply continued with a new face.

What did they decide here? Germany would be divided into zones of occupation. The country would be disarmed. The Nazi party and its structures would be dismantled. Germany's eastern border would move west, to the line of the Oder and Neisse rivers, with huge consequences for millions of people living there. During these same weeks, a declaration was issued calling on Japan to surrender. The shape of the Cold War, in many ways, began to form in these rooms.

If you go inside, the conference rooms are the heart of the visit. The great hall still holds a large round table, where the delegations sat facing one another. Each delegation also had its own working rooms and its own entrance, so the leaders did not have to walk through each other's spaces. Stand in the hall and notice how modest it is. Wood panelling, a high window, a staircase. It is a family room, not a palace throne room.

Before you leave, step into the main courtyard. Here you will find a red star, planted in flowers. The Soviet hosts created it for the conference, and it has been replanted ever since. It is a striking thing to see. A symbol of Soviet power, blooming in red, in front of a German royal house built in an English style. Few places hold so many layers in one view.

The story did not end in 1945. For decades, this was East German territory. The nearby lake shore became part of the border zone between East and West, and the Berlin Wall ran along the water not far from here. Today, the palace and the New Garden belong to the UNESCO World Heritage site of the palaces and parks of Potsdam and Berlin. The building is cared for as a museum, and part of it has long served as a hotel.

As you walk back through the park, turn once more toward the house. Look at the chimneys against the sky, the dark beams, the quiet windows. It is easy to imagine a family having tea here. It is harder to imagine the cars, the guards, the interpreters, and the decisions of that summer. Both stories belong to these walls. Let the gravel crunch under your feet again, and take the quiet of the trees with you.

What to look for

Before you go

Practical tips

Book the interior tour separately to see the conference rooms.
Combine your visit with a walk through the New Garden.
Walk the lakeshore nearby to see the former border zone.
Arrive early to enjoy the courtyards before tour groups gather.
Wear comfortable shoes for the gravel paths and park lawns.
