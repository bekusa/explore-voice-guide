The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your shoes. It crunches softly with every step, a pale, steady sound that has followed visitors along these paths for nearly three centuries. Above you, the trees open onto a long green slope. At the top, a low yellow palace sits on its hill like a summer house that never quite grew up. The air here smells of cut grass, damp stone and, in the warmer months, the faint sweetness of the vines on the terraces. This is Sanssouci Park in Potsdam.

The name is French, and it means "without worries." That choice tells you a lot about the man who made this place. He was Frederick the Second of Prussia, remembered as Frederick the Great. He spoke and wrote in French by preference, and he wanted a private retreat away from the formality of court life in Berlin. In the 1740s, early in his reign, he began to shape this hillside into his personal refuge.

The palace at the top was built between 1745 and 1747. The architect was Georg Wenzeslaus von Knobelsdorff, but the king himself made sketches and pushed hard for his own ideas. The result is surprisingly modest. The palace has only one storey. It holds a small number of rooms, enough for the king and a few chosen guests. Frederick did not want a grand monument here. He wanted somewhere to read, write, play the flute and talk late into the night.

Now look at the slope beneath the palace. Those broad steps of green are terraces, cut into the hill for growing grapes and fruit. Glass-fronted niches line the walls, and they once sheltered figs and vines from the cold Prussian winds. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. This famous royal garden began as a working vineyard. The palace was placed on top almost as a crown for the orchard below.

When you reach the palace, look up at the facade. Carved figures support the roofline, laughing and leaning, with grapes in their hair. They are followers of Bacchus, the Roman god of wine. They fit the vineyard below them. Then find the golden lettering across the front. It reads "Sans, Souci" — and there is a comma between the two words. Nobody knows for certain why. Scholars and guides have argued about it for generations, and the small mark is still a quiet puzzle in gilded letters.

Just to the side of the upper terrace, you will find a plain stone slab set into the ground. This is the grave of Frederick the Great. He asked to be buried here, close to the graves of his beloved greyhounds. His wish was ignored at his death in 1786, and he was laid to rest elsewhere in Potsdam. Only in 1991, after the reunification of Germany, was he finally brought back to this terrace, more than two hundred years later.

Look closely at the slab, and you will probably see potatoes lying on it. This is not litter. Frederick promoted the potato as a crop for ordinary Prussians, at a time when many farmers distrusted it. Visitors now leave potatoes on his grave as a small, humorous tribute. Locals also tell a popular tale that he had potato fields guarded so peasants would think the crop was valuable and steal it. That story is legend, not documented history — but it is a good one.

From the foot of the terraces, a long, straight avenue runs west through the park for more than two kilometres. It ties the whole landscape together. Along the way, you pass statues, fountains, lawns and dense woodland. The park is far larger than most visitors expect. Many people see the palace, take a photograph and leave. If you walk on, you will find that the rest of the grounds are often much quieter.

At the foot of the vineyard is the Great Fountain. Here is the second surprise. In Frederick's own lifetime, it barely worked. His engineers could not bring enough water up to the park, and the fountains stayed mostly dry. The problem was solved only in the 1840s, under a later king, with a steam-powered pumping station on the river Havel. That pumping station was disguised as a mosque, with a minaret for its chimney. You can still see it in Potsdam today.

At the far western end of the main avenue stands the New Palace. Frederick built it after the Seven Years' War, a long European conflict that left Prussia exhausted but still standing. This palace is the opposite of Sanssouci. It is huge, red and cream, and crowded with statues along its roofline. Frederick himself reportedly called it a show of strength. He wanted to prove that Prussia could still afford to build on a grand scale.

Between these two palaces, the park holds many smaller surprises. The Chinese House is a gilded garden pavilion. Its golden figures sit drinking tea and playing music, and they show how eighteenth-century Europeans imagined China. Nearby, the Picture Gallery was built to display the king's paintings. In the nineteenth century, later kings added an Orangery Palace in an Italian style, and in the south the small Charlottenhof Palace and the Roman Baths, both shaped by the architect Karl Friedrich Schinkel.

All of this has been protected since 1990 as part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, together with other palaces and parks in Potsdam and Berlin. Near the main palace, you will also see a historic windmill. Legend has it that Frederick wanted the mill removed, and the miller threatened to take the king to court. In the popular tale, the king backed down and respected the law. Historians treat this story with caution, but it remains a favourite in Potsdam.

Before you leave, stop at the top of the terraces and turn around. Look down over the vines, the steps and the fountain, toward the trees beyond. This is the view Frederick chose for his retreat from the world. Listen again for the gravel, the birds and the water. Then, as you walk away, notice how quickly the paths fill with quiet — the same quiet the king came here to find.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the main avenue west to escape the crowds.
Visit the palace interior with a separate timed ticket.
Wear comfortable shoes for long gravel paths.
Bring a potato if you want to join the grave tradition.
Come early morning for quieter terraces and softer light.
