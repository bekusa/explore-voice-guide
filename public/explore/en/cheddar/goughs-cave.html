The guide

The story

Cool air meets you first. Even on a warm Somerset afternoon, the temperature drops as you walk under the rock. The air feels damp and clean, with a mineral smell, like wet stone after rain. Underfoot the path is smooth and a little slick. Somewhere in the dark, water drips at its own slow pace, a single note repeated again and again. You are walking into the limestone of the Mendip Hills, beneath Cheddar Gorge.

It helps to picture what is above you. Cheddar Gorge is a deep cut through grey limestone, with cliffs rising on both sides of a winding road. It is often described as the largest gorge in Britain. Limestone dissolves slowly in water that carries a little acid from soil and air. Over a very long time, water ran through cracks in this rock, widened them, and left behind the passages you are standing in now.

Gough's Cave takes its name from Richard Cox Gough, a local man who explored these passages in the late nineteenth century. Gough cleared away rubble, opened up new chambers, and saw what visitors would pay to see. He turned the cave into a show cave, with paths and lighting, at a time when Victorian travellers were curious about geology and the deep past. His family ran it for years, and the name has stayed ever since.

Look at the walls as you move forward. The colours are not just grey. You will see cream, rust orange, soft pink and dark streaks where minerals have stained the stone. Iron gives the warmer tones. The shapes come from calcite, a mineral that water leaves behind, drop by drop. Each drip carries a tiny amount. Over thousands of years, those tiny amounts become curtains, columns and frozen waterfalls of stone.

The early guides gave the larger chambers grand names. One formation is called Saint Paul's, after the cathedral in London. Another space is known as Solomon's Temple. These names tell you something about the people who first showed the cave. They wanted visitors to feel the same awe they felt inside a great church. Stand still for a moment in one of these chambers, and the comparison makes sense. The ceiling rises, the sound changes, and voices grow quieter without anyone asking.

Now for the first surprise. In 1903, workers digging in the cave found a human skeleton. He became known as Cheddar Man. Scientists date him to around ten thousand years ago, soon after the end of the last Ice Age. He is often described as the oldest almost complete human skeleton found in Britain. The original remains are now cared for by the Natural History Museum in London, but his story starts right here, in this rock.

Cheddar Man made headlines again in 2018. Researchers studied ancient DNA from his bones. The results suggested he probably had dark skin and blue or pale eyes. That combination surprised many people. It showed that the first people to resettle Britain after the Ice Age looked different from what many had imagined. A man who lived in this gorge ten thousand years ago changed how a whole country pictured its ancestors.

The second surprise is darker. Excavations in Gough's Cave found human bones far older than Cheddar Man, from late in the Ice Age. Some of these bones carry cut marks. Archaeologists studying them concluded that people here had processed human remains, and that some skulls had been carefully shaped into cups. Researchers still discuss what this meant. It may have been part of funeral customs, not simply hunger. Either way, it is among the most unusual finds from prehistoric Britain.

Why did people come here at all? Think of the gorge as a natural corridor. Animals moved through it between the uplands and the lower land. The cave mouth gave shelter from wind and rain. Water was close by. For hunters at the edge of the Ice Age, this was a practical place to stop. Stone tools and animal bones found in the cave show that people returned here over long periods.

Here is a detail many visitors walk past without noticing. The cave keeps a steady cool temperature through the whole year. It does not get hot in summer or freeze in winter. That stability has a very local use. Cheddar cheese, the famous English cheese, takes its name from this village. Today, some cheddar is still matured inside Gough's Cave, where the cool, damp air helps the flavour develop slowly.

Listen again to the water. Rain that falls on the Mendip Hills sinks into the limestone and travels underground. Some of it flows through hidden channels below the gorge before it comes out near the village. The cave is not finished. It is still being shaped, very slowly. The drip you can hear may be adding a thin layer of calcite to a formation that will look a little different in another thousand years.

As you walk, notice the old and the new side by side. The paths and lights are modern, but they follow routes cut and cleared by Gough and his workers. The formations above them took many thousands of years to grow. Try not to touch them. The oils on human skin can stop calcite from forming, and a mark from one careless hand can last for centuries. Many formations here were damaged by early visitors who broke off souvenirs.

When you leave, the change will be sudden. Warm air, birdsong, and the bright green and grey of the gorge will return all at once. Take a moment at the entrance and look up at the cliffs. Somewhere in that rock, water is still finding its way down. And people have stood at this same opening for thousands of years, stepping out of the cool dark into the daylight, just as you are about to do.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a light jacket for the cool underground air.
Wear shoes with good grip for damp paths.
Avoid touching formations to protect growing calcite.
Walk the gorge clifftop path after your cave visit.
Taste locally made cheddar in the village afterwards.
