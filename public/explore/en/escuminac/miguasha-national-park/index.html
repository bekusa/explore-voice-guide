The guide

The story

Listen first. Below the cliffs, small waves turn over grey pebbles with a dry, rattling sound. The air smells of salt and wet clay. Above you, a wall of layered rock rises in grey and greenish bands, stacked like the pages of a closed book. That comparison is more accurate than it sounds, because these cliffs hold one of the most complete records of ancient fish life ever found on Earth.

You are standing on the southern edge of the Gaspé Peninsula in Quebec, Canada. The water in front of you is the estuary where the Restigouche River meets the Baie des Chaleurs. Across the water, on a clear day, you can see the low green hills of New Brunswick. It is a quiet, rural coastline of farms and small villages. Few people passing by would guess that the crumbling bank at their feet changed how scientists understand our own origins.

The rocks here belong to a layer that geologists call the Escuminac Formation. They formed during the Late Devonian period, roughly 380 million years ago. The Devonian is often called the Age of Fishes. At that time, almost all complex animal life lived in water. The land was only beginning to turn green, with early plants spreading along the shores. No dinosaurs had appeared yet. Even the first insects were still far in the future.

This place looked very different then. The continents sat in other positions, and this coast lay much closer to the equator. Scientists believe the area was a warm estuary, a place where a river met the sea. Fine mud settled slowly on the bottom. When fish died, the mud covered them quickly and gently. Over millions of years, that mud hardened into the shale and sandstone you see in the cliff today.

That gentle burial is the secret of Miguasha. In most fossil sites, bodies are broken, scattered, or eaten before they can be preserved. Here, many fish were buried whole. Researchers have recovered specimens with their scales, fins, and even fine internal structures still in place. Some fossils show soft tissue details that almost never survive. For a palaeontologist, a scientist who studies ancient life, that level of detail is extremely rare.

People noticed the fossils here in the nineteenth century. Geologists described the site in the 1840s, and word spread quickly through scientific circles. Collectors came and carried specimens away to museums in Europe and North America. For many decades, some of the best fish from these cliffs sat in display cases far from Quebec. In recent years, a number of important specimens have returned home to the collection here.

One fish became the unofficial star of this coast. Its name is Eusthenopteron, and locals sometimes call it the Prince of Miguasha. It was a lobe-finned fish, which means its fins grew from fleshy, muscular bases rather than thin rays. Inside those fins, scientists found bones arranged in a pattern that looks surprisingly familiar. There is one upper bone, then two lower bones. That is the same basic plan as your own upper arm and forearm.

Here is the first surprise that many visitors miss. In 2010, researchers found a complete specimen of another fish here, called Elpistostege. The fossil was about one and a half metres long, and it was whole from nose to tail. When scientists published their study in 2020, they described something remarkable in its fin. Inside, they saw small bones arranged in rows, very similar to the bones of fingers.

That discovery matters because it sits close to one of the great turning points in the history of life. It is the move from water to land. Every animal with four limbs, including frogs, lizards, birds, and humans, comes from fish like these. So when you look at the fins in these fossils, you are looking at a very early version of a structure you are using right now to hold your phone.

The fish are not the only story in these rocks. The cliffs also preserve early plants, small invertebrates, and traces of life along the ancient shore. Among the plants is Archaeopteris, one of the earliest trees that resembled a modern tree, with a woody trunk and leafy branches. Together, these fossils let scientists rebuild a whole ecosystem, not just a single animal. That is part of why UNESCO named Miguasha a World Heritage Site in 1999.

The second surprise is in the cliff itself. It is not a fixed monument. Every winter, frost and waves loosen the rock, and pieces fall to the beach below. That erosion is a kind of slow excavation. Each year, new fossils come to the surface. Scientists and park staff watch the cliff closely, because the next important specimen could appear after a single storm.

Because of this, the rules here are firm. Visitors may not dig, chip, or take fossils from the cliff or the beach. If you see something that looks like a fossil, the best response is to leave it in place and tell a staff member. The park museum shows specimens up close, along with models and explanations of how these animals lived. It is worth spending time there before or after your walk along the shore.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look at the layers again. Each thin band of grey rock was once a quiet layer of mud on the floor of a warm estuary. Notice the wind, the gulls, the green hills across the water. Then look down at your own hand, and think of those small bones inside an ancient fin... a very old story, still slowly coming out of the cliff.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the park museum before walking down to the shore.
Wear sturdy shoes for stairs and the pebble beach.
Check the tide before walking along the cliff base.
Report possible fossils to staff instead of touching them.
Keep a safe distance from the eroding cliff face.
