The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your feet as you cross toward the low, pale walls. The land here is flat, and the sky over Mantua feels wide. The air off the surrounding lakes can be soft and damp, even on a bright day. Ahead of you stands a building that looks calm, almost modest, from the outside. Do not trust that first impression. Palazzo del Te was designed to play games with your eyes, and it starts doing so the moment you step into the courtyard.

This was never meant to be a working palace. It was a pleasure villa, built on the edge of the city for Federico the Second Gonzaga, the ruler of Mantua, in the early sixteenth century. The Gonzaga family already had an enormous palace in the centre of town. Federico wanted somewhere else, a place for horses, banquets, guests and private life. Many accounts say he also wanted a retreat where he could spend time with his mistress, Isabella Boschetti, away from the formal court.

The name itself comes from the land. In those days this ground was an island in the marshes around Mantua, known locally by a name often shortened to Te. Historians are not fully agreed on its original meaning. One common explanation links it to a local word for a small hut or shelter. Either way, the name has nothing to do with drinking tea. Before the palace rose, Federico kept stables here, because the Gonzaga were famous across Europe for breeding fine horses.

The man behind almost everything you will see is Giulio Romano. He trained in Rome under Raphael, the great painter of the High Renaissance, and worked in his busy workshop. After Raphael died, Giulio came to Mantua and became the court's leading artist. Here he was architect, painter and designer all at once. He worked with a large team of assistants, and over roughly a decade they turned a stable island into one of the most daring buildings of its age.

Now, look closely at the walls of the main courtyard. The stone blocks are rough and heavy, cut to look rustic and strong. Above the columns runs a band of decoration from classical architecture. Every so often, one of its small upright blocks seems to have slipped downward, as if the building were coming apart. It is deliberate. Giulio knew the classical rules perfectly, and he broke them on purpose, as a joke for educated guests who would notice.

This taste for tension and surprise has a name: Mannerism. It is the style that followed the balanced calm of the High Renaissance. Mannerist artists stretched figures, unsettled space and liked to make the viewer slightly uneasy. Palazzo del Te is often described as one of the clearest examples of it in architecture. So when something here feels a little wrong, a little off balance, you are probably reading the building exactly as Giulio intended.

Inside, the rooms run one after another around the courtyard. One of the most memorable is the Hall of Horses. Along its walls stand painted portraits of Gonzaga horses, shown at life size, each one proud and individual. They stand between painted columns and false niches, so the animals seem to step out of the architecture. For a family that built its reputation partly on breeding, these were not decorations. They were trophies, as important as any portrait of a duke.

Next comes the Hall of Cupid and Psyche, and the mood changes completely. This room tells the ancient love story of Psyche, a mortal woman, and Cupid, the god of love. The ceiling is full of episodes from her trials. Around the walls, gods and nymphs feast, bathe and embrace. The scenes are sensual and frank, which suits a room built for private pleasure. Take your time here. The details of food, wine and bodies are full of humour as well as beauty.

In 1530, the Holy Roman Emperor Charles the Fifth came to Mantua. During that visit he raised Federico from marquis to duke, a great step up for the family. The emperor was entertained at this villa, and he returned a few years later. Parts of the decoration celebrate imperial power and loyalty. So this pleasure house also had a serious political role. It showed the most powerful man in Europe that the Gonzaga had taste, wealth and ambition to match.

And then there is the room most visitors remember long after they leave: the Chamber of the Giants. Walk in, and the painting does not stop at the walls. It covers every surface, from the floor line up to the domed ceiling. High above, Jupiter, king of the gods, hurls thunderbolts down from the heavens. Around you, huge giants who dared to rebel against him are crushed by falling columns, rocks and collapsing buildings. There is no frame and no safe distance.

The effect is meant to overwhelm. The corners of the room are rounded, so your eye cannot find a firm edge. The walls seem to be falling toward you. Many visitors notice that the room also changes sound, and voices can carry and echo strangely. Some writers have suggested a fire in the hearth would once have made the scene flicker. Whatever the details, Giulio built a total experience here, centuries before anyone used the word immersive.

Here is a surprise that most people miss. Giulio Romano is the only Renaissance artist named by William Shakespeare. In the play The Winter's Tale, a statue is praised as the work of that rare Italian master, Julio Romano. Nobody knows exactly how the playwright heard of him. But it shows how far his reputation travelled. An artist working in a marsh town in northern Italy had become a byword for skill, even on a London stage.

Before you leave, walk out to the garden side. Behind the palace, the grounds open toward a curved structure at the far end, closing the view. Small spaces, including a secret garden and a little grotto, sit tucked away near the edges. Today the palace is a museum, and it also hosts exhibitions. But the bones of the villa are still easy to read: courtyard, rooms of pleasure, then the garden, all arranged for strolling guests.

As you walk back across the gravel, turn once and look at the courtyard walls again. Those slipping stones, those rough blocks, those quiet proportions. Now you know what they are hiding. Somewhere behind them, giants are still falling, horses still stand at attention, and a goddess still feasts on a painted ceiling. Notice the flat light over the lakes, and the city waiting beyond. Five centuries on, the joke is still working, and you are now one of the guests who gets it.
