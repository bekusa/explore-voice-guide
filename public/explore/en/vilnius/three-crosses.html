The guide

The story

Start with the climb. The path rises through old trees, and the ground under your shoes changes from packed earth to worn steps. The air feels cooler under the branches. Then the trees open, the wind finds you, and three white crosses stand against the sky. Below them, the red roofs and church towers of Vilnius spread out like a map. This is the Hill of Three Crosses, one of the clearest viewpoints over the Lithuanian capital.

Take a moment before the history. Turn slowly and look for the landmarks. To one side, the brick tower of Gediminas Castle rises on its own hill across the valley. Below, the old town fills the low ground with domes, spires, and tiled roofs. The small Vilnia River curves through the green at the foot of the slope. On a clear day, the newer towers of the city stand far off on the other bank of the larger Neris River.

The hill sits inside Kalnai Park, a wooded area of steep slopes just east of the old town. Its name in Lithuanian means simply the Hill of Three Crosses. For a long time, people knew it by another name — the Bald Hill. That older name probably came from the bare summit, which stood out above the forested ridges around it. Today the trees reach close to the top, but the crown of the hill stays open to the sky.

Now, the story that gives this place its meaning. It is a legend, and locals tell it with feeling. According to the tale, in the fourteenth century, when Lithuania was still a pagan land, a group of Franciscan friars came to preach in Vilnius. Some of them were killed for their faith. Legend says several were thrown into the river, and three were put to death on crosses on this hill. Historians debate the details. The story itself has lasted for centuries.

Whatever happened here, the memory took a physical form. Wooden crosses stood on this hilltop for a very long time, at least from around the seventeenth century. Wood does not last forever in a Baltic climate. Rain, snow, and frost wore the crosses down, and people replaced them again and again. Each new set carried the same idea forward. For the city below, three crosses on the hill became a familiar sight on the skyline.

In 1916, the wooden crosses gave way to something more permanent. The sculptor and architect Antanas Vivulskis designed a monument in reinforced concrete. He chose a clean, simple form — three tall white crosses, joined at the base and visible from across the city. It was a modern design for its time. And here is a detail many visitors miss: this was built during the First World War, while the city was under German occupation.

Then came a darker chapter. After the Second World War, Lithuania was part of the Soviet Union. The Soviet state was openly hostile to religious symbols, especially ones as visible as this. In 1950, the authorities blew up the three crosses. The hilltop stood empty for almost four decades. For many people in Vilnius, the empty summit carried its own silent message. Everyone knew what had stood there, even if no one could say so in public.

Look down the slope near the monument, and you may notice something unusual. Large broken pieces of concrete lie half-buried in the grass. These are the remains of the crosses destroyed in 1950. They were left here on purpose, as a memorial. Their rough edges and exposed metal stand in sharp contrast to the smooth white crosses above. It is one of the quietest and most powerful details on the hill.

The crosses returned in 1989. This was the time of Lithuania's national revival, when people across the country were pushing for freedom from Soviet rule. The sculptor Henrikas Šilgalis designed the new monument, based on the earlier form by Vivulskis. Large crowds gathered for the unveiling. For many Lithuanians, the return of the crosses was a sign that change was really coming. Within a year, Lithuania declared its independence restored.

So the white shapes in front of you carry several layers at once. There is the old legend of the friars. There is the long tradition of wooden crosses, replaced by hand over generations. There is the concrete monument of 1916, destroyed by force. And there is the rebuilding at the end of the Soviet era. The crosses you see today are a reconstruction, but the story behind them is much older than the concrete.

There is one more layer, and this one is uncertain. Medieval Vilnius had several castles. One of them, often called the Crooked Castle, was made of wood and was destroyed during wars in the late fourteenth century. Its exact location is still debated. Some researchers believe it stood on this hill, while others place it elsewhere in the hills nearby. No one can point to a wall and say, with certainty, here it was. The question is still open.

Today the hill has a gentler daily life. Runners and dog walkers come up the paths in the morning. In the evening, couples and friends sit on the slopes to watch the light change over the roofs. In summer, the grass fills with people. In winter, snow covers the steps and the crosses almost disappear against a white sky. The views at sunset are especially loved, when the old town turns gold and then blue.

As you stand here, notice the sounds. Church bells sometimes carry up from the old town. Traffic hums faintly far below. The wind moves through the trees on the slope. From up here, the city feels close and far away at the same time. Many visitors combine this hill with Gediminas Castle across the valley, or with the small riverside district of Užupis, just a short walk away.

Before you go down, look once more at the three white crosses, then at the broken concrete in the grass beside them. One set was destroyed. Another set rose again in its place. Then turn toward the city and pick out the tower, the river, and the roofs where you will walk next. As you follow the path back under the trees, the crosses will drop out of sight behind you. But from the streets below, look up — you will find them again on the skyline.
