The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the paving stones of Cathedral Square and listen. Behind you, the bell tower marks the hour. In front of you rise pale walls, tall windows, and steep tiled roofs. The building looks centuries old. But the stones under your feet cover a stranger story. For nearly two hundred years, there was almost nothing here at all.

This is the Palace of the Grand Dukes of Lithuania. It sits in the area that historians call the Lower Castle. Look up and to the right, and you will see the Upper Castle. That is the red-brick Gediminas Tower on its wooded hill. Together, the two castles once formed the political heart of a very large medieval state. From here, rulers governed lands that reached far to the east and south.

It helps to know how large that state was. In the late Middle Ages, the Grand Duchy of Lithuania was one of the largest countries in Europe. Its territory included much of what is now Belarus and Ukraine. Vilnius was its capital. The rivers here mattered too. The Neris runs just beyond the trees, and the smaller Vilnia joins it nearby. Water and high ground made this a natural place for a fortress.

The first buildings on this site were defensive. Over time, a fortified castle became a residence. The great change came in the sixteenth century. Two rulers stand out — Sigismund the Old and his son, Sigismund Augustus. They were kings of Poland as well as Grand Dukes of Lithuania. Under them, the palace was rebuilt in the Renaissance style. That is the style of balanced proportions and classical ornament that spread north from Italy.

That Italian connection was personal. Sigismund the Old married Bona Sforza, a princess from a powerful Italian family. She brought Italian tastes, Italian craftsmen, and Italian ideas about court life. Historians often link her influence to the new elegance of the royal residences. So when you see the arcades and the ordered windows here, you are looking at an Italian idea, adapted to a northern climate with cold winters and heavy snow.

Now for one of the best-known stories connected to these walls. Sigismund Augustus fell in love with Barbara Radziwiłł, a widow from a powerful Lithuanian noble family. They married in secret. The marriage caused a political scandal, and many nobles, and the king's own mother, opposed it. He defended the marriage, and Barbara was crowned queen. But she died very soon after. Her story still appears in Lithuanian and Polish plays, paintings, and novels.

Under Sigismund Augustus, the court here was a real centre of culture. Music, books, and art came to Vilnius from across Europe. The king collected a large library. Ambassadors arrived, and feasts filled the halls. For a time, this palace was one of the places where Renaissance Europe met the forests and rivers of the Baltic lands. That is a surprise for many visitors, who imagine this corner of Europe as a distant edge.

Then came decline. In the mid-seventeenth century, war reached Vilnius, and the city was occupied and badly damaged. The palace suffered heavily and never fully recovered. The rulers now spent most of their time elsewhere. The building stood half-empty, and its condition grew worse. At the very start of the nineteenth century, when Vilnius was part of the Russian Empire, the authorities had the palace demolished. Only fragments of its foundations stayed underground.

Here is the second surprise. What you see today is a reconstruction of the twenty-first century. Archaeologists excavated this site for many years and found foundations, pottery, coins, and many decorated stove tiles. Builders then raised a new palace on the old footprint, using historical drawings and these finds as a guide. The palace opened to the public in 2013. Some critics argued against rebuilding something that no longer existed. Supporters saw it as a return of national memory.

That debate is worth keeping in mind as you walk inside. The museum does something clever. In its lower levels, you can see the original excavated walls, separate from the new construction. You can see old brick and rough stone beside clean modern surfaces. In this way, the building shows you the difference between what survived and what was rebuilt. You do not have to choose between them. You can look at both, side by side.

Upstairs, the mood changes. The reconstructed ceremonial halls give an impression of court life — high ceilings, carved wood, painted decoration, and furniture of the period. Pay special attention to the stove tiles in the collections. They carry coats of arms, portraits, and plant patterns in green, brown, and blue glaze. In a cold northern palace, a great tiled stove was a source of heat and also a display of wealth.

When you step back outside, look at how the palace connects to everything around it. The white cathedral stands just beside it, with its row of columns. The bell tower rises on its own nearby. Above, the path climbs to the tower on the hill. These buildings belong to the same story of power, faith, and defence. From the hilltop, you can see how closely they sit together, at the meeting point of the old town and the rivers.

So take one last look at the pale walls in front of you. Some of the stones are new, and some, deep below, are five hundred years old or more. Notice the people crossing the square, the pigeons near the bell tower, and the green slope of the castle hill. As you walk away, listen for the cathedral bells again. They mark the hour over a palace that disappeared, and then came back.
