The guide

The story

Step through the doors and let your eyes adjust. Everything is white. Not a flat, painted white, but a deep, layered white that holds the light like fresh snow. Above you, around you, on every arch and every corner, figures crowd the walls. Angels, saints, soldiers, flowers and fruit, all in sculpted plaster. The air is cool and quiet. Even a whisper seems to travel a long way in here.

You are standing in the Church of Saint Peter and Saint Paul, in the Antakalnis district of Vilnius, a short way east of the old town and close to the Neris River. From the outside, the building looks calm and almost modest. Two towers frame a pale facade. Nothing quite prepares you for the interior. That contrast is part of the experience, and it is deliberate. The plain exterior keeps the surprise for the moment you step inside.

To understand why this church exists, we need to go back to the middle of the seventeenth century. In 1655, Muscovite forces captured Vilnius. The occupation lasted for several years, and the city suffered fire, looting and disease. When the Grand Duchy of Lithuania took the city back, the survivors wanted to give thanks. One powerful man decided to do it on a grand scale. His name was Michał Kazimierz Pac, a nobleman and military commander of the Grand Duchy.

Pac paid for this church, and building work began in the late 1660s. Look up at the facade as you leave later. You will see a Latin inscription: Regina pacis funda nos in pace. It means Queen of peace, establish us in peace. Notice the word pacis. It sounds almost exactly like the founder's own name, Pac. Many people believe this wordplay was intentional. A prayer for peace, and a signature, in one short line.

Now for the walls themselves. The decoration is stucco, a type of plaster that craftsmen shape by hand while it is still soft. The main masters were Italians, Giovanni Pietro Perti and Giovanni Maria Galli, working with a large team. Sources often say there are more than two thousand figures in this church. Nobody expects you to count them. But try to follow one wall from floor to ceiling, and you begin to sense the scale of the work.

Here is something many visitors miss. Almost all of this was done without colour. The craftsmen chose white plaster against white walls. That means every shape depends only on light and shadow. On a bright morning, the faces of the saints look sharp and alive. On a grey afternoon, they soften and seem to sink back into the stone. The same church looks different every hour of the day.

The figures are not random. Different parts of the church tell different stories. You will find scenes connected to the two apostles, Saint Peter and Saint Paul, who give the church its name. Elsewhere there are saints important to Lithuania, as well as military figures, armour and weapons. That is no accident either. The founder was a soldier. Faith and war sit side by side on these walls, just as they did in his life.

Look for one object that surprises almost everyone. Hanging in the nave is a large chandelier shaped like a ship. It is made of glass beads and brass, and it catches the light in small sparks. It is much younger than the stucco. It was added in the early twentieth century, made by craftsmen from Liepāja, a port town in Latvia. A ship is an old Christian symbol for the Church carrying believers through a stormy world.

Now walk back toward the entrance and pause. There is a story here about the founder's grave. Pac asked to be buried at the threshold of his church, under the place where people walk in. A stone there carries words in Latin, often translated as Here lies a sinner. The idea is humility. Everyone who entered would step over the man who built it all. Whether you read it as true modesty or a clever final gesture is up to you.

There are also older tales attached to this ground. Legend has it that a pagan shrine once stood near this spot, before Lithuania became Christian in the late fourteenth century. Locals sometimes connect it to a goddess of love. There is no firm evidence for this, so treat it as folklore. Still, it tells you something real. People in Vilnius have long felt that this place was special, long before the white figures arrived.

The church was connected to a monastery of the Canons Regular of the Lateran, a community of Catholic priests who lived by a shared rule. Over the following centuries, the city changed hands many times. It was part of the Russian Empire, then Poland, then the Soviet Union, before Lithuania regained independence in 1990. Through all of that, this interior survived. Many churches in the region were closed or damaged. Here, the plaster saints kept their places.

This is still a working church, not only a museum. You may see people praying quietly, or a service in progress. Lower your voice, move slowly, and give worshippers space. If you can, sit on one of the benches for a few minutes. From a seated position, the decoration on the vaults feels closer, almost like a ceiling of carved clouds. Give your eyes time. New details appear the longer you look.

Before you leave, stand once more in the centre of the nave. Turn slowly in a full circle. Notice the hands of the figures, the folds of their robes, the small leaves and shells tucked into the corners. Each one was shaped by a person, working on a scaffold, a long way above the floor. Then step outside into the light of Antakalnis. Look back at the quiet facade, and remember what it hides.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit on a sunny morning to see the stucco at its sharpest.
Sit on a bench to study the vault decoration up close.
Keep voices low; this is an active place of worship.
Combine the visit with a walk along the nearby Neris River.
Bring a zoom lens or binoculars for the high carvings.
