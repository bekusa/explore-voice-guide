The guide

The story

Listen first. A heavy door closes somewhere behind red brick, and the sound travels down a long corridor before it fades. Underfoot, the concrete is worn smooth. Above you, high windows let in a thin grey light. For more than a century, this was the sound of confinement in Vilnius. Today it is just as often the sound of an artist hauling equipment into a studio, or a crowd waiting for a concert.

You are standing inside Lukiškės Prison, one of the oldest prison complexes in Lithuania. It sits close to the centre of the city, a short walk from Gediminas Avenue and Lukiškės Square, with the Neris River nearby. That location surprises many visitors. Most cities push their prisons to the edges. Here, the walls stand among offices, apartments and cafés, and daily life has always moved past them.

The complex was built in the early twentieth century, when Vilnius was part of the Russian Empire. The imperial authorities wanted a modern prison, built on the ideas of that time about order and supervision. Look at the brickwork around you. The deep red brick, the arched windows and the strong, repeated shapes give the buildings a heavy, almost formal look. It was meant to show the power of the state, and it still does.

The main prison building follows a cross-shaped plan, with long wings that meet at a central point. This design had a practical purpose. A guard at the centre could see down several corridors at once. Stand where the wings meet and turn slowly. You can feel how the building was arranged around watching. Every line of sight leads back to the middle.

Inside the walls, the complex once held a church as well as cells. This is a detail that many visitors miss. Prison reformers of the period believed that faith and reflection could change a person, so worship was built into the routine of the prison. The idea sounds strange to us now. Yet it tells you how the builders saw their work. For them, a prison was not only a place to lock people away, but a place to reshape them.

The twentieth century brought one regime after another to Vilnius, and each one used these walls. After the Russian Empire came the interwar years, when the city was under Polish rule. Then came the Second World War, with Soviet and Nazi occupations. After the war, Lithuania became part of the Soviet Union for nearly five decades. Through all of these changes, Lukiškės kept working as a prison, and the people inside changed with the politics outside.

That is the hardest part of this place to hold in your mind. Ordinary criminals were held here. But so were political prisoners — people jailed for their beliefs, their activism, or simply for who they were. During the Nazi occupation, the prison was part of the machinery of the Holocaust in Vilnius, a city that once had one of the great Jewish communities of Europe. Under Soviet rule, the cells held people the regime saw as enemies. Many names are known only to historians and families.

Just outside, Lukiškės Square adds another layer. In the 1860s, after an uprising against Russian rule, the imperial authorities carried out public executions on this square. Some of the uprising's leaders died there. So this small part of Vilnius carries a long memory of punishment and resistance. When you walk between the prison and the square, you are crossing ground that has seen much of the city's struggle.

After Lithuania regained independence in 1990, the prison stayed in use for almost three more decades. It became known as a harsh and outdated place, far from modern European standards. In 2019, the last prisoners were moved to other facilities, and the gates closed on the prison era. Many people expected the buildings to stand empty for years, or to be sold off quickly. Something different happened.

The complex opened again as a cultural space, often called Lukiškės Prison 2.0. Artists, musicians and craftspeople moved into former offices and workshops. Some cells became studios. The yards and halls now host concerts, festivals, theatre and talks. It is an unusual idea — to make art inside walls that were built to limit human freedom. Some visitors find the contrast moving. Others find it uncomfortable. Both reactions are part of the experience.

Here is a second surprise. The prison has also become a filming location for international productions. Its long corridors and tiers of cells appeared in the fourth season of the series Stranger Things, where the buildings stood in for a Soviet prison. In a way, the screen returned the complex to an older role. Real history and fiction now meet in the same corridors.

If you join a guided tour, you can usually walk into the cell blocks and see how small the rooms are. Notice the thickness of the doors, the narrow openings, the metal fittings that are still in place. Pay attention to the temperature, too. Even on a warm day, the inner corridors can feel cool and damp. These quiet physical details often say more than any plaque. They help you imagine a day lived inside these spaces, hour after hour.

When you leave, pause for a moment at the outer wall. Behind you are the cells, the studios and the echoing corridors. In front of you, Vilnius goes on with its ordinary day — buses, footsteps, someone on a bicycle. Look back once at the red brick. It held fear, faith, punishment and now music. As you walk away, listen for the city sounds coming back. Then notice how easily you can choose your own direction.
