The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look at the red brick in front of you. It is not one smooth wall. It is a layered surface of ridges and narrow ribs that catch the light and throw small shadows. In the morning the façade looks almost pink. Late in the afternoon it turns a deep, burnt orange. Listen too. You may hear bicycle wheels on the cobbles, footsteps, and church bells somewhere across the Old Town. This is the Church of Saint Anne, one of the most loved buildings in Vilnius.

The church belongs to the late Gothic tradition. Gothic is the medieval style of pointed arches and tall, vertical lines that spread across Europe from France. By the time this church was built, around the turn of the sixteenth century, that style had grown light and decorative. Builders treated stone and brick almost like lace. Here in Lithuania, where good building stone was scarce, they worked in fired clay brick. This façade is one of the finest late Gothic brick fronts anywhere in northern Europe.

Now look more closely at the bricks themselves. Most walls use one standard brick shape, repeated thousands of times. This façade is different. Its builders used more than thirty different shapes of brick, each one moulded for a specific job. Some form the thin, rounded ribs. Some turn the corners of the window frames. Others build the small pointed arches near the top. Those many shapes are the reason the surface looks carved, even though nothing here was carved at all.

Let your eyes travel upward. The front is organised around strong vertical lines that pull your gaze toward the sky. Three slender towers rise from the top, the central one the tallest. Between them, the brick forms a pattern of interlocking arches and narrow frames. Some arches curve in a gentle S shape before they meet at a point. Builders called this an ogee arch. The whole front feels like it is rising, even though it is quite small.

That smallness surprises many visitors. Photographs often make Saint Anne's look grand. In person, it is modest in size, almost intimate. You can take in the whole façade without turning your head very far. This is part of its charm. The richness is concentrated, not spread across a vast wall. A small building that carries so much detail rewards the person who stands close and keeps looking.

There is a famous story about this church. Legend has it that Napoleon Bonaparte saw it when his army passed through Vilnius in 1812. According to the popular tale, he said he wished he could carry the church back to Paris in the palm of his hand. There is no reliable record that he actually said this. But locals repeat the story with real affection. It captures something true — this building feels like a precious object, almost a piece of jewellery.

The reality of 1812 was much harsher. Napoleon's Grande Armée came through Vilnius on its march toward Moscow, and later on its terrible retreat. Accounts from the time describe French troops using churches and religious buildings in the area for stabling horses and for storage. Saint Anne's and its neighbours suffered damage during those months. So the romantic legend sits beside a harder history of occupation and war, which this city knows well.

Now turn your attention to the larger church just behind and beside Saint Anne's. That is the Church of Saint Francis and Saint Bernard, often called the Bernardine Church. It belonged to a Franciscan monastery. Its walls are taller and heavier, and it carries the marks of defensive design. The two buildings together form one of the best views in the Old Town. The small, delicate church and the large, solid one seem to stand in conversation.

Look to the side of Saint Anne's and you will see a separate bell tower. It is younger than the church. It was added in the nineteenth century in a neo-Gothic style, which means a later revival of medieval forms. Its builders tried to match the spirit of the old façade. Compare the two carefully. The tower is neater and more regular. The older brick of the church has softer edges and slight variations in colour.

If the door is open, step inside. Many visitors expect the interior to continue the Gothic drama of the front. It does not. Over the centuries, fires and changing tastes reshaped the inside. Much of what you see today belongs to the later Baroque period, a style of rich altars and curving forms. The space is calm and fairly simple. This is still a working Catholic church, so move quietly and respect anyone who is praying.

Saint Anne's sits inside the Old Town of Vilnius, which UNESCO lists as a World Heritage Site. That status recognises one of the largest surviving medieval old towns in eastern and central Europe. Walk a few minutes from here and you reach the Vilnia River. Across a small bridge lies Užupis, a district of artists and quiet courtyards. The church sits at a meeting point of old Vilnius and its more playful, creative side.

Nearby, in the small green space by the Bernardine Church, stands a monument to Adam Mickiewicz. He was a nineteenth century poet who wrote in Polish and studied at Vilnius University. Both Lithuanians and Poles claim him as part of their heritage. His presence here is a reminder that Vilnius has always been a city of many languages and faiths. This church has watched Lithuanian, Polish, Jewish, Russian and many other communities pass along this street.

Before you walk on, step back across the street once more. Look at how the light moves over the ribs of brick. Notice how the colour shifts as a cloud passes. Find the tallest of the three towers, then trace the arches down to the doorway. Somewhere in that façade, more than thirty brick shapes are fitting together. As you leave, glance back once more over your shoulder. The church looks a little different from every angle — and it is worth that final look.

Practical tips

Visit late afternoon when the brick turns deep orange.
Cross the street for a full view of façade.
Frame both churches together from the small park.
Dress modestly and stay quiet during services inside.
Walk on to Užupis across the nearby Vilnia bridge.
