The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the pavement of Gediminas Avenue and look down at the base of the building in front of you. The grey stone blocks are cut with names. Row after row of them, carved in plain capitals. People walk past with shopping bags and coffee cups, and most do not slow down. But if you lean closer, you can read surnames, first names, and dates. These are people who were held, tortured, or killed inside these walls.

This is the Museum of Occupations and Freedom Fights in Vilnius. For many years it carried a different name, the Museum of Genocide Victims, and you may still hear locals call it that. The name changed in 2018. The new title reflects the full story told here. It covers the occupations that Lithuania lived through in the twentieth century, and the people who resisted them.

The building itself was not designed as a prison. It went up at the very end of the nineteenth century as a courthouse, when Vilnius was part of the Russian Empire. Look at the facade. It has the calm, ordered look of a government office of that era. Tall windows, heavy cornices, a sense of authority. That ordinary civic face is part of what makes this place so unsettling once you go inside.

In the decades that followed, the building changed hands with the regimes. During the Nazi occupation in the Second World War, the Gestapo, the German secret police, used it. Before and after that, Soviet security services took it over. The best-known of these was the KGB, the Soviet secret police and intelligence agency. For most of the Soviet period, this address meant one thing to people in Vilnius. It was where you were taken, and where you might not come back from.

The Soviet security services stayed here until Lithuania regained its independence in 1991. Only a year later, in 1992, the museum opened. That speed matters. These rooms were not reconstructed decades afterwards from old photographs. Many of them were preserved close to how they were left. When you walk through, you are looking at the actual spaces where interrogations and imprisonment took place.

The upper floors trace the wider history. Here the museum tells the story of the first Soviet occupation in 1940, the German occupation that followed, and the return of Soviet rule in 1944. You will see documents, photographs, uniforms, and personal belongings. Pay attention to the small objects. A handwritten letter, a pair of worn shoes, a homemade toy. They often carry more weight than the large display panels.

One section focuses on the mass deportations. Tens of thousands of Lithuanians were loaded onto trains and sent east, to Siberia and other remote parts of the Soviet Union. Families were often given very little time to pack. Many deportees died from cold, hunger, and hard labour. Some survivors came home years later. Others never did. Their stories are told here through letters, objects, and photographs from the camps and settlements.

Another part of the museum tells a story that surprises many visitors. After the Second World War, thousands of Lithuanian men and women went into the forests to fight Soviet rule. They are known as the partisans, or the Forest Brothers. This armed resistance lasted for years, well into the 1950s. The fighters lived in hidden bunkers dug into the ground. They printed underground newspapers. Most were eventually killed, captured, or betrayed.

Now the descent. The prison is in the basement, and this is the part people remember. The corridors are narrow and low. The air feels cooler. The cells have heavy doors with small viewing hatches. You can step inside some of them. Notice how little space there is, and how little light. Prisoners could be held here for weeks or months during interrogation.

Among the cells are special punishment rooms. One is a padded cell, lined with soft material so that a prisoner could not harm himself. Its purpose was to isolate and break people, not to protect them. Another is a cell with a floor of cold water and a tiny raised platform. A prisoner had to balance on that small dry spot, hour after hour. If they slipped or fell asleep, they fell into the water.

At the end of the basement is the execution chamber. According to the museum, more than a thousand people were killed in this room in the years after the war. Part of the floor is covered with glass. Under it you can see objects found during excavation of the room — personal items left behind. Many visitors go quiet here. It is fine to stand still for a while. There is no need to rush through.

As you climb back up to the street, you may notice how ordinary everything looks again. Across the avenue is Lukiškės Square, an open public space where people sit, walk dogs, and cross toward the old town. For decades, residents walked past this building without speaking about it. Today it is a museum, and the names on its walls are read aloud in the quietest possible way, by visitors who stop and look.

Before you leave, go back to those carved stones at the base of the building. Find one name, any name, and read it slowly. Then look up at the windows above you, and down toward the basement level you have just walked through. The trams and cars on Gediminas Avenue continue to move past. Life in Vilnius goes on around this building — and that ordinary sound of the city is, in its own way, part of what was fought for here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least two hours for both floors and basement.
Start upstairs for context, then finish in the basement prison.
The basement cells may be intense for young children.
Take the audio or printed guide for room-by-room context.
