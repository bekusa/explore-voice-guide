The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the paving of Daukanto Square. The ground is open and wide, and the city noise of the Old Town drops away behind you. In front of you runs a long, pale facade with a row of columns. Above it, a flag moves in the breeze. This is the Presidential Palace of Lithuania, the working office of the head of state. It sits only a few minutes' walk from the Cathedral and the university courtyards.

At first glance, the building looks calm and orderly. It is almost too tidy for a city famous for its baroque church towers and crooked lanes. That restraint is deliberate. The palace you see today belongs to the classicist style. This is the style of the early nineteenth century, which borrowed straight lines, columns and symmetry from ancient Greece and Rome. But the story of this site is much older than its smooth plaster suggests.

For centuries, this ground belonged to the Church. From the late Middle Ages, the bishops of Vilnius kept their residence here. They lived close to the cathedral and close to the power of the Grand Dukes of Lithuania. Over the generations, the bishops' palace was rebuilt and extended many times. It took on Gothic, Renaissance and baroque layers, one on top of another. Very little of that earlier look is visible from the square today. Most of it lies hidden behind later walls.

The great change came after the end of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, in the late eighteenth century. This was the large joint state that Lithuania had shared with Poland for about two hundred years. When it was divided among its neighbours, Vilnius passed to the Russian Empire. The old bishops' residence became the seat of the imperial governors-general. They were the officials who ruled this region on behalf of the Tsar.

In the early nineteenth century, the building was remodelled into the shape you see now. The work followed designs connected with the Russian architect Vasily Stasov. He was a leading figure of the Empire style in Saint Petersburg. Look at the portico, the porch with columns at the centre of the facade. Notice how the columns are plain and heavy rather than carved and decorative. The message was simple: order, authority and the presence of the empire.

Now for a surprise that many visitors miss. In the summer of 1812, two enemies slept under this roof within weeks of each other. Tsar Alexander the First stayed in Vilnius as the threat of war grew. Then Napoleon's Grand Army crossed into Lithuania, and the Tsar left the city. Napoleon arrived soon after and is said to have used the palace as his headquarters. Months later, the defeated French army came back through Vilnius in the freezing winter retreat.

The building also has darker memories. After the uprising of 1863 against Russian rule, the governor-general Mikhail Muravyov governed from this palace. He led a harsh campaign against the rebels and against the Lithuanian and Polish culture of the region. Many local people remember him with a grim nickname, 'the Hangman'. Repression followed, including a ban on printing Lithuanian in the Latin alphabet. So the same rooms that held grand balls also held cold orders.

The twentieth century brought new owners almost every few decades. During the interwar years, Vilnius was under Polish control, and the palace served the officials of that state. The Second World War damaged the building. In the Soviet period it was restored and given a cultural role. For a time it was used by the artists' association, and locals often called it the Palace of Artists. In a way, the building moved from ruling people to hosting painters.

Then, in the 1990s, Lithuania restored its independence. The palace was renovated and became the official office of the President of the Republic. Think about that turn for a moment. For more than a century, this was the seat of foreign governors. Today it is the workplace of an elected Lithuanian head of state. The same walls now carry a very different meaning.

Look up at the flagpole. Lithuania's yellow, green and red tricolour flies over the palace. You may also see the president's own standard, a dark red flag with the white knight on horseback, called the Vytis. This galloping knight with a raised sword has been the symbol of Lithuania for centuries. When you notice it, you are looking at a sign that this is a working building, not only a monument.

The guards at the entrance belong to a ceremonial tradition. On some days, you can watch a changing of the guard or a flag ceremony in front of the palace. The soldiers move slowly and precisely, and the square goes quiet around them. If you see a small crowd gathering near the gates, it is worth waiting a few minutes. Schedules vary, so check them locally rather than guessing.

Walk around to the back of the building, and the mood changes. Behind the palace lies a garden with lawns and neat paths. It feels softer and more private than the formal square. On certain days, the public can join guided tours of the interior, usually with an advance reservation. Inside, the halls are arranged for official receptions, with high ceilings and polished floors. Even from outside, the garden gives you a sense of how the palace breathes.

Now turn around and look at the square itself. It carries the name of Simonas Daukantas, a nineteenth-century historian. He wrote the history of Lithuania in the Lithuanian language, at a time when that was a bold act. Across the square stand the older buildings of Vilnius University and the narrow streets that lead deeper into the Old Town. It is a fitting address: a palace of power facing a monument to language and memory.

Before you move on, take one more look at the long facade. Imagine the bishops in their robes, the Tsar's officers, Napoleon's tired soldiers, the painters, and today's presidential staff. All of them crossed this same ground. As you walk away toward the university or the cathedral, listen to your footsteps on the open stone. The square is quiet, and the flag is still moving above the columns.

What to look for

Plain heavy columns of the central classicist portico.
The white knight Vytis on the red presidential standard.
Ceremonial guards standing motionless at the palace gates.
Lithuania's yellow, green and red flag above the roofline.
The open paved expanse of Daukanto Square itself.

Practical tips
