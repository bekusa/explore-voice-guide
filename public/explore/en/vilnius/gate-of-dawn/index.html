The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment beneath the arch. Feel the slight slope of the cobbles under your feet, worn smooth by centuries of carts, boots and bare knees. Above you, a window glows with gold. Around you, people slow their steps. Some stop completely. A few lower their heads, and an older woman may cross herself without breaking her stride. This is the Gate of Dawn, and it is a city gate and a place of prayer at the same moment.

Let us start with the stone. In the early sixteenth century, Vilnius was the capital of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, a large state that stretched far into what is now Belarus and Ukraine. The threat of Tatar raids pushed the city to build a defensive wall. That wall had several gates, each guarding a road out of town. Today, almost all of the wall and its gates are gone. This gate is the only one that survives. You are walking through the last door of the old fortified city.

The gate faced south, toward the road to Medininkai, an old castle town to the southeast. That is why some old records call it the Medininkai Gate. Its Lithuanian name, Aušros Vartai, means Gate of Dawn. In Polish it is known as Ostra Brama, often translated as the Sharp Gate. Nobody agrees on exactly where these names come from. One common explanation links the Polish name to an old district called the Sharp End. The poetic one, dawn, is the name that stuck in Lithuanian.

Now walk a few steps through the arch and turn around to look at the outer side, the side that once faced attackers. Here the gate looks like a soldier. You can see small openings in the wall, gun ports for defenders. Above them sits a decorative Renaissance top, with a coat of arms held by two griffins, mythical creatures with the body of a lion and the head of an eagle. The coat of arms shows a knight on horseback, the symbol of Lithuania that you will see across the city.

Walk back through the arch to the city side, and the gate changes character completely. The defensive face disappears. Instead, you see a pale classical facade with columns, and that large window with the glowing image behind it. This is the chapel. It sits directly above the passage, so every person who enters the Old Town here walks underneath it. That arrangement is the key to the whole place. The gate was built to keep danger out, and the chapel was placed to bless everyone who came in.

Behind that window hangs a painting of the Virgin Mary, known as the Mother of Mercy. It dates from the seventeenth century and was painted on wooden boards. Look closely at the pose — it surprises many visitors. Mary does not hold the infant Jesus, as she does in most images. She stands alone, her head gently tilted, her hands crossed over her chest. Almost the whole painting is covered with a gilded silver casing. Only her face and hands show through the metal.

The image is surrounded by a halo of rays and a crescent moon at the bottom. The silver glints differently depending on the hour. In the morning, the light outside is cool and the gold looks soft. After dark, the chapel lamps make the window shine against the stone, and the whole facade seems to hang in the street. If you can, come back at both times. It is the same window, but it does not feel like the same place.

The painting was not always behind glass and stone. For a long time, it was simply part of the gate. The chapel was cared for by Carmelite friars, a Catholic religious order, who served at the neighbouring Church of Saint Teresa. They built a wooden chapel here in the later seventeenth century. Fire destroyed it in the early eighteenth century, and it was rebuilt in masonry. The classical facade you see now was added in the early nineteenth century.

The gate is full of stories, and not all of them are history. One tradition tells of a small boy who fell from a height near the gate and was brought back to life after prayers to the image. The Carmelites recorded it as a miracle. Believers have told stories like this for generations. Whatever you make of them, the evidence of belief is real. Inside the chapel, the walls are covered with small silver hearts and other offerings. People left them to give thanks for answered prayers.

This gate also belongs to literature. The great poet Adam Mickiewicz grew up in this region and studied at Vilnius University. He wrote in Polish, and the opening lines of his epic poem, Pan Tadeusz, call on the Virgin who shines above the Gate of Dawn. Generations of Polish schoolchildren learned those lines by heart. That is one reason you will hear so much Polish around you here. For Poles, Lithuanians and Belarusians alike, this small window carries deep meaning.

If you would like to see the chapel itself, look for the entrance on the side street, through the courtyard of Saint Teresa's Church. A staircase leads up to the chapel. Inside, it is small, quiet and usually busy with people praying. Services take place here, sometimes in Lithuanian and sometimes in Polish. Please keep your voice low and your phone quiet. In 1993, Pope John Paul II prayed in this chapel during his visit to Lithuania. Many people still remember that day.

Back down on the street, notice how the crowd moves. Tour groups stop and point their cameras up at the window. Local people walk past and nod, or make a small sign of the cross. Some men remove their hats, an old custom here. On busy days, you may even see someone kneeling on the pavement beneath the arch. The street is a thoroughfare and a church aisle at the same time, and both uses carry on side by side.

From here, the street runs downhill toward the Town Hall square, and the Old Town opens up around you. Along this short stretch, you pass the Church of Saint Teresa, the Orthodox Church of the Holy Spirit behind its gateway, and the Basilian gate with its curving Baroque top. Within a few minutes' walk, Catholic, Orthodox and Eastern-rite Catholic traditions stand close together. That mix is very typical of Vilnius.

Before you go, look up one last time. You are standing in the only place where the old city wall still meets the road. Above you, the silver figure looks down at the street, as it has for centuries. Listen for a moment — footsteps on the cobbles, a church bell somewhere, a quiet prayer behind you. When you walk on into the Old Town, you will follow the same path that travellers have taken through this arch for five hundred years.

Practical tips
