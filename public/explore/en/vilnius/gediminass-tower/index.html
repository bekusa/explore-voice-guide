The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, faintly, the bells of the cathedral belfry. Around you, the wind moves through the trees on the slope. Under your feet is old brick and packed earth, worn smooth by many visitors. The tower in front of you is not tall. But from up here, it seems to hold the whole city in view. Red roofs, church spires, and two rivers spread out below. This is Gediminas's Tower, on the hill where Vilnius began.

The hill sits where two rivers meet. The wide Neris curves along to the north. The smaller Vilnia joins it from the east, and the city takes its name from that smaller river. A steep hill above two rivers was an obvious place to defend. Long before any brick tower stood here, people built wooden fortifications on this summit. Archaeologists have found traces of settlement on and around the hill going back many centuries.

The tower carries the name of Gediminas, Grand Duke of Lithuania in the early fourteenth century. He was a powerful ruler of a large, pagan state at a time when most of Europe was Christian. In 1323, his letters to other European cities and rulers mention Vilnius by name. Those letters invited merchants, craftsmen, and monks to come and settle. For this reason, Lithuanians treat 1323 as the date the city first appears in the written record.

There is also a legend, and every Lithuanian child knows it. According to the story, Gediminas went hunting in the forests near here. He killed a great beast and decided to sleep on the hill for the night. He dreamed of an iron wolf standing on the summit, howling as loudly as a hundred wolves. A pagan priest named Lizdeika explained the dream. The wolf, he said, meant that a great and famous city would rise on this spot.

Remember, that is folklore, not documented history. But the story is still very much alive in the city. Down the hill, near the cathedral, you will find a statue of Gediminas. The iron wolf appears in songs, in names, and on souvenirs. Even the basketball fans of Vilnius know it. The legend matters because it gives the city a beginning that feels personal — a ruler, a hill, a dream.

Now, look closely at the tower itself. It is built from red brick, resting on a base of rough fieldstone. It has an eight-sided shape, which you can see best when you walk around it. Here is the first surprise. What you see is not the castle of Gediminas. His fortress was almost certainly made largely of wood. The brick building you see today belongs to a later period, after fire and rebuilding.

In the early fifteenth century, the castle complex on the hill was damaged by fire. It was rebuilt in brick under Grand Duke Vytautas, one of the most famous rulers in Lithuanian history. At that time, this tower was only one part of a much larger Upper Castle. Walls ran along the summit. There were other towers and a palace building. Below, at the foot of the hill, stood the Lower Castle, where the rulers later lived.

Over the following centuries, the castle slowly lost its purpose. Wars damaged it. The city grew outward, and stone and brick were taken for other buildings. By the nineteenth century, much of the Upper Castle was in ruins. The tower you see was partly reconstructed in the twentieth century. As you walk around it, notice the differences in the brickwork. Older sections and restored sections do not look quite the same. The wall shows centuries of repair.

Here is the second surprise. This small tower became one of the strongest symbols of modern Lithuanian independence. In 1988, during the final years of Soviet rule, the yellow, green, and red national flag was raised here again. Crowds gathered below to watch. A year later, in 1989, a human chain called the Baltic Way began in Vilnius. Around two million people joined hands across Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia, all the way to Tallinn. Vilnius was the southern end of that chain.

Today the flag still flies from the top of the tower. Inside, there is a small museum, part of the National Museum of Lithuania. It shows models of the castles as they once looked, along with weapons and armour from the period. The models are worth a slow look. They help you imagine the full castle, with all its walls and towers, standing on this hill. Then you can climb to the observation platform at the top.

From the platform, find the white belfry and the cathedral directly below. Beyond them, the Old Town spreads south in a mix of baroque domes and tiled roofs. Turn east, and across the Vilnia valley you will see a hill with three white crosses. Turn north, and the Neris runs past glass towers on the far bank. In one view, you see medieval Vilnius, baroque Vilnius, and the modern city. Few places in Vilnius show you all three at once.

The hill itself is fragile. In recent years, parts of the slopes have slipped after heavy rain. Engineers have worked to stabilise the ground, and some paths may be closed or changed when you visit. You can walk up the paved path from the Old Town side, or take the short funicular from the courtyard near the museum buildings below. Either way, the climb is short. The view at the top more than pays for it.

Before you go, stand still for a moment beside the brick. Feel the wind come up the hill. Look again at the two rivers meeting below, the reason anyone built here at all. Then, as you walk down, glance back over your shoulder. From the streets of the Old Town, you will see this small tower again and again, with the flag on top. Wherever you are in Vilnius, it is never far away.
