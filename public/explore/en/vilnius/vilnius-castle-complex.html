The guide

The story

Start with your feet. On the path up Gediminas Hill, the ground shifts under you, from cobbles to gravel to worn steps. The air changes as you climb. Down below, you hear traffic on the river roads and the bells of the cathedral. Up here, the sound thins out. You hear wind in the trees, a few voices, and maybe the snap of a flag above a red brick tower. This is where Vilnius began.

What people call the Vilnius Castle Complex is not one building. It is a group of fortifications and palaces that grew around a single green hill. Two rivers meet nearby, the wide Neris and the smaller Vilnia. Together they gave early rulers water, trade routes, and a natural defence. Historians usually speak of three parts: the Upper Castle on the hilltop, the Lower Castle at its foot, and a third, the Crooked Castle, which has almost vanished.

The name on the tower belongs to Gediminas, a Grand Duke of Lithuania in the early fourteenth century. His letters to European cities, written in 1323, are among the earliest documents that name Vilnius. But locals usually tell a different origin story. Legend has it that Gediminas fell asleep here after a hunt. He dreamed of an iron wolf on the hill, howling like a hundred wolves at once.

According to the tale, a pagan priest named Lizdeika explained the dream. The iron wolf meant a strong city. The howling meant that its fame would travel across the world. So Gediminas built his castle here. Nobody can prove the dream, of course. It is folklore, not history. Still, you will see iron wolves in Vilnius today, in sculpture and in local symbols, and the story still matters to many people in the city.

Now look at the tower itself. Gediminas Tower is the best-known surviving piece of the Upper Castle. It stood on the western side of a larger brick fortress. The first castles here were probably wooden. Brick and stone came later, and the fortress you could have seen in the early fifteenth century was much larger than this one tower. Much of what you see now comes from restoration in the twentieth century.

Here is something many visitors do not notice. Look at the shape of the tower as your eye moves up. The lower part has one form, and the upper part becomes octagonal, with eight sides. This is a small clue that the tower changed over time. Builders rebuilt it, repaired it, and restored it. The walls carry the marks of many generations, not just one moment.

The flag on top matters too. During the Soviet period, the national tricolour of yellow, green and red could not fly here. In 1988, as the independence movement grew, people raised it on the tower again. A year later, in August 1989, the Baltic Way began near this spot. About two million people held hands in a human chain from Vilnius to Tallinn. So this small tower is also a place of modern history.

Turn now towards the view. From the top of the hill, the Old Town spreads out in red roofs, church towers, and narrow streets. To one side, you see the newer business district across the Neris, with glass towers. On another hill nearby, you can see three white crosses. That hill was once the site of the Crooked Castle. Teutonic Knights destroyed it in 1390, during the long wars between the Order and Lithuania.

Now look down towards the foot of the hill. The large white building with tall windows next to the cathedral is the Palace of the Grand Dukes of Lithuania. This was the heart of the Lower Castle. Here the rulers held court, welcomed ambassadors, and lived in a style influenced by the Italian Renaissance. For a time, this palace was one of the important royal residences of eastern Europe.

The palace you see is not the original building. War in the seventeenth century badly damaged the castles, and the palace fell into ruin. In 1801, under the Russian Empire, the authorities demolished most of what remained. For almost two centuries, the site was mostly empty. Then archaeologists dug here and found foundations, tiles, and everyday objects. The reconstruction opened in the early twenty-first century, and inside you can still see original walls under the new ones.

The cathedral beside the palace belonged to the same castle world. Its free-standing bell tower is a second surprise. Look at the lower part. Many experts believe it was once a defensive tower in the Lower Castle walls. Later, people added the upper levels and bells. So when you hear the bells, you may hear them from part of an old fortress. Nearby, at the foot of the hill, arsenal buildings now hold collections of the National Museum of Lithuania.

The hill itself also has a story. Gediminas Hill is mostly made of soft earth and sand. In recent years, heavy rains caused landslides on its slopes, and engineers worked to stabilise them. When you see fences, netting, or closed paths, this is the reason. The ground under this famous tower is fragile. Every visit depends on careful work you mostly do not see.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Feel the wind at the top of the hill, and listen to the city below. Look at the red brick again, then at the white palace, the cathedral tower, and the three crosses on the next hill. Here, wood became brick, palaces became ruins, and ruins became museums. As you walk down, notice how the noise of the city returns step by step. You are walking from the city's oldest heart into its present.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the funicular up if you want to save your legs.
Come near sunset for warm light over the Old Town roofs.
Wear shoes with good grip for the steep hill paths.
Visit the palace interior to see the original foundations.
Check online for slope closures before you climb.
