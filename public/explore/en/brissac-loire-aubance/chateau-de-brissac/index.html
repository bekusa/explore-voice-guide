The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and tilt your head back. The pale stone keeps rising... and rising. Gravel crunches under your feet, and somewhere in the park a crow calls from the cedars. In front of you is a building that looks almost too tall for its own frame. Two round medieval towers hold a narrow, ornate façade between them, like old soldiers guarding a much younger guest.

This is the Château de Brissac, in the heart of Anjou, the old province around the city of Angers. People here call it the Giant of the Loire. The name is fair. It is often described as the tallest château in France, with seven levels from cellar to roof. Most Loire châteaux spread out across the land. This one climbs upward instead, and you can feel that difference as soon as you stand at its base.

The story begins with a fortress. In the fifteenth century, a powerful lord named Pierre de Brézé built a castle on this spot above the little Aubance river. Those two heavy towers with their conical roofs come from that earlier building. They were built for defence, not for beauty. Look at their thick walls and their solid, practical shape. They are the oldest part of what you see today.

In 1502, the estate was bought by René de Cossé. That purchase started one of the longest family stories in France. The Cossé-Brissac family still lives here today, more than five centuries later. That is the first surprise for many visitors. This is not a museum frozen in the past. It is a family home, and the people who open its doors to you carry the same family name as the dukes on the portraits inside.

The château's real turning point came in the 1590s. France was torn apart by the Wars of Religion, a long civil conflict between Catholics and Protestants. Charles de Cossé, a marshal of France, was governor of Paris. In 1594, he opened the city to King Henri IV and allowed the king to enter without a bloody siege. Henri rewarded him generously, and the title of Duke of Brissac followed.

With new rank came a new ambition. Charles decided to tear down the old fortress and build a grand modern residence. The work is usually credited to the architect Jacques Corbineau. The new building rose in the early seventeenth century, in a rich, decorated style. Look at the central pavilion. You will see columns stacked one above the other, carved window frames, and stone ornament that becomes more elaborate as your eye moves higher.

And here is the second surprise. The château was never finished. The plan was to demolish the medieval towers and replace them with matching new wings. But Charles died in 1621, and the building work stopped. So the old towers stayed. That accident is why the façade looks so unusual. You are looking at two centuries side by side — a fortress and a palace — joined by chance rather than by design.

Before you go inside, there is a story locals love to tell. Legend has it that the château is haunted by a figure known as the Green Lady. According to the tale, she was Charlotte de Brézé, a royal daughter of King Charles the Seventh and his famous companion Agnès Sorel. Her husband, Jacques de Brézé, is said to have found her with a lover and killed them both. People say she still walks the upper rooms, dressed in green, with empty holes where her eyes should be.

The murder of Charlotte is recorded in history. The ghost, of course, belongs to folklore. But the story gives the tall staircases and quiet corridors a certain shiver, especially on a grey afternoon. Families often ask the guides about her, and the guides seem happy to keep the legend alive. Listen for it on your tour. Every guide tells it a little differently.

Inside, the rooms carry the weight of many generations. Painted ceilings with gilded beams, tapestries, heavy furniture, and family portraits fill the main floors. The château is said to hold around two hundred rooms, though visitors see only a part of them. Notice how the ceilings in the great rooms seem to float high above you. That height is the vertical idea of the building, carried on the inside as well as the outside.

One room catches many visitors off guard: a small private theatre. In the late nineteenth century, a member of the family, the Vicomtesse de Trédern, was a passionate singer. She had this theatre built inside the château so that she could perform for guests. It is decorated in red and gold, in the style of the Belle Époque, the elegant period before the First World War. It feels like a jewel box hidden in a fortress.

After the house, take time for the grounds. The park spreads out around the château with wide lawns, old trees, and tall cedars. The Aubance river runs quietly through the landscape. The estate also has its own vineyards, and wine is still made here, as it has been for a very long time in this part of Anjou. The cellars below the château belong to that tradition. Down there, the air feels cool and smells faintly of stone and old wood.

From the far side of the lawn, turn around and look back. From this distance, the château's unusual shape becomes clear. The central tower looks slim and tall, almost squeezed between the wide medieval towers. The slate roofs catch the light, sometimes silver, sometimes nearly blue. In late afternoon, the soft limestone, called tuffeau in this region, turns warm and golden. It is the same pale stone that gives so many Loire buildings their gentle colour.

So before you leave, pause once more. Look at the two old towers and the tall palace between them. One man's plan stopped halfway, and that unfinished plan became the building's character. Listen to the wind in the cedars and the crunch of gravel as other visitors walk past. Five hundred years of one family stand in front of you... still unfinished, and still lived in.
