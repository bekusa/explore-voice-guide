The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere in the beech trees a woodpigeon calls. Ahead of you, a long white building sits low against the sky. It is not tall or showy. It looks calm, like a large country house that expects guests but is in no hurry to greet them. This is Soestdijk Palace, and for much of the twentieth century it was one of the most familiar homes in the Netherlands.

A quick note on where you are standing. The palace lies in the wooded country between Baarn and Soest, a short drive east of Laren and the villages of the Gooi region. The road in front of the gates is the old route between Amsterdam and the east. That road matters to this story. Wealthy city families travelled along it to escape the noise and smells of Amsterdam, and this quiet edge of the province of Utrecht became their retreat.

The story begins in the seventeenth century, the Dutch Golden Age. During this period, Amsterdam merchants grew rich on global trade. One powerful Amsterdam family, the De Graeffs, built a country house here. Cornelis de Graeff was a leading figure in the city government. His house was modest by later standards. It was a place for hunting, fresh air, and a little distance from politics. Picture a much smaller house than the one in front of you today.

Then royalty arrived. In the 1670s, the house passed to William the Third of Orange. He was the Dutch stadtholder, a kind of chief executive of the Dutch Republic, and he later became King of England. William loved hunting, and the woods around here suited him. Under the House of Orange, the house slowly changed from a merchant's retreat into a princely residence. That link to the Orange family would last for more than three centuries.

Now look at the shape of the building. Notice how the two side wings curve forward, like arms reaching out toward the lawn. Those wings came later, in the nineteenth century. After the Battle of Waterloo in 1815, the Dutch nation gave this estate to the Crown Prince, the future King William the Second. He had fought at Waterloo and was wounded there. The gift was a thank-you from the country, and he rebuilt the house on a grander scale.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Not far from the palace, along the road, stands a tall stone obelisk. Locals call it the Waterloo Needle. It was raised to honour that same battle and the prince who fought in it. So the needle and the palace belong together. One marks the battle. The other marks the reward. If you drove in along the main road, you may have passed it without a second look.

The white facade in front of you is plaster over brick. It gives the building its clean, pale look, especially on a grey Dutch day when the sky and the walls almost match. The style is restrained and classical. There are no towers, no gold, no dramatic statues on the roof. Royal houses in the Netherlands often look like this. The country has long preferred its monarchs to seem practical rather than grand.

For most Dutch people, though, this palace means one person above all: Queen Juliana. She moved here with Prince Bernhard in the 1930s, and she kept living here for the rest of her life. Even as queen, she chose Soestdijk over the palaces in The Hague and Amsterdam. Her four daughters grew up in these rooms and gardens. One of them, Beatrix, later became queen herself.

And now the second surprise. Every year on Queen's Day, the thirtieth of April, the lawn in front of you filled with people. A long parade of ordinary citizens walked past the steps. There were schoolchildren, farmers, choirs, and decorated floats. They carried gifts and flowers for the queen, who stood on the steps and waved. The event was shown on national television. For decades, this lawn was where the Dutch royal family met the public face to face.

Life here was not always calm. Prince Bernhard became the centre of a major scandal in the 1970s, involving payments from an American aircraft company. It shook the country and changed his public role. Queen Juliana herself was known for her simple manners and her interest in social causes. Inside these walls, the family lived through both admiration and hard public questions. A palace can hold both.

Queen Juliana and Prince Bernhard both died in 2004, only months apart. After that, the house stood empty, and the state had to decide its future. A royal home without royals is a difficult thing to keep. In the end, the government sold the estate to a private developer. The plan was to restore the palace, open it to visitors, and give the grounds a new life.

Today you can walk where the parade once passed. Guided tours take visitors through the rooms, though it is worth checking ahead, because access and arrangements change. Take time in the park as well. The old trees, the long lawn, and the straight views toward the house were all designed for slow walking. Stand at the far end of the lawn and look back. That is the view the crowds once had on the thirtieth of April.

Before you go, pause for a moment. Look again at those curving white wings and the plain steps at the centre. Imagine a woman in a simple coat, waving at a farmer with a basket of tulips. Then listen to the gravel under your feet and the pigeons in the beeches. The crowds are gone. The house is still here, quiet and pale against the trees, waiting for the next visitor.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check tour arrangements ahead, as access to the rooms varies.
Walk to the far end of the lawn for the classic view.
Bring comfortable shoes for the gravel paths and wooded park.
Visit on an overcast day to see the white facade glow softly.
Combine your visit with a stop at the nearby Waterloo Needle.
