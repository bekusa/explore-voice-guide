The guide

The story

The street climbs, and the colors climb with it. Lime green, coral pink, turquoise, buttercup yellow. On the lower slopes of Signal Hill, whole rows of small flat-roofed houses are painted like sweets. Cobblestones shine underfoot. Above the rooftops, a minaret stands against the mountain. This is the Bo-Kaap, one of the oldest living neighborhoods in Cape Town.

The name is Afrikaans for above the Cape, and that is exactly where you stand. The district grew in the eighteenth century as rental housing on the edge of town. Rows of small houses were built for artisans and laborers. Many of the people who came to live in them had not chosen Cape Town at all.

The Dutch East India Company ran the Cape as a supply station, and it ran on forced labor. Enslaved people were brought here from Indonesia, Malaysia, India, Sri Lanka, and other parts of Africa. Among them were craftsmen, cooks, tailors, and religious scholars. Their descendants became known as Cape Malays, though their roots span half the world. Their culture, faith, and cooking shaped this neighborhood and the whole city.

Islam holds the community's story. Walk along Dorp Street and you pass the Auwal Mosque, founded in 1794, the oldest mosque in South Africa. Its first imam, Tuan Guru, was a scholar from the island of Tidore who had been held prisoner on Robben Island. While captive, he wrote out the Quran from memory. When he was freed, he taught here, and the faith he carried took deep root.

Stay a while and you may hear the call to prayer drift over the rooftops, answered from another minaret a few blocks away. On Fridays the streets fill with worshippers, and greetings pass from stoop to stoop. During Ramadan the neighborhood keeps late, gentle hours, and the smell of cooking rises after sunset.

Why the bright paint? People here tell different stories. In the rental years the houses were plain, mostly washed in lime. As families gained ownership, color spread door by door. Many residents describe the painting as a celebration, a way of saying, this house is ours. Whatever its origin, the tradition holds, and neighbors often choose their shades together so the rows stay in harmony.

Look closely at the buildings themselves. Many blend Cape Dutch and Cape Georgian styles. You will see curved parapets hiding flat roofs, tall sash windows, and raised front stoops where people sit and talk in the evening. The Bo-Kaap Museum on Wale Street occupies a house from the 1760s, one of the oldest in the area, furnished to show family life in the nineteenth century.

The kitchen is where Bo-Kaap wins hearts. Cape Malay cooking blends Southeast Asian spice with Cape ingredients. Bobotie is a baked dish of spiced minced meat under a savory custard. Koeksisters here are soft, spiced doughnuts soaked in syrup and rolled in coconut. Samoosas are folded thin and fried crisp. Spice shops grind their own masala blends, and several residents teach cooking classes in their own homes.

At midday, do not be alarmed by a sudden boom. On Signal Hill above the neighborhood, the noon gun has fired since 1806, Monday through Saturday. It once let ships in the bay set their clocks. Now it startles visitors and barely turns a local head.

This community has had to defend its place. Under apartheid, the Group Areas Act zoned Bo-Kaap for one community only, and neighbors of other backgrounds were forced to leave. In recent years, rising property prices have brought a different pressure. Residents campaigned for years, and in 2019 the neighborhood gained formal heritage protection that guards its historic character.

Walk here as a guest, not a spectator. These painted houses are homes, not a film set. Ask before you photograph people or step close to doorways. Better still, join a walking tour led by a resident. The stories, told firsthand, are worth more than any picture.

Find a high corner late in the afternoon, when the light softens. The houses glow. The mountain turns gold behind the minarets. Children's voices echo down the cobbles, as they have for more than two centuries. Bo-Kaap is not a monument to a finished past. It is a community, still here, still cooking, still praying, still painting its walls bright.
