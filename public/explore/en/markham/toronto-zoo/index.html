The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere ahead there is a long, low call, maybe a gibbon or a peacock. Under that you can hear wind moving through tall grass and the crunch of gravel under your shoes. The air out here smells of earth, hay and river water. This does not feel like a city zoo squeezed between buildings. It feels like a valley, because that is where you are standing.

Welcome to the Toronto Zoo. It sits on the far eastern edge of Toronto, in the Rouge Valley, right where the city meets Markham and the farmland beyond. The grounds are very large, spread across wooded ravines, open meadows and sloping hillsides. Many visitors are surprised by how much walking it takes. Some paths run along ridges, and others drop down toward the river. That spread is part of the original design.

The zoo opened in 1974. Before that, Toronto's animals lived at the Riverdale Zoo, a compact Victorian-era site near downtown. Riverdale had small cages and hard floors, which was common for its time. When the new zoo opened, planners wanted something very different. They wanted space, natural landscapes, and animals grouped by the parts of the world they come from. The old Riverdale grounds still exist today as a small working farm.

That idea of grouping by geography still shapes your walk. The zoo is divided into zoogeographic regions, which simply means zones arranged by continent and habitat. You will move through Indo-Malaya, Africa, the Americas, Australasia and Eurasia. There is also a Canadian section and a northern tundra area. Each region tries to carry the plants, sounds and climate of its home. As you cross from one to the next, notice how the planting changes underfoot.

Several regions are built around large indoor pavilions. These are warm, humid buildings with glass roofs and tropical plants. On a cold Ontario day, stepping inside feels like walking into another season. Your glasses may fog up. The air is heavy and green. Birds move through the canopy above you, and water runs somewhere out of sight. These pavilions let the zoo keep warm-climate animals through long Canadian winters.

Here is something many visitors miss. Because of those pavilions, this zoo stays open through the winter. Snow changes the place completely. Animals from cold climates, such as tigers from the Amur region, or wolves, often become more active in the cold. Paths are quieter. Your breath shows in the air. If you only know the zoo in summer, a winter visit can feel like a different attraction altogether.

The Tundra Trek is one of the more recent areas, and it brings you close to the far north. Polar bears are the main draw here. Watch for the deep pool and the underwater viewing windows. When a bear swims past, you can see the long, wide paws working through the water. Their fur looks white from a distance, but up close it often shows a cream or yellow tint.

The zoo has also made headlines over the years. Between 2013 and 2018, a pair of giant pandas lived here on loan from China, and two cubs were born during their stay. Around the same period, the zoo's African elephants were moved to a sanctuary in California. That decision caused public debate in Toronto. Both stories show how zoos keep changing their ideas about what good care looks like.

That change is the second surprise. Much of the work here happens out of sight. The zoo runs conservation and breeding programs for endangered species, including some native to Canada. Turtles, frogs and other small animals from Ontario are part of this effort. Some are raised here and later released into the wild. So the quiet tanks and ponds you pass may matter more than the large animals that draw the crowds.

The land around the zoo matters too. The Rouge Valley is now part of Rouge National Urban Park, the first national urban park in Canada. That means wild forest, wetlands and farmland continue just past the fences. Deer, foxes, herons and hawks live in this valley on their own terms. Keep your eyes on the treetops and the sky. Not every animal you see today belongs to the zoo.

As you walk, pay attention to the scale of things. The African Savanna area opens up into long views across grassland. Giraffes stand above the trees at the edge. Zebras move in tight groups. On a clear afternoon, the light comes in low and gold across the grass. In this part of the zoo, the planning of open space works best, and the animals have room to stay at a distance.

A practical thought. Most people cannot see everything in one visit, and that is fine. It often works better to choose two or three regions and give them time. Sit on a bench. Wait at one enclosure for ten minutes. Animals that seem asleep often wake and move if you are patient. The people who wait are usually the ones who leave with the best stories.

Now, wherever you are standing, take a moment. Look past the nearest enclosure to the trees behind it. Listen again for the sounds you heard at the start, the calls, the wind and the gravel. As you walk on, notice where the zoo ends and the valley begins. Often there is no clear line. This place was built that way on purpose.
