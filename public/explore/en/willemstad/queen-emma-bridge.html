The guide

The story

Feel the planks shift, just slightly, under your feet. The bridge is floating. Below you, the green water of Saint Anna Bay moves in slow swells, and the whole walkway rides them, rising and settling like the deck of a moored boat. Warm wind comes off the Caribbean. Gulls call overhead. Ahead and behind, the town glows in pastel colours, lemon, coral, mint and sky blue.

This is the Queen Emma Bridge, a pedestrian pontoon bridge in Willemstad, the capital of Curaçao. A pontoon bridge does not stand on fixed pillars. It rests on a row of floating hulls, linked together and anchored at the ends. Here it joins the two halves of the old city. On one side lies Punda, the original fortified town. On the other lies Otrobanda, a name that means the other side. Thousands of people walk across every day.

The crossing opened in the late nineteenth century, in 1888. Before that, people paid boatmen to row them over the channel. The bridge was the idea of an American, Leonard Burlington Smith, who served as the United States consul on the island. He was a businessman as much as a diplomat. He also brought ice and electric light to Curaçao. The bridge was named after Queen Emma of the Netherlands, who ruled as regent for her young daughter Wilhelmina.

At first, crossing was not free. Smith charged a toll. And here is a story locals love to tell. The fee, they say, applied only to people wearing shoes. Anyone barefoot could cross without paying, because going barefoot was taken as a sign of poverty. According to the tale, some people who owned shoes took them off before crossing, just to save the coins. Treat it as folklore, but it shows how deeply this bridge sits in local memory.

Now look along the channel toward the open sea. Saint Anna Bay is not a quiet backwater. It is the working entrance to one of the deepest natural harbours in the Caribbean. Tankers, cargo ships and cruise liners all pass through here. So the bridge has to move. When a ship approaches, the whole structure swings away like a gate on a hinge.

This is the moment visitors wait for. One end stays fixed to the shore. At the other end, a small engine house sits on a pontoon. It drives propellers that push the bridge sideways in a long, slow arc until it lies flat along the Otrobanda quay. The channel opens. Then a huge ship glides past, often so close that you can see the crew on deck. Locals gave the bridge an affectionate nickname for this motion... the Swinging Old Lady.

What happens to pedestrians while the Old Lady swings aside? They take the ferry. Small boats shuttle people across the bay whenever the bridge is open. If a whistle sounds and the gate at the end of the bridge starts to close, do not panic. Wait at the edge, watch the show, and take the short boat ride. Many visitors end up enjoying the ferry as much as the bridge itself.

The bridge you stand on today is not the original. Salt water, sun and constant use wear down any structure, and this one has been rebuilt and restored more than once. The most recent major renovation took place in the 2000s. Engineers kept the old floating design and the swinging movement. So the experience of crossing is close to what islanders knew more than a century ago.

Lift your eyes now toward Punda and the waterfront called Handelskade, the trading quay. These merchant houses line the water in bright, cheerful colours. Their gables and arched windows echo the canal houses of Amsterdam, but the colours belong to the Caribbean. Legend has it that an early governor ordered the buildings painted because the glare of white walls gave him headaches. Historians debate that story, so enjoy it as a tale rather than fact.

This view carries real weight. In 1997, the historic area of Willemstad, including the inner city and the harbour, was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List. UNESCO recognised it as a Dutch colonial town that grew in a tropical setting and mixed European plans with local styles. The bridge connects both protected halves. In a sense, you are walking through the middle of the listing.

Further along the bay, look up at the high arch of the Queen Juliana Bridge. It carries cars far above the water, high enough for big ships to pass underneath. The two bridges make a sharp contrast. One is massive, fixed and modern. The other is light, low and moving. Cars took the heavy traffic away, which is why the Queen Emma Bridge is now for people on foot.

Listen to the voices around you. You will probably hear Papiamentu, the island's own language. It mixes Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch, English and African roots. Many people here move easily between three or four languages. On the bridge you may pass schoolchildren, office workers, fishermen and tourists from the cruise ships. For locals, this is not a sight. It is simply the way to the other side.

After dark, the bridge changes character. Strings of lights along its arches switch on, and the reflections spill across the black water. Couples stop to lean on the railings. The facades of Handelskade glow softly across the bay. It is worth coming back in the evening, even if you crossed during the day. Before you step off, pause for a moment. Feel the gentle rise and fall under your feet once more. Notice the smell of salt and diesel, the slap of water against the pontoons, the colours on both banks. When you reach solid ground, look back. You have just crossed a floating road that has carried this city's daily life for well over a century.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wait near the bridge to watch it swing for passing ships.
Take the shuttle ferry when the bridge is open.
Return after dark to see the arches lit up.
Photograph Handelskade from the Otrobanda side in afternoon light.
Hold children's hands as the deck moves with the waves.
