The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. On top of this lone hill above Mukachevo, the air moves differently than it does in the streets below. It comes across the flat plain, meets the stone walls, and turns along the ramparts. Under your feet the ground is uneven, part old cobble, part worn rock. Lift your eyes and the whole valley opens out, with the town, the river and the soft blue line of the Carpathian foothills beyond.

This is Palanok Castle. The hill under it is volcanic, a single rise in otherwise level land. That is the first thing to notice, and it explains almost everything else. Anyone who wanted to watch the roads and the river crossing here needed this hill. For many centuries, different powers fought for it, held it, lost it, and rebuilt it. The castle you see is the result of all those layers placed one on top of another.

The name itself is a clue to its early life. Palanok comes from an old word for a palisade, a wall of sharpened wooden stakes. Before the heavy stone defences, a wooden stockade probably crowned this hill. Wood rots and burns, so almost nothing of that first fortress survives. What remains is the name, carried forward by local people long after the timber was gone.

The figure most closely tied to the castle's early stone phase is Prince Fedir Koriatovych. He was a prince from the Lithuanian ruling family who came to this region in the late fourteenth century. He received Mukachevo and its lands from the Hungarian crown. Local tradition credits him with strengthening the fortress and with building up the town and its monastery. You will find his statue here, and his name appears again and again in local stories.

One of those stories concerns the deep well inside the castle. Legend has it that the well was dug on Koriatovych's orders, and that it took years of labour through hard rock. Some versions of the tale add treasure, curses or prisoners to the story. Treat these as folklore. What is certain is simple and practical. A castle on a dry hilltop could not survive a siege without water, and this well was the castle's lifeline.

As you walk, you will notice the castle is arranged in three main levels. There is a lower castle, a middle castle and an upper castle, each one higher and more protected than the last. An attacker who broke through one gate still faced another wall above. Stop between the levels and look back down the slope. You can see exactly how a defender looked down on anyone trying to climb.

The most famous chapter in the castle's history belongs to a woman. In the 1680s, Ilona Zrínyi, a Hungarian noblewoman, defended Palanok against the armies of the Habsburg emperor. Her husband, Imre Thököly, led an uprising against Habsburg rule, and she held the fortress while he was away. The siege lasted around three years. Inside the walls with her was her young son, Francis Rákóczi. In the end the castle surrendered, but her long resistance became a symbol of courage in Hungarian memory.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. That boy, Francis Rákóczi, grew up to lead his own great uprising against the Habsburgs in the early eighteenth century. This war of independence lasted from 1703 to 1711. Mukachevo and this castle were important to him during the struggle. So the child who watched his mother defend these walls later returned to them as the leader of a national rebellion. Look for the statue of mother and son together. It is one of the most moving images on the hill.

After the uprising failed, the castle changed character completely. Under Austrian rule it became a prison, and it held prisoners for roughly a century. Among those kept here was Alexander Ypsilantis, a leader of the Greek struggle for independence from the Ottoman Empire. It is a surprising link between this quiet corner of the Carpathians and the wars of southern Europe. Thick walls built to keep enemies out were now used to keep people in.

The twentieth century brought new borders again and again. After the First World War, this region became part of Czechoslovakia. It returned briefly to Hungary, then became part of the Soviet Union, and today it lies in Ukraine. The castle saw military use and other practical roles during these years. Each new government left its own marks. That is why the walls carry so many different kinds of repair, from rough old stone to cleaner modern restoration.

Today most of the buildings hold a museum. Inside you will find displays on the history of the castle and the wider region, with weapons, documents, folk costumes and art. The rooms are cool, even on hot summer days, because the walls are so thick. Take a moment in one of the deep window openings. Feel how far you must lean to reach the outer edge of the stone. That depth was the castle's armour.

Now return to the ramparts and look at the view again. Below you lies Mukachevo, a town shaped by Hungarian, Ukrainian, Slovak, Jewish, German and Roma communities over many generations. On a clear day you can follow the line of the Latorytsia River as it runs past the town. The hill gives you the same view that every commander, prince and prisoner had here. The roads and the river have not moved very far.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment on the old stone. Notice the warmth of the sun on the walls, the sound of voices drifting up from the gates, perhaps a bird turning above the towers. This hill has been fought over, defended, surrendered and rebuilt. Today it is simply a place to stand and look. As you walk back down, glance up once more at the walls rising behind you, and the long story still written in their stone.

Practical tips

Bring a light layer for windy ramparts and cool halls.
Visit on a clear day for long valley views.
Allow extra time to explore the museum rooms inside.
Walk all three levels to understand the defences.
