The guide

The story

From almost anywhere in the Old City, you can catch a glimpse of it. A golden dome, lifting above the rooftops, catching the sun. Up close, it seems to glow from within. Blue tiles cover the walls below, patterned like the pages of an open book. This is one of the oldest and most beautiful buildings in the Islamic world.

The Dome of the Rock stands on a wide stone platform in the heart of Jerusalem. Muslims call this place the Haram al-Sharif, the Noble Sanctuary. Jews know the same hill as the Temple Mount. Few patches of ground on Earth carry as much meaning for as many people, and few have been longed for by so many.

The Dome was built in the seventh century, finished around the year 691, under the Umayyad ruler Abd al-Malik. That makes it more than thirteen hundred years old. It is not a mosque in the usual sense. It has no long rows laid out for prayer. It was built as a shrine, to shelter and to honor the bare rock at its center.

That rock is the reason the building exists. It is a large outcrop of stone, the natural peak of the hill. In Islamic tradition, it is linked to the Prophet Muhammad's Night Journey to heaven. In Jewish tradition, the same summit is tied to the binding of Isaac and the heart of the ancient Temple. Beneath it lies a small cave, reached by a few steps, known as the Well of Souls.

Look at the shape of the building. It is an octagon, eight straight walls, with the round dome resting on top. Two rings of columns inside once guided pilgrims around the rock in slow circles. The design draws your eye and your steps along a curve. The form is simple, balanced, and deeply calm.

The bright tiles on the outside are not the originals. The first surface was glass mosaic. In the sixteenth century, the Ottoman ruler Suleiman replaced much of it with painted ceramic tiles. Their blues, greens, and whites have been repaired and renewed many times since. From a distance they read as pure color. Up close, they carry flowing Arabic script and twisting vines.

The dome itself has changed over the centuries. It was once covered in lead, and later in other metals. The gold you see today was added in the 1990s, during a major repair paid for by the king of Jordan. The covering is thin as paper, but in full sunlight it turns the whole crown of the building to fire.

Inside, bands of writing run around the building. Some of these are among the earliest known verses of the Quran set into a wall. The band of script is one of the longest of its kind to survive from that early age. It is more than decoration. It is a message, fixed in gold and glass, meant to be read by anyone who entered.

As you reach the platform, you pass under free-standing rows of arches at the top of each staircase. In local tradition these are called the scales, from a belief that scales will hang here to weigh souls one day. Nearby stands a smaller open dome on slender columns, the Dome of the Chain. It echoes the great Dome beside it, like a sketch made before the finished work.

Today, the inside of the Dome is open only to Muslims. Visitors of other faiths can still come up onto the platform at set hours, through one particular gate. Even from outside, walking the open stone around the building gives you a clear sense of its scale, its color, and its quiet.

The platform around the Dome is wide and open to the sky. Cypress trees give patches of shade. Older people sit and talk in the morning cool. Children cross on their way home from school. The pace here is slower than in the narrow lanes below. The dome above seems to hold the whole square in its calm.

It helps to know that this place is also one of the most contested in the world. Its meaning runs deep for more than one people, and that depth has brought both devotion and conflict. Treat it with care. Speak softly. Dress modestly. You are a guest on ground that many hold holy.

Stand for a moment and watch the light move across the tiles. This building has seen empires rise and fall, and it still stands, still gold, still circling its ancient stone. Few human works have lasted so long while meaning so much. When you leave the platform, that golden dome will follow you across the city skyline.

Practical tips

Dress modestly and carry your passport for entry checks.
Approach through the gate open to general visitors.
Stay respectful and avoid loud talk near prayer times.
