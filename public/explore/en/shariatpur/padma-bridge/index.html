The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the water on the Janjira side and listen. The river has a low, steady sound, the sound of a great volume of water moving without hurry. The air smells of wet silt and diesel from passing boats. And out across the grey-brown surface, a long line of steel and concrete runs toward the far shore until it thins into the haze. This is the Padma Bridge, and from here you can feel how much river it has to cross.

The bridge links two banks that were once separated by hours of uncertainty. On this side lies Shariatpur District, in the south of Bangladesh. On the far side lies Mawa, in Munshiganj District, the gateway toward the capital, Dhaka. The structure runs for a little over six kilometres, which makes it the longest bridge in the country. When it opened to traffic in June 2022, it changed the daily geography of millions of people living in the southwest of Bangladesh.

To understand why, you have to understand the river. The Padma is the name Bangladeshis give to the main channel of the Ganges after it enters the country. It carries water from the Himalayas and the plains of northern India toward the Bay of Bengal. During the monsoon months, it swells enormously. Its currents are fast, and its bed is made of soft, shifting sand and silt. Engineers often describe the Padma as one of the hardest rivers in the world to bridge.

Before this crossing existed, the journey from here to Dhaka depended on ferries and launches. Travellers waited at crowded ghats, the local word for river landing places. In fog, in storms, or when the channel shifted, crossings could be delayed for hours. Trucks carrying fish, vegetables and jute queued along the roads. Families travelling home for religious festivals faced long, sometimes dangerous trips. Accidents on overloaded boats were a painful part of life on this river.

Now look at the shape of the bridge itself. It is a double-deck structure, built from steel trusses resting on tall concrete piers. The upper deck carries a four-lane road. The lower deck, hidden inside the steel frame, carries a railway line. The bridge is divided into forty-one spans, and each span measures about 150 metres. If you count the piers from the shore, you will see how evenly they march out into the water.

Here is something most visitors never think about. The most difficult part of this bridge is the part you cannot see. Because the riverbed is so soft and so deep, the foundations had to reach far down into the earth. The steel piles that hold up the piers were driven more than one hundred metres below the riverbed. At the time of construction, they were among the deepest bridge piles anywhere in the world.

The story of how the bridge was paid for is just as important as how it was built. The project was planned for many years, with support expected from international lenders, including the World Bank. In 2012, the World Bank withdrew its funding after allegations of a corruption conspiracy. A court case in Canada later dismissed the related charges. But by then, the government of Bangladesh had made a bold decision — to build the bridge with the country's own money.

That decision gave the bridge a meaning far beyond transport. For many Bangladeshis, it became a symbol of national self-reliance. The main construction work was carried out by a Chinese engineering company, alongside thousands of Bangladeshi workers. Separate teams built river training works along the banks. These are long protective embankments designed to guide the current and stop the restless river from cutting away the approaches.

The opening day in June 2022 drew enormous public excitement. People travelled from across the country to see the bridge. Some stood on the riverbanks for hours. In the first days, so many visitors stopped their vehicles to take photographs that the authorities had to remind people the deck is a highway, not a viewing platform. Rail services followed later. Trains began running across the lower deck in 2023, connecting Dhaka more directly with the southwest.

For people on this bank, the change was practical and immediate. A journey to the capital that once took most of a day can now take a few hours by road. Farmers and fish traders can move fresh goods to city markets more quickly. Students, patients and workers can travel with more confidence. Economists expect the bridge to help the southwestern region, including the port of Mongla, grow in ways that were difficult when the river was a barrier.

The river, of course, still has its own life. Look out at the water and you will likely see wooden fishing boats with patched sails or small outboard motors. The Padma is famous for hilsa, called ilish in Bengali, a silvery fish that many Bangladeshis consider their national treasure. Near the old ferry landings, small eateries still fry hilsa in mustard oil and serve it with rice. The smell of that frying fish is part of the experience of this riverbank.

As the light changes, so does the bridge. In the early morning, mist often lies on the water and the far end of the structure disappears completely. At midday, the steel looks pale and sharp against the sky. Toward sunset, the river turns copper and gold, and the piers throw long reflections. After dark, the lights along the deck form a bright line across the black water, with the headlights of trucks moving slowly in both directions.

Before you leave, take one more look at the place where the bridge meets the land. Notice the stone-lined banks below the approach, and the way the current curls around the nearest pier. Listen for the hum of tyres overhead and, if you are lucky, the longer rumble of a train passing inside the steel. Then turn back toward the river itself. The boats are still here, still working the water, as they have for generations — now moving beneath a road that finally joins the two shores.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walking on the bridge deck is not permitted for visitors.
View the bridge from riverbank areas near Janjira or Mawa.
Come at sunrise for mist or sunset for warm river light.
Try fried hilsa at eateries near the old ferry landings.
Avoid exposed riverbanks during monsoon storms and strong winds.
