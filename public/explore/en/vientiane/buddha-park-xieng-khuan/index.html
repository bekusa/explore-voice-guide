The guide

The story

Listen first. Under your feet, a dusty path softens into grass. Somewhere nearby, a rooster calls, and a motorbike hums along the road toward the Mekong. Then you look up, and dozens of grey concrete faces look back at you. Gods with many arms. Serene Buddhas. Demons with bared teeth. Every surface is dark with damp and patched with green moss, as if the jungle has been trying to take these figures back for a thousand years. It has not been a thousand years, though. Not even close.

Welcome to Xieng Khuan, a name that translates roughly as "spirit city". Most visitors simply call it Buddha Park. It sits on a riverside plot southeast of central Vientiane, not far from the bridge that crosses into Thailand. And the first surprise is this. Despite the weathered skin of these statues, almost everything you see was made in the second half of the twentieth century. Construction began in 1958. These are not ancient relics. They are the work of one man's vision, and the hands of the people who followed him.

That man was Bunleua Sulilat, a Lao spiritual teacher who was part monk, part mystic, and part showman. He drew freely from both Buddhism and Hinduism, and he wanted a place where the two traditions could stand side by side. According to the story he told his followers, as a young man he fell into a cave and met a hermit who taught him his spiritual path. Whether that happened, nobody can confirm. But it became the founding legend of the park, and it shaped the strange, dreamlike mood of everything here.

Notice the material. These are not carved from stone. They are built from reinforced concrete, shaped by hand over metal frames. Bunleua was not trained as a sculptor, and neither were most of the people who helped him. That is why the proportions sometimes feel a little unusual. A hand might be larger than you expect. A face might lean in a direction no classical sculptor would choose. Rather than a flaw, this gives the park its character. It feels personal, almost homemade, on a grand scale.

Walk toward the long reclining Buddha, which stretches along one side of the grounds. This is one of the largest figures in the park. In Buddhist tradition, a reclining Buddha usually represents the moment of passing into nirvana — the final release from the cycle of rebirth. Look at the calm expression and the head resting on one hand. Stand near the feet and gaze along the full length of the body. The scale only becomes clear when you see people walking beside it, small as birds next to the folded robes.

Now look around for the Hindu gods mixed among the Buddhas. You may spot a figure riding a great elephant with three heads. In Hindu and Southeast Asian tradition, this elephant is Erawan, the mount of Indra, king of the gods. Elsewhere, you will see figures with many arms holding weapons and symbols, likely drawn from deities such as Shiva and Vishnu. For many visitors from outside the region, this blend is surprising. In Laos and Thailand, though, Hindu stories have long lived alongside Buddhist practice in art and festivals.

The most memorable structure here is the giant round building near the entrance. Many people say it looks like an enormous pumpkin. You enter through the open mouth of a demon's head — yes, you walk straight between the teeth. Inside, the space is dim and close. The building is arranged in three levels, which represent hell, earth, and heaven. As you climb, you pass small figures and scenes in the darkness. The air is warm and still, and the walls feel cool when you touch them.

The climb is part of the meaning. You begin in the lowest level, among scenes of suffering, and you move upward through narrow passages and steep steps. Watch your head, and take your time. At the very top, you come out into daylight on a small platform. From there, the whole park spreads out below you. You can see the reclining Buddha, the crowded rows of gods, and the green line of trees toward the river. It is the best view in the park, and the reward for the climb.

Here is the second surprise. Bunleua did not stay in Laos. After the communist government took power in 1975, he left and crossed the Mekong into Thailand. In the town of Nong Khai, on the opposite bank, he began a second sculpture park, known as Sala Keoku. It holds even larger figures in the same unmistakable style. So if you travel on to Thailand, you can see the next chapter of the same story, only a short distance across the river from where you stand now.

Take a moment to look closely at the surfaces of the statues. The tropical climate works hard on concrete. Rain, heat, and humidity leave dark streaks down the cheeks and shoulders. Lichen spreads in pale patches. In places, small cracks show the edges of the original layers. This is why the figures look so much older than they are. Every rainy season adds another layer of age. In a sense, the weather has become a co-author of the park.

The park is also a living place, not only a sculpture garden. You may see local families walking between the statues, children running along the paths, and people leaving small offerings of flowers or incense at certain figures. Vendors near the entrance sell drinks and snacks, and the smell of grilled food sometimes drifts across the grass. For many Lao visitors, this is a weekend outing as much as a spiritual site. The mix of play and devotion feels very natural here.

The light changes the mood of the place through the day. In the middle of the day, the sun is high and flat, and shadows almost disappear. The grey concrete can look pale and dusty. Later in the afternoon, the light comes in lower and warmer, and the faces gain depth and expression. The moss looks greener. If you can, stay a while and watch how the same statue looks different as the sun moves. It is a quiet lesson in how much light shapes what we see.

Before you leave, find a spot in the shade and simply sit for a minute. Look at the crowd of figures around you — the calm Buddhas, the fierce guardians, the gods on their animals. They were made by ordinary people, following one unusual teacher, in a time of great change for this country. As you walk back toward the road, listen again. The rooster, the motorbikes, the wind in the trees. And behind you, a whole city of spirits, standing patiently in the heat.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in late afternoon for warmer, softer light.
Wear shoes with grip for the pumpkin's steep steps.
Bring water and a hat for the open sun.
Climb to the pumpkin's top for the best overview.
Combine the trip with a stop near Friendship Bridge.
