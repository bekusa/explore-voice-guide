The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. Up here on Mont Auxois, it comes across the open plateau with nothing to stop it. Below you, the land falls away into green valleys, hedged fields, and the slow line of small rivers. Cattle graze where armies once dug trenches. Listen for a moment. You may hear a tractor, a church bell from the village, birds in the hedges. It is a quiet place now. But more than two thousand years ago, this hilltop held one of the most important sieges in European history.

This is Alesia. In 52 BC, it was a fortified hilltop town, which the Romans called an oppidum. It belonged to a Gaulish people called the Mandubii. That summer, it became the last refuge of Vercingetorix, a young Gaulish chieftain from the Arverni people of central France. He had done something rare. He had united many rival Gaulish tribes against Rome. Opposite him stood Julius Caesar, governor and general, who had spent years conquering Gaul piece by piece.

Most of what we know comes from Caesar himself. He described the siege in his own account of the war, the Commentaries on the Gallic War. That matters. It is a vivid text, full of detail, but it is also political writing by the winner. Caesar wanted readers in Rome to admire him. So historians read his numbers with care. Still, the broad story is clear, and archaeology on and around this hill has confirmed much of what he described.

Here is the first surprise. Caesar did not attack the hill directly. The slopes were steep, and the defenders were many. Instead, he decided to starve them out. His legions dug a ring of ditches, earth ramparts, wooden walls, and towers around the whole hill. Then Caesar learned that a large Gaulish relief army was coming. So he built a second ring, facing outward. His soldiers worked between two walls — one to hold the Gauls in, one to keep the Gauls out.

Caesar also describes the traps in front of these lines. There were pits with sharpened stakes hidden at the bottom, covered with brushwood. The soldiers gave them a joking name — lilies — because of their shape. There were branches with sharpened ends, buried in trenches. There were iron spikes set in wooden blocks, hidden in the grass. Excavations in the plain below have found traces of ditches and defensive works that match this kind of system. It was engineering as a weapon.

Inside the town, conditions became terrible. Food ran low. According to Caesar, the Mandubii sent their women, children, and elderly out of the town to save supplies. Caesar refused to let them pass through the Roman lines. They were trapped between the two armies. Caesar records this briefly, almost coldly. Standing here, it is worth pausing on that detail. The siege was not only a contest between two great leaders. It was hunger, fear, and suffering for ordinary people.

Then the relief army arrived. For several days, the Romans fought on two fronts at once. Gauls attacked from the hill, and Gauls attacked from outside. The final battle was fought at a weak point in the Roman lines. Caesar writes that he rode into the fight himself, in his red general's cloak, so his men could see him. The Roman cavalry struck the relief army from behind. The Gaulish attack broke, and the outer army scattered.

The next day, Vercingetorix surrendered. Caesar's account is short. Later Roman writers added a more dramatic picture of the chieftain riding out in full armour and laying his weapons at Caesar's feet. That version may be legend. What we do know is grim. Vercingetorix was taken to Rome as a prisoner. Years later, he was displayed in Caesar's triumphal procession and then executed. After Alesia, organised Gaulish resistance faded, and Gaul became part of the Roman world.

Now turn toward the great bronze figure on the western end of the plateau. This is the statue of Vercingetorix, with long hair, a heavy moustache, and a sword resting before him. It was made in the 1860s by the sculptor Aimé Millet. It was a gift of Emperor Napoleon III. Here is the second surprise. Many people have noticed that the face looks rather like Napoleon III himself. An emperor honoured a Gaulish rebel, and gave him something of his own features.

Why was a French emperor so interested in this hill? Napoleon III was writing a history of Julius Caesar. He sponsored large excavations here in the 1860s to find the site of the siege. His teams uncovered ditches, weapons, and coins in the surrounding plain. The inscription on the statue's base celebrates the idea of a united Gaul as one nation. In that age, Vercingetorix became a national hero. He was the first Frenchman, in the popular imagination — even though France did not yet exist.

Was this really Alesia? For a long time, people argued about it. Other places in France, including one in the Jura mountains, were proposed. Today, most archaeologists accept that Alise-Sainte-Reine is the site. Later excavations, including Franco-German work in the 1990s, added more evidence from the siege lines. Up here on the plateau, you can also see the remains of the Gallo-Roman town that grew after the conquest. Low stone walls mark streets, public buildings, and a theatre.

The village below has its own story. Its name comes from Saint Reine, a young Christian martyr from the early centuries of the Church. According to local tradition, she refused to marry a Roman official and was killed for her faith near here. Her legend drew pilgrims for centuries, and a spring linked to her was believed to heal. So this hill holds two layers of memory: a pagan war of conquest, and a Christian story of quiet resistance.

Today, the MuséoParc Alésia helps visitors read this landscape. In the plain below stands a round interpretation centre, designed by the architect Bernard Tschumi. Its circular shape echoes the rings of the Roman siege. Nearby, full-size reconstructions show Roman ramparts, towers, and traps. Up on the plateau, there is a museum building near the archaeological remains. Take your time between them. The real power of this place is the view that links the hill and the plain.

Before you go, look out once more across the valleys. Try to picture the long line of Roman walls circling the low hills. Picture smoke from cooking fires, the sound of digging, the noise of thousands of people waiting. Then let the picture fade, and see what is really there: fields, hedges, roads, a village roof or two. As you walk back past the bronze chieftain, feel the wind again. It has crossed this plateau for a very long time.
