The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere across the grass, a deer pulls at the turf with a soft tearing sound. The park rises and falls in long green waves. Ahead of you, a long grey wall of stone stretches out, with towers, gables and chimneys along the top. It looks less like one house and more like a small town that decided to stay indoors.

This is Knole, on the edge of Sevenoaks in Kent. It is one of the largest houses in England. Its story is less about one owner and more about power: who held it, who took it, and who never let it go. Archbishops, a king, a queen's cousin and a single family have all left their mark here.

The building is made largely of Kentish ragstone. This is a hard, grey local stone, rough and slightly uneven. On a dull day it looks almost blue. When the sun comes out, it warms to a pale honey colour. Run your eyes along the walls and you will see patches of different tone. These patches show centuries of repair, one generation patching what the last one left behind.

The great house began in the fifteenth century. In 1456, Thomas Bourchier, the Archbishop of Canterbury, bought the estate. He rebuilt the house as a palace fit for one of the most powerful churchmen in the land. For decades afterwards, Knole belonged to the archbishops. Medieval bishops were not quiet scholars in small rooms. They were great lords, with large households, guests and political business to run, and this house was built for that kind of life.

Then came Henry the Eighth. In the 1530s, as the king broke with Rome and took control of the English church, he also took control of its property. Archbishop Thomas Cranmer gave Knole to the Crown. The story is that Cranmer offered a smaller house, and the king pressed for the bigger prize. Whatever the exact conversation, the result was the same. The archbishop's palace became a royal house, and Henry spent money enlarging it further.

After the Tudors, the house passed into the hands of the Sackville family. Thomas Sackville was a statesman, a poet and a cousin of Queen Elizabeth the First. He took possession in the early seventeenth century, and the Sackvilles have been connected to Knole ever since. That is more than four hundred years. Today the house is cared for by the National Trust, which took it on in 1946. Members of the family still live in part of the building, so some doors you pass lead to private homes.

Here is a story you will hear often. Locals say Knole is a calendar house — that it has 365 rooms, 52 staircases, 12 entrances and 7 courtyards, one for each day, week, month and day of the week. It is a lovely idea. But treat it as legend. Nobody has produced a reliable count that matches it neatly. The truth is simply that the house is very large, and it grew piece by piece over time.

Inside, the showrooms hold one of the most important collections of seventeenth-century furniture in the country. Much of it arrived through Charles Sackville, the sixth Earl of Dorset. He served as Lord Chamberlain to the royal household. In that role, he was allowed to take old royal furniture when the palaces were refurnished. So chairs, beds and stools that once stood in royal rooms came here instead. Many of them have stayed in place for over three centuries.

One piece of furniture even took its name from this house. The Knole settee is a sofa with tall, hinged arms that can be lowered with ropes or ratchets. Furniture makers around the world still copy the design. When you see one in a modern shop, you are looking at a descendant of a seat from these rooms. Watch also for the textiles. Velvets and silks have faded unevenly, and that soft, worn colour is part of their age.

The Great Staircase is worth a slow walk. Its walls are painted in grey and white, in a style called grisaille, which imitates carved stone with flat paint. Look at the posts of the banisters. Small carved leopards sit on top, holding shields. The leopard is a Sackville symbol, and once you notice it, you will spot the animal again and again around the house.

Now a different kind of story. In 1892, a girl named Vita Sackville-West was born here. She loved Knole deeply. But under the rules of inheritance at the time, the house passed only through the male line, so she could never own it. Her friend, the novelist Virginia Woolf, wrote the novel Orlando in 1928. It is a playful fantasy that follows a hero through the centuries, and it draws on Vita and on Knole itself. Many readers see it as Woolf's gift to a woman who lost her home.

Here is a surprise most visitors miss. In early 1967, the Beatles came to this park to film the promotional film for Strawberry Fields Forever. Scenes for Penny Lane were also shot here. The band gathered around a dead tree and an old piano among these slopes. So the same ground that knew archbishops and Tudor courtiers also knew four musicians from Liverpool in winter coats.

The park around you is around a thousand acres, and it has been a deer park for centuries. Herds of fallow deer and sika deer move freely across it. They are wild animals, even if they seem calm. Give them space, especially in autumn when the males are rutting. Even if you never go inside, a walk here shows you the house the way travellers once saw it: grey stone, rising slowly out of the trees.

Before you go, turn back once more. Look at the long front of the house and the chimneys against the sky. Listen to the grass, the birds and the deer. Behind those walls are rooms where kings' chairs still stand. Out here, the park is quiet, and the stone keeps its pale grey colour as the light changes. Take that view with you as you walk away.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the deer park even if you skip the house.
Keep a respectful distance from deer, especially in autumn.
Wear sturdy shoes for the hilly, uneven park paths.
Allow extra time to walk slowly through the showrooms.
Bring water, as the park walks cover long distances.
