The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here on the edge of the Wetterau, the wind moves across open fields with almost nothing to stop it. Larks rise from the grass. The ground underfoot is soft, chalky, and slightly springy. In front of you, a smooth green mound swells out of the landscape like the curve of a buried shoulder. It looks almost too perfect to be natural... and it isn't. This is Glauberg, one of the most important Celtic sites in all of Europe.

The Wetterau is a gentle, fertile basin to the northeast of Frankfurt. Its soils are rich, which made it attractive to farmers for thousands of years. Above the fields rises a long, flat-topped hill. That plateau was settled again and again, from the Stone Age to the Middle Ages. But the people who made this place famous lived here roughly two and a half thousand years ago. They were Celts of the early Iron Age, and they left behind something no one expected to find.

Here is the first surprise. For most of the twentieth century, nobody knew this burial mound existed. Centuries of ploughing had flattened it almost completely into the fields. Then, in the early 1990s, aerial photographs showed strange circular and linear marks in the crops. From above, the outline of a vast ditched enclosure appeared. Archaeologists began digging in the mid-1990s. What they uncovered changed how scholars think about Celtic society north of the Alps.

Inside the mound, excavators found rich warrior graves. One burial held a man with a gold neck ring, weapons, and fine bronze vessels. Among them was a beaked wine flagon — a jug with a long spout shaped like a bird's beak. Objects like this connect the Celts of Hesse with the wider world of the Mediterranean, where wine drinking was a mark of status. These were not isolated tribes. They were part of long trade networks reaching south across the mountains.

Then came the find that made headlines. In 1996, in a ditch beside the mound, archaeologists found a sandstone statue. It shows a man standing upright, roughly human size, perhaps a little taller. He carries a shield and wears a sword at his side. Around his neck is a heavy ring, very like the gold one from the grave. And on his head sits something extraordinary: a strange headdress with two large, leaf-like shapes rising above his ears.

This headdress is often called a leaf crown. Similar shapes appear on a few other Celtic objects, but the statue gives them a full human face. Nobody knows exactly what the crown meant. Some researchers think it marked a ruler. Others think it marked a priest, or a man who was both. Either way, the statue is one of the finest pieces of early Celtic stone sculpture ever found. People here simply call him the Celtic prince of Glauberg.

Now turn and look away from the mound, out across the fields. Archaeologists found that a long, straight avenue once led towards this burial. It ran for several hundred metres and was lined on both sides by ditches. It may have been a processional way, a route for ceremonies or funerals. Today the line has been marked again in the landscape. Walking it, you follow, more or less, the same path that people walked in the Iron Age.

Here is a second surprise, and it is one many visitors miss. Some researchers believe the ditches around the site may have been aligned with the movements of the moon. The idea is that the layout tracked long lunar cycles, which some ancient peoples watched closely. This theory is debated, and it is not proven. But it is worth keeping in mind as you look at the lines in the grass. The plan of this place may have been about the sky as well as the dead.

The mound you see today is a reconstruction. After the excavations, it was rebuilt to give visitors a sense of its original height and shape. The ring ditch around it has also been recreated. Up close, it feels quiet and very simple. There is no grand stone tomb, no carved entrance. Just earth, grass, and sky. That simplicity is part of its power. You are looking at the same form the Celts chose to honour their dead.

Behind you, or perhaps already in view, stands the museum known as the Keltenwelt am Glauberg, which means the Celtic World at Glauberg. It opened in the early 2010s. The building is a long box clad in rust-coloured steel. One end projects out over the slope, towards the mound, almost like a lens. Inside, a large window frames the burial mound directly. You can stand beside the original sandstone prince and see his grave in the same glance.

The museum displays the finds from the graves: jewellery, weapons, vessels, and the statue itself. Seeing the stone prince in person is very different from a photograph. You notice how the sandstone has weathered. You notice the patterns on his armour, and the calm, slightly blank expression of his face. The galleries also explain the wider Celtic world of central Europe. They show how people here lived, farmed, traded, and buried their leaders.

If you have time, climb up to the plateau of the hill itself. The Celts fortified this summit with walls and ramparts, and traces of those defences still shape the ground. Much later, in the Middle Ages, a castle stood up here too. Today only fragments and foundations remain among the trees. From the top, the view opens across the Wetterau. On a clear day you can see how this hill watched over the whole basin below.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment beside the mound. Feel the wind again. Notice how small the steel museum looks against the open fields, and how the green earth seems to hold everything in place. Somewhere beneath this soil, a man was laid to rest with gold around his neck, and a stone version of him stood watch nearby. As you walk back along the avenue, listen to the larks... and to your own footsteps on that ancient line.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the museum first to see the finds in context.
Walk the processional avenue out and back to the mound.
Wear sturdy shoes for the climb up to the plateau.
Bring a windproof layer; the open fields catch the breeze.
Come on a clear day for long views over the Wetterau.
