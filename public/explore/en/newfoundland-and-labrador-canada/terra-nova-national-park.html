The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind moves through spruce and fir with a steady, dry hiss. Somewhere below, cold salt water slaps against dark rock. The air smells of resin, wet moss, and the sea, all at once. Underfoot, the ground is soft and springy, a deep layer of needles and peat that gives a little with every step. This is the boreal forest meeting the North Atlantic, and you are standing right where the two touch.

Welcome to Terra Nova National Park, on the eastern coast of the island of Newfoundland. The name is Latin, and it means simply "new land". It echoes the name of the island itself. The park was established in 1957, and it was the first national park created in Newfoundland after the province joined Canada. It is also the most easterly national park in the country. When the sun rises here, it reaches this coast before almost anywhere else in Canada.

Look at the shape of the water around you. The coastline here does not run in a clean line. Instead, long fingers of sea reach deep into the land, between steep wooded hills. These inlets are called sounds, and they belong to the wider waters of Bonavista Bay. Newman Sound is the best known, a long arm of sea that runs far inland. On a calm day, it holds the reflection of the hills almost perfectly, like a dark mirror.

That shape is the work of ice. During the last ice age, thick glaciers covered this whole region. They ground down the old rock, scraped out the valleys, and carried boulders far from where they started. When the ice melted, the sea flooded the low ground. What remains is a landscape of ridges, ponds, bogs, and drowned valleys. Many of the large, lonely rocks you may see sitting in the woods were dropped by melting ice thousands of years ago.

Here is a detail that surprises many visitors. The moose you might see along the roadside are not native to Newfoundland. Historical records show that a small number were brought to the island in the early twentieth century, in 1904. With few predators and plenty of food, they spread across the whole island. Today they are a familiar sight, and their browsing has changed the forest itself. In parts of the park, staff work to protect young trees so the forest can regrow.

The forest has quieter residents too. Black bears live here, as do red foxes, snowshoe hares, and beavers in the ponds and streams. One of the rarest animals is the Newfoundland marten, a small, slender relative of the weasel with rich brown fur. It is a threatened species, and the park is an important place for it. You are unlikely to see one, since they are shy and active mostly at dawn and dusk. Still, it is worth knowing they are out there.

Now turn your attention to the open, wet ground between the trees. These are bogs and fens, and they cover a surprising share of the park. They look flat and plain from a distance. Up close, they are full of small detail: cushions of sphagnum moss in red, green, and gold, low shrubs, and tiny orchids in early summer. Look carefully for the pitcher plant. It is the floral emblem of Newfoundland and Labrador, and it catches insects in its water-filled leaves.

The sea is just as alive as the land. The sounds are home to eelgrass meadows, which shelter young fish and other small creatures. Seabirds work the water, and bald eagles and ospreys hunt along the shore. In late spring and early summer, a lucky visitor may even see an iceberg drifting along the outer coast. These icebergs break away from glaciers in Greenland and travel south on the cold Labrador Current. Each one carries ice that formed long before any human settlement here.

People have lived beside these waters for a very long time. Long before European settlement, Indigenous peoples travelled this coast and used its resources. Among them were the Beothuk, the people of Newfoundland whose way of life came to a tragic end in the early nineteenth century. Later, European settlers built small outport communities around Bonavista Bay. They fished, cut timber, and lived closely with the rhythm of the seasons. Some of those communities still sit just beyond the park boundary.

The Trans-Canada Highway runs right through the park. For many travellers, this is their first glimpse of Terra Nova, seen through a car window at speed. But the real character of the place appears when you stop. Short trails lead from the road to lookouts, ponds, and shoreline. Longer routes follow the edge of the sounds for hours. A good place to start is the visitor centre near Newman Sound, where you can learn about the marine life of the bay.

If you climb to a high point, the view opens wide. From the hills, you can see the pattern of the whole landscape: dark forest, silver ponds, pale bogs, and the long blue arms of the sea. The forest here is mostly black spruce and balsam fir, with white birch adding pale trunks and bright autumn colour. In late September and October, the birch and the low shrubs turn yellow and red against the dark evergreens.

The weather can change quickly on this coast. Fog may roll in from the sea in minutes, softening every outline and muffling every sound. A bright morning can turn grey and wet by noon, then clear again by evening. This is part of the experience. In fog, the forest feels closer and quieter. In sun, the water sparkles and the colours sharpen. Either way, it is worth carrying a warm layer and something waterproof.

One more thing many visitors miss: the silence at night. Away from city lights, the sky over Terra Nova can be very dark. On a clear night, the stars stand out sharply over the water. You may hear the call of a loon on a pond, a long, rising, almost human sound. It is one of the most memorable sounds of the northern wilderness, and it carries a long way across still water.

Before you move on, take a moment where you stand. Notice the colour of the water, the texture of the moss, the smell of spruce in the cool air. This coast has been shaped by ice, by the sea, and by the people who lived beside it. It will keep changing, slowly, long after you leave. As you walk back, listen for the wind in the trees. It is the same sound that greeted you when you arrived.

Before you go

Practical tips

Pack a waterproof layer for sudden coastal fog and rain.
Start at the visitor centre near Newman Sound.
Watch for moose on the highway at dawn and dusk.
Visit in late spring for a chance to see icebergs.
Come in early autumn for red and yellow foliage.
