The guide

The story

Listen first. A bell rings somewhere ahead, then another, and the sound carries down the hillside through the trees. The air smells of camphor smoke, crushed jasmine and wet stone. Under your feet the steps are smooth, polished by countless bare feet. You are climbing toward one of the oldest and most loved temples in Andhra Pradesh — the Varaha Lakshmi Narasimha temple on the hill of Simhachalam.

The name gives you the first clue. Simhachalam means, roughly, the hill of the lion. The lion here is Narasimha, one of the forms of the Hindu god Vishnu, who appears as half man and half lion. But this temple adds a twist that most visitors do not expect. The deity worshipped here combines two forms of Vishnu into one — Narasimha, the man-lion, and Varaha, the boar. That pairing is rare, and it shapes everything about this place.

The hill rises on the northern edge of Visakhapatnam, a busy port city on India's eastern coast. From the top, on a clear day, the land rolls away in green folds toward the Bay of Bengal. Pilgrims arrive two ways. Some take the winding road by bus or car. Others choose the long stone stairway, climbing slowly, sometimes pausing at each step to touch it with their hand. That climb is itself a form of devotion.

The story behind the temple is a legend, not documented history, and locals tell it with real affection. According to the tale, a young prince named Prahlada was a devoted follower of Vishnu. His father, a powerful demon king, tried again and again to kill him for that devotion. Each time, Prahlada survived. In the end, Vishnu appeared as Narasimha to protect the boy. Many believe this hill is where Prahlada first built a shrine to his protector.

History, the kind written in stone, comes later. The walls of the temple carry a large number of old inscriptions, carved by rulers and donors over many centuries. Many date from the time of the Eastern Ganga kings, a dynasty that ruled from the region of present-day Odisha in the medieval period. Later inscriptions record gifts from other rulers, including the Vijayanagara emperor Krishnadevaraya, who is said to have visited. Scholars treat these carvings as an archive of the region's past.

Now look at the building itself. The main shrine follows the Kalinga style of architecture — the temple tradition of Odisha and the northern Andhra coast. In this style, the tower above the sanctum rises in a curved, ribbed shape, almost like a tall beehive. The dark stone is covered with dense carving. Look for figures of gods, dancers, animals and floral bands, wrapped around the walls in layer after layer.

Here is something many visitors miss. Most Hindu temples face east, toward the rising sun. This one is widely described as facing west. Priests and pilgrims explain the difference through tradition and local belief rather than any single recorded reason. Whatever the cause, it changes how light falls on the building. In the late afternoon, the sun comes straight toward the entrance and warms the carved stone.

The second surprise waits inside. You will probably not see the face of the deity. For almost the whole year, the image in the sanctum is covered in a thick layer of sandalwood paste. It looks like a smooth, rounded mound, pale and fragrant. Devotees explain that the cool sandalwood calms the fierce energy of Narasimha. The covering also makes the image feel hidden, private, and somehow closer.

Once a year, that covering comes off. During a festival called Chandanotsavam, held around the spring festival of Akshaya Tritiya, the sandalwood is removed. For a short time, pilgrims can see the deity's true form. This view is called Nija Rupa Darshanam, which means the sight of the real form. Huge crowds gather for it. Then fresh sandalwood is applied again, and the image disappears for another year.

As you move through the complex, watch where people stop. Many pilgrims go to a pillar known as the Kappa Stambham. They wrap their arms around it and hold on for a moment, eyes closed. Local belief says the pillar grants wishes, especially for couples hoping for children. You may also notice people with freshly shaved heads. Offering hair is a common vow here, a sign of thanks or surrender.

The hill also holds water. Natural springs flow from the slopes, and one of the best known is called Gangadhara. Pilgrims often wash their hands and feet in spring water before entering the inner shrine. The sound of running water, the cool shade of the trees and the warm stone of the courtyards create a strong contrast. It feels like a quieter world than the port city below.

Before you leave, many visitors collect prasadam, the blessed food offered to the deity and then shared with devotees. Here it usually comes as sweet laddus, round and heavy, wrapped for the journey home. Families eat a little on the spot and save the rest for relatives. In this way, the temple travels far beyond the hill, into kitchens across the city and the wider region.

So pause for a moment where you stand. Notice the scent of sandalwood that seems to hang in the air even outside the sanctum. Listen to the bells, the chanting, the soft slap of bare feet on stone. Look up at the carved tower against the sky. When you walk back down the hill, the city will grow louder with every step... but this sound of bells will follow you a little way.

Before you go

Practical tips

Remove footwear before entering the temple complex.
Wear modest clothing covering shoulders and knees.
Expect very large crowds during the Chandanotsavam festival.
Climb the stairway early morning to avoid midday heat.
Carry a small bag for prasadam laddus to take home.
