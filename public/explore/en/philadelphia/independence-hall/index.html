The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Footsteps on brick, the murmur of a tour group, a bird somewhere in the trees of the square. In front of you stands a red brick building with white trim and a tall white steeple. It looks modest, almost domestic. You might expect something grander for the place where a nation was argued into being. But that modesty is part of the story. This is Independence Hall, and the ideas shaped inside these walls still travel around the world.

The building did not begin as a monument. It was the Pennsylvania State House, the seat of the colony's government. Construction began in 1732 and continued for about two decades. Its style is Georgian, an English fashion of the eighteenth century built on symmetry, balance, and calm proportion. Look at the façade. Windows line up evenly on either side of the central doorway. The brick is laid in a careful pattern. Nothing here shouts. Everything is ordered and steady.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The steeple you see today is not the original one. The first wooden tower rotted and was taken down in the late eighteenth century. For years the building stood without a proper spire. In 1828, the architect William Strickland designed a new steeple, close in spirit to the old one. So when you look up at that white tower, you are seeing a nineteenth-century tribute to the colonial building, not the eighteenth-century original.

In May 1775, delegates from the colonies gathered inside as the Second Continental Congress. Fighting had already broken out in Massachusetts. One of their early decisions, made in this building, was to name George Washington commander of the new Continental Army. Think about that moment. A tall Virginian, chosen in a Philadelphia statehouse, to lead soldiers he had not yet met, against the strongest military power of the age.

Then came the summer of 1776. In a ground-floor chamber called the Assembly Room, the delegates debated a break with Britain. On July 2, they voted for independence. On July 4, they approved the final wording of the Declaration of Independence. Most of the signatures were added later that summer. Four days after its adoption, on July 8, the Declaration was read aloud to a crowd in the yard behind the building.

That yard is the green space around you now. Imagine it without the paths, the benches, and the cameras. Imagine a summer crowd of townspeople, craftsmen, and soldiers, standing in the heat to hear words that most of them had never heard before. Bells rang across the city that day. For people at the time, this was not a sacred text yet. It was news — dangerous, uncertain news.

Eleven years later, in 1787, the Assembly Room filled again. This time delegates came to fix a struggling government. The Articles of Confederation, the first framework of the United States, had left the central government weak. The delegates met through a hot Philadelphia summer. According to accounts of the convention, they kept their talks secret, and the windows were often shut. Picture the stuffy room, the wool coats, the long hours of argument.

The result was the United States Constitution, signed here on September 17, 1787. Washington presided from a carved wooden chair with a half sun on its top rail. James Madison recorded a famous remark by Benjamin Franklin about that sun. During the debates, Franklin had wondered whether it was rising or setting. At the end, he said he now felt it was a rising sun. That chair is original, and it still stands in the room.

Here is a second surprise. Most of the other furniture in the Assembly Room is not original. The British occupied Philadelphia during the war, and much of the room's contents was lost over the years. What you see is a careful re-creation, based on research into what the room held. The rising sun chair is the main survivor. When you stand inside, let your eye find it first. It is the one object that truly watched both signings.

Another survivor is a silver inkstand made by the Philadelphia silversmith Philip Syng. Tradition holds that delegates used it to sign both the Declaration and the Constitution. It is displayed nearby with early printed copies of the founding documents. Across the hall from the Assembly Room is a courtroom that once served the Pennsylvania Supreme Court. Its tall windows and raised bench show how justice was staged in the colonial era.

The building has its own later chapters. When the Marquis de Lafayette, the French general who fought for American independence, returned in 1824, the city welcomed him here. In 1865, the body of Abraham Lincoln lay in state inside the building, and long lines of mourners passed through. In 1979, Independence Hall was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognized for ideas that shaped constitutions far beyond this country.

Look at the two smaller buildings on either side. To the west is Congress Hall, where the United States Congress met in the 1790s, when Philadelphia served as the national capital. To the east is Old City Hall, which housed the early Supreme Court of the United States. Together they form a small campus of early American government. Across Chestnut Street, the Liberty Bell now hangs in its own glass pavilion. It once hung in this building's steeple.

Before you move on, take one more look at the brick. Some of it has been repaired and replaced over the centuries, and you can spot slight changes in colour. Notice the scale of the doorway, made for people on foot, not for parades. Listen again to the square — the footsteps, the voices, the traffic beyond the trees. Ordinary city life runs right up to these walls. That was true in 1776 too. Big ideas were argued here, in a working building, on a busy street, by people who could not know how the story would end.
