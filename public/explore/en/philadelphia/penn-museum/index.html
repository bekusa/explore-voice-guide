The guide

The story

Start with the sound of your own footsteps. They fall on stone, and they echo a little, under ceilings built for silence and slow looking. Outside, traffic hums along South Street and students cross toward the university campus. Inside, the air is cooler, and the light is softer. You have walked into one of the oldest working archaeology museums in North America. And it is still a place where research happens, not only a place where objects rest.

The full name tells you what it does. This is the University of Pennsylvania Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology. Locals simply call it the Penn Museum. It was founded in 1887, and its building opened to visitors at the end of the nineteenth century. From the start, it sent its own teams into the field. Many objects you will see today were not bought from dealers. Penn's own researchers dug them out of the ground.

Before you go deeper, look again at the building itself. The walls are warm red brick, laid with care, and trimmed with stone and decorative tile. The design borrows from the old churches and cloisters of northern Italy. A team of Philadelphia architects shaped it, and they wanted it to feel crafted by hand rather than stamped from a mould. Notice how the brick changes colour slightly from one course to the next. That small variation was part of the intention.

Now, the guest who greets nearly everyone. In the main entrance hall sits a sphinx — the body of a lion, the head of a king. It is carved from red granite. It is the largest sphinx in the Western Hemisphere. Inscriptions name Ramesses the Second, one of the most powerful pharaohs of ancient Egypt. Here is the first surprise. For about a century, this sphinx sat in a lower gallery. Only in 2019 did the museum move it, slowly and carefully, up to the entrance where you see it now.

The sphinx came from Memphis, the ancient capital of Egypt near modern Cairo. It was excavated in the early twentieth century and shipped across the Atlantic. Look at the surface. The granite carries pits, worn patches and the marks of long exposure to sand and water. Some details of the face are softened. Others are still sharp. Walk slowly around it. The king's expression shifts as the light from the windows moves across the stone.

The Egyptian collection goes far beyond one statue. There are coffins, painted with careful eyes, and human remains wrapped in linen. There are tools, jewellery and small figures placed in tombs to work for the dead in the afterlife. Penn also excavated parts of a royal palace built for the pharaoh Merenptah, a son of Ramesses the Second. Massive columns and carved doorways from that palace belong to the museum. Standing beside them, you get a real sense of human scale against royal ambition.

Next, travel east in your imagination, to southern Iraq, to the ancient city of Ur. In the 1920s and 1930s, Penn and the British Museum ran a joint excavation there. The team was led by the British archaeologist Leonard Woolley. They uncovered what became known as the Royal Cemetery. It contained gold, silver, red carnelian and blue lapis lazuli, buried more than four thousand years ago.

The museum holds some of the most remarkable finds from Ur. One is the elaborate headdress of a woman named Puabi, made from gold leaves, rings and strings of beads. Another is a figure often called the Ram in a Thicket. It shows a goat-like animal rearing up against a flowering plant. And here is the second surprise. There were two of these figures. One stayed in London. The other stands here in Philadelphia. The pair was split when the finds were divided.

Spend a moment with the gold. Ancient craftspeople hammered it thin enough to tremble. They had no electric light and no magnifying lenses. Yet the detail is tiny and exact. Lean close to the glass and look at the edges of a single leaf. Then step back and picture the whole headdress on a living person, moving as she walked. Objects like these are not only treasure. They are evidence of skill, trade routes and belief.

Now look up. The museum has a large circular hall under a high dome, built from layered tile. This is the Chinese Rotunda, and its scale changes the mood. Sound moves differently here. Voices rise, soften and drift. In the middle sits a large sphere of clear crystal. According to a popular tradition, it once belonged to the Empress Dowager of China in the late imperial period. The museum treats that story with care, and so should we.

Around the walls of the rotunda, you will find two stone horses carved in relief. They come from the tomb of a seventh-century Chinese emperor of the Tang dynasty, one of China's great ruling houses. The emperor ordered carvings of six warhorses that carried him into battle. Two of those six are here. Look for the arrow carved into one horse, and the soldier who is pulling it free. It is a battlefield moment, frozen in stone.

The museum's reach goes further still. There are galleries for Africa, for ancient Greece and Rome, for the Middle East, and for Mexico and Central America. In the Americas galleries, carved stone monuments from the Maya world stand at full height. Their surfaces carry rows of glyphs — signs that combine sounds and meanings. Researchers spent generations learning to read them. Some of that work happened in this very institution.

Remember that this building is not frozen. Behind the gallery walls are laboratories, storerooms and offices. Conservators clean and stabilise fragile objects. Researchers study bones, seeds, pottery and soil. The collection runs to roughly a million objects, and only a small share is on display at any time. So every gallery you walk through is a carefully chosen edge of something much larger, most of it out of sight.

Before you leave, pause near the entrance once more. Look at the sphinx, then at the red brick and the courtyard outside. Listen for the shift from museum quiet back to city sound. Somewhere a student is hurrying to class. Somewhere a bus is braking. The objects here came from deserts, river valleys and royal tombs across the world. Now they share a single address in West Philadelphia — and for these few minutes, so did you.
