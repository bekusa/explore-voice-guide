The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic on the Benjamin Franklin Parkway hums behind you, steady and close. Then you pass through a stone gateway, and the noise softens. Gravel crunches underfoot. Trees close in. A long, still pool holds the reflection of the sky. Ahead, on a stone pedestal, a bronze man sits with his chin on his hand. He has been thinking in this spot for nearly a century. Welcome to the Rodin Museum, one of the quietest corners of Philadelphia.

This small museum holds one of the largest collections of Auguste Rodin's work anywhere outside Paris. Rodin was a French sculptor who lived from the middle of the nineteenth century into the early twentieth. Many people see him as the bridge between classical sculpture and modern art. He did not carve smooth, perfect gods. He shaped bodies that twist, strain, and doubt. Here you can walk among his bronzes, his plaster studies, and his drawings. You can see how one restless mind worked over a lifetime.

The collection exists because of one man, Jules Mastbaum. He was a Philadelphia businessman who made his fortune with a chain of movie theatres. In the 1920s, he fell in love with Rodin's work. He began to buy sculptures, drawings, and letters with great energy. Mastbaum wanted a museum for his home city, a gift to the public. Here is the sad part. He died in 1926, before the building was finished. His widow carried the project forward, and the museum opened its doors in 1929.

Now look at the gateway you just passed through. It is not an invention of Philadelphia's architects. It copies a real façade in France. Rodin lived and worked for many years at Meudon, just outside Paris. On his property there, he rebuilt the old stone front of a ruined château, the Château d'Issy. Rodin is buried near that spot, with a cast of The Thinker above his grave. So this entrance is a quiet echo of the place where the sculptor rests.

The building and gardens were designed by two Frenchmen, the architect Paul Cret and the landscape designer Jacques Gréber. Both men also worked on the plan of the Parkway itself. Their style here is Beaux-Arts, a French tradition of symmetry, order, and classical detail. Notice the pale stone, the balanced wings, the calm rhythm of columns and arches. Nothing shouts. The architecture steps back so the sculptures can take the stage. Even the garden paths lead your eye straight toward the art.

Let us return to the man on the pedestal. The Thinker is perhaps the most recognised sculpture on Earth. But here is something many visitors never learn. He did not begin as a philosopher. Rodin first imagined him as a poet, often linked with Dante, the Italian writer of the Inferno. The figure was meant to sit high above a pair of enormous doors and look down on the souls of the damned. Only later did he take on a life of his own as a symbol of human thought.

Those doors are here too. Walk toward the building and you will find The Gates of Hell, set into the main façade. In 1880, the French state asked Rodin to design bronze doors for a planned museum of decorative arts. That museum was never built. Rodin kept working on the doors anyway, for decades, adding and removing figures. He never saw them cast in bronze. Mastbaum ordered the first bronze casts. One came to Philadelphia, and another went to Paris.

Stand close to the Gates and let your eyes adjust. At first, the surface looks like a storm of shapes. Then the bodies appear. Figures fall, cling, reach, and tumble through the frame. Some are tiny. Some seem ready to break free from the bronze. Above the doors sits a small version of The Thinker, in his original place. Many of Rodin's famous single works began life as figures on this door. Lovers, mourners, and wanderers were all born here first.

One of those figures is a couple wrapped in an embrace. Rodin later enlarged them into the sculpture known as The Kiss. They are often identified with Paolo and Francesca, two lovers from Dante's poem. In the story, their forbidden love sends them to hell. On the Gates, they belonged to that grim world. Standing alone, they became one of the most tender images in Western art. It is a strange journey for a single idea, from damnation to devotion.

Inside, the galleries feel bright and intimate. Light falls from above onto bronze and plaster. Take your time with the smaller works. You will see hands without bodies, heads without shoulders, and torsos without limbs. Rodin did this on purpose. He believed a fragment could carry as much feeling as a complete figure. Look for the marks of his fingers and tools in the surfaces. Rodin wanted you to see the work of making, not hide it.

Rodin also worked closely with bronze foundries. He usually modelled in clay, and craftsmen then made moulds and cast the bronzes. This is why the same sculpture can exist in several museums at once. A cast in Philadelphia is not a copy in the usual sense. It comes from the artist's original model. As you move around the rooms, notice how the dark patina changes in the light. Some surfaces look almost wet. Others look rough as bark.

Step back outside into the garden. Over the years, the grounds lost some of their original look. In the early twenty-first century, the gardens were restored to follow the design that Gréber had planned. The trees, hedges, and reflecting pool frame the sculptures like rooms without walls. Watch how the light moves on the bronze as clouds pass. Morning light is soft and even. Late afternoon brings long shadows and warm colours across the Thinker's shoulders. Each visit can look a little different.

The museum is part of a larger story along the Parkway. This grand avenue runs from City Hall toward the Philadelphia Museum of Art, which also cares for this collection. Along the way, you pass fountains, flags, libraries, and other museums. The Parkway was planned as a Paris-style boulevard in an American city. In that sense, the Rodin Museum fits perfectly. It is a small piece of France, placed on purpose, in the middle of Philadelphia's cultural mile.

Before you go, find a bench or a quiet patch of path. Look once more at the Thinker, the stone gateway, and the dark doors behind him. Listen to the gravel under passing shoes and the city humming just beyond the trees. One collector's passion, one sculptor's restless hands, and one quiet garden came together here. As you walk back to the Parkway, notice how the noise returns. Then notice that you carry a little of this stillness with you.

Practical tips

Circle The Thinker slowly to see changing expressions.
Pair your visit with a walk along the Parkway.
Sit by the reflecting pool before entering the galleries.
Spend extra minutes studying the Gates of Hell up close.
