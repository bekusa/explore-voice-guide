The guide

The story

Stand on the edge of Logan Square for a moment and listen. Behind you, the water of the Swann Memorial Fountain splashes and hisses. Traffic hums along the Benjamin Franklin Parkway. And in front of you rises a wall of dark brownstone, warm in sunlight and almost purple in shade. Above it sits a copper dome, weathered to a soft green. This is the Cathedral Basilica of Saints Peter and Paul. It is the mother church of the Catholic Archdiocese of Philadelphia, and its story begins in fear.

In 1844, Philadelphia saw violent riots. Groups known as Nativists, who were hostile to Catholic immigrants, especially the Irish, attacked Catholic neighbourhoods. Two Catholic churches in the city were burned. People died in the streets. For the Catholic community, it was a deep shock. They had come to a city founded on religious tolerance, and they had watched their churches burn. Only two years later, in 1846, work began on this cathedral. It was a statement: we are here, and we are staying.

Now look up at the side walls of the building. Notice something unusual about the windows. In many great churches, large windows sit at eye level, filled with coloured glass. Here, the windows are placed high on the walls, well above the street. According to a widely repeated account, this was deliberate. The builders wanted the glass out of reach of stones thrown by angry crowds. Historians debate how much this shaped the design, but the story has stayed with the building for generations.

The cathedral took nearly two decades to build. Construction started in the 1840s and the church was dedicated in 1864, while the American Civil War was still being fought. Much of the money came in small amounts from ordinary working families, many of them Irish immigrants. Two architects are linked with the project. Napoleon LeBrun led the design, and John Notman is credited with the grand front facade. Together they gave Philadelphia something it had not seen before.

That something is the Italian Renaissance style. Renaissance means rebirth — the period when European builders returned to the forms of ancient Rome. The design draws on church architecture in Rome itself. So look at the front. You see tall columns with ornate capitals, a heavy triangular pediment above them, and clean, balanced proportions. There are no pointed Gothic arches here, no spiky towers. Instead, the building feels calm and solid, almost like a temple. Many people say it looks more Roman than American.

At the top of the facade, and around the building, you will find references to the two saints in its name. Saint Peter and Saint Paul are the great apostles of Rome. Peter is often shown with keys, the keys of heaven. Paul is often shown with a sword, a reminder of his martyrdom. Naming an American cathedral after these two saints was a way to tie Philadelphia's Catholics directly to Rome and to the oldest traditions of their faith.

When you step inside, the change is sudden. The noise of the parkway drops away. The air feels cooler and carries the faint smell of candle wax and old stone. Your eyes need a moment to adjust. Then the space opens up around you — a long, wide nave leading to the altar, under a ceiling heavy with gilded detail. Light falls from those high windows in soft beams. It is the largest Catholic church in Pennsylvania, and you can feel its scale at once.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Some of the paintings in this church are linked to Constantino Brumidi. He was an Italian-born artist who later became famous for painting the inside of the dome of the United States Capitol in Washington. Before and during that work, he also painted for churches. So the same hand that decorated the heart of American government also left its mark on this Catholic cathedral. Take a slow look at the painted surfaces around the sanctuary.

Now walk toward the dome. Stand under it if you can, and look straight up. The dome gathers light and pulls your eye upward, as Renaissance builders intended. Around you, side chapels hold altars, statues, and quiet corners where people kneel to pray. You may see someone lighting a candle, or sitting alone in a pew. This is not a museum. It is a working church, with daily Masses, weddings, and funerals. Moving softly is part of the experience.

The cathedral has welcomed popes. Pope John Paul the Second celebrated Mass here in 1979, during his first visit to the United States as pope. Decades later, in 2015, Pope Francis also celebrated Mass here, when he came to Philadelphia for a large international gathering of Catholic families. Few churches in the country can say two popes have stood at their altar. In 1976, the church received the title of basilica, an honour given by the Vatican to churches of special importance.

The cathedral is also a place of burial. Beneath and within it rest several of the city's bishops and archbishops. In 2018, the remains of Saint Katharine Drexel were moved here. She was a Philadelphia heiress who gave away a great fortune. She spent her life founding schools for Black and Native American children. She is only the second American-born person to be named a saint. Many pilgrims come to the cathedral just to pray at her tomb.

When you go back outside, notice how the building fits its setting. In the twentieth century, Philadelphia built the Benjamin Franklin Parkway, a wide, tree-lined boulevard inspired by the grand avenues of Paris. Museums and libraries now line it. The cathedral, much older than the parkway, became one of its anchors. Look across Logan Square and you will see other civic buildings facing it. A church born in a time of riots now sits at the centre of the city's cultural life.

Before you leave, stand once more by the fountain and turn back toward the facade. Look at the dark brownstone, the columns, the green dome against the sky. Then look at the high windows again, set far above the pavement. Think of the families who gave their small coins to build this place, so soon after their churches burned. As you walk away, listen. The fountain is still splashing. The traffic still hums. And the cathedral stays exactly where they wanted it — here, and staying.
