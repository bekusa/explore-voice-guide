The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Cars hum along Linwood Avenue, and a bus sighs to a stop somewhere nearby. The houses here are low, with tin roofs, wooden fences, and small front gardens. The building in front of you does not try to stand out. It looks much like its neighbours. That modesty matters, and it is a good place to begin.

This is the Linwood Islamic Centre, a mosque in the eastern suburbs of Christchurch. Before anything else, remember that this is a working place of worship. People come here to pray five times a day, to meet friends, and to raise their children within a community of faith. If you have come to pay respects, you are standing where many others have stood before you. Please keep your voice soft, and let the space belong to those who use it.

Linwood is an ordinary, hard-working part of the city. Many families here live in weatherboard homes, the timber-clad houses typical of New Zealand. The eastern suburbs carried some of the worst damage from the Canterbury earthquakes in 2010 and 2011. Roads cracked, homes sank, and whole streets near the river were later cleared. People who live here know about rebuilding. That shared experience became part of the story of this mosque.

For years, the centre served a small and diverse congregation. Its worshippers came from many backgrounds — Afghan, Somali, Fijian Indian, Malaysian, Arab, and New Zealand-born converts, among others. In a smaller mosque like this one, people tend to know each other by name. Friday prayers draw the biggest crowd of the week. That is when the room fills, shoes line the entrance, and conversation drifts out into the street afterwards.

Then came Friday the 15th of March, 2019. A gunman attacked the Al Noor Mosque, a few kilometres west near Hagley Park, during Friday prayers. He then drove across the city to Linwood. Here, he opened fire on worshippers. Seven people were killed at this mosque. Across both sites, fifty-one people lost their lives, and many more were wounded. It was the deadliest mass shooting in New Zealand's modern history.

What happened next at Linwood surprised many people around the world. Abdul Aziz Wahabzadah, a worshipper who had come to New Zealand as a refugee from Afghanistan, ran outside toward the attacker. Reports say he grabbed the first thing he could find — a small card payment machine — and threw it. He then picked up an abandoned weapon and chased the gunman back to his car. His actions are widely credited with stopping further deaths here.

He was not alone. Other worshippers inside helped the injured, shielded each other, and tried to warn those still praying. Many of their stories came out later, through court hearings and the press. The courage at Linwood came from ordinary people on an ordinary Friday. They were taxi drivers, students, fathers, and grandfathers. That is perhaps the detail most visitors miss. Heroism here did not wear a uniform.

The response across New Zealand was immediate. Within weeks, Parliament passed a law banning most military-style semi-automatic weapons. A government buyback scheme followed. Around the country, people left flowers, notes, and soft toys outside mosques. Here in Linwood, the fences and footpaths filled with tributes. Many women of other faiths wore headscarves in a public gesture of solidarity during the days that followed.

In August 2020, the attacker was sentenced to life imprisonment without the possibility of parole. It was the first time a New Zealand court had ever handed down that sentence. During the hearing, survivors and families spoke directly to the court. Some spoke with anger, some with grief, and some with forgiveness. A Royal Commission of Inquiry, which is an independent public investigation, also examined how the attack had happened and what the state could learn from it.

The mosque reopened to worshippers not long after the attack. Since then, the building has been repaired and renewed. Like many places marked by violence, it now carries two roles at once. It is a memorial, and it is a living mosque. On a typical day, you will see the second role far more often than the first. Children arrive with parents. Elders greet each other at the door. Someone always seems to be checking their watch before prayer.

If you are not Muslim and wish to visit inside, it is best to contact the centre beforehand. Many mosques in New Zealand welcome guests outside prayer times, but customs apply. You will be asked to remove your shoes. Modest clothing is expected, covering arms and legs. Women are often asked to cover their hair. A small gesture of respect, like a quiet greeting, goes a long way here.

Step back now and look at the street itself. Notice how close the mosque sits to the houses beside it. There are no great walls, no grand plaza, no tall tower. This closeness is part of what made the attack feel so personal to Christchurch. It happened in a neighbourhood, among people's homes, on a road many locals drive every day. For many residents, Linwood Avenue will never feel quite the same.

And yet, the neighbourhood carries on. Buses keep their schedules. Kids ride bikes along the footpath. Neighbours wave over fences. In the years since 2019, the mosque community has hosted open days, shared meals, and interfaith gatherings with churches and other groups across the city. Grief has not disappeared. But it now sits alongside friendship, which many here say is the truest memorial of all.

Before you walk away, take one more quiet look. See the doorway, the shoes perhaps lined up outside, the plain walls in the soft Canterbury light. Listen again to the traffic, the birds, the ordinary sounds of a suburb at work. This is what the people of Linwood chose to protect — not a monument, but daily life. As you leave, you carry a small part of that care with you.
