The guide

The story

Before you see the castle, you feel the climb. The path tilts upward under your shoes, and the stone underfoot is dark, rough and grey-black. This is not ordinary limestone. It is volcanic rock, cooled long ago from molten lava. Above you, walls and towers grow straight out of the cliff. From down here it is hard to tell where the mountain ends and the fortress begins.

That dark rock explains almost everything about this place. Southeastern Styria, the province of Austria you are standing in, sits on an old volcanic landscape. Locals call the region the Steirisches Vulkanland, the Styrian Volcano Land. Millions of years ago, eruptions pushed up hard basalt and volcanic stone. Softer ground around it wore away over time. What remained were steep, isolated hills — natural towers in the middle of rolling farmland. Riegersburg stands on one of the most dramatic of them.

Now imagine you are an attacker. You arrive across open fields, and every approach to the summit is steep. On several sides the cliff drops almost vertically. The only practical route is the one you are probably walking now — a long, winding path that passes through a series of fortified gates. Each gate could be closed and defended on its own. An enemy who broke through one would simply face the next, often under fire from the walls above.

This matters because of where Riegersburg sits on the map. For centuries, this corner of Styria was a frontier. To the east lay Hungary, and beyond it the expanding Ottoman Empire. Raiding parties crossed into these valleys again and again, burning villages and taking captives. Fortresses like this one gave the local population somewhere to run. Riegersburg earned a reputation as one of the strongest strongholds on the border. Tradition holds that it was never taken by force.

The person most associated with that strength is a woman. In the seventeenth century, the castle came into the hands of Katharina Elisabeth von Galler, remembered locally simply as the Gallerin. She was a formidable figure. She married three times, fought long legal battles, and had a sharp reputation among her neighbours. She also poured money and energy into the fortress. Under her direction, the defences were greatly expanded into much of what you see today.

Her story has collected many tales. Some are documented, and others are clearly the colourful exaggerations that gather around any strong personality. Locals still describe her as stubborn, fearless and difficult. Whatever the truth of each anecdote, one thing is clear. She shaped this castle at the moment when the Ottoman threat was very real. The thick walls, bastions and gate towers along your climb are, in large part, her legacy.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Since the early nineteenth century, Riegersburg has belonged to the princely family of Liechtenstein — the same family that gives its name to the small country in the Alps. The family still owns the castle today. So when you walk these halls, you are not in a state museum in the usual sense. You are in a private fortress that a noble family has opened to the public.

Inside, the mood changes. The defensive outer works give way to residential rooms, with painted ceilings, carved woodwork and heavy doors. Exhibitions trace the life of the castle and its owners. Look at how thick the walls are where window openings cut through them. Those deep recesses show how much stone stands between you and the outside world. Even in summer, many rooms stay cool, and the air carries the faint smell of old timber.

One exhibition turns to a much darker chapter. In the seventeenth century, this region saw witch trials, as did much of central Europe. Accused men and women were interrogated, tortured and in some cases executed. One of the best-known figures connected with Riegersburg is a woman remembered as the flower witch. According to the popular tale, she could make flowers bloom out of season. That gift, so the story goes, was used as evidence against her. The exhibition treats these events as a human tragedy, not a spectacle.

It is worth pausing on that contrast. The same walls that protected people from raiders also stood in a society gripped by fear of an invisible enemy. Neighbours accused neighbours. Rumours turned into trials. Standing here, you can hold both stories at once — the fortress as shelter, and the age as one of terrible suspicion. The castle does not hide that second story, and that honesty is part of what makes a visit here memorable.

The fortress also carries scars from a much more recent war. In the last weeks of the Second World War, fighting reached this part of Styria, and the castle suffered damage. It was later repaired. As you move around the outer walls, notice places where the stonework looks slightly different in tone or texture. Repairs from different centuries sit side by side, and the masonry shows a long history of patching, rebuilding and care.

Now find a viewpoint on the walls and look outward. On a clear day, the land rolls away in every direction — vineyards, orchards, patches of forest and small villages with church spires. Other volcanic hills rise here and there across the countryside. Some carry their own castles. The view helps you understand why this summit mattered. Watchmen up here could see danger long before it arrived, and signal fires could carry warnings across the valleys.

The area below the castle has its own pleasures. The village of Riegersburg gathers at the foot of the rock, with cafés and local producers. This part of Styria is known for pumpkin seed oil, a dark green oil with a nutty taste, and for its fruit and wine. Nearby there is also a well-known chocolate maker that draws its own stream of visitors. Many people combine the castle with a slow afternoon of tasting and wandering.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and put your hand on the stone. It is the same volcanic rock that rose from the earth long before any human arrived. People shaped it into gates, towers and living rooms. They defended it, feared within it and repaired it again and again. As you walk back down the path, listen to your footsteps on that dark ground... and notice how the castle slowly sinks back into the mountain behind you.
