The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and breathe in. The air in Santoña carries salt, seaweed and, on some mornings, the faint oily smell of the anchovy canneries near the port. Gulls call over the rooftops. Somewhere a door creaks, and a bell may mark the hour. This is a town that has always lived facing the water, and the place in front of you belongs to that same quiet, working rhythm.

This place carries the name Our Lady of Covadonga. That name did not begin here. It travelled east along the Cantabrian coast from the mountains of Asturias, the neighbouring region to the west. I want to be honest with you from the start. Detailed records about this particular local dedication are hard to confirm, so I will not give you an invented founding date or builder. Instead, let us look at the story behind the name, and at the town around you.

Covadonga itself is a cave high in the Picos de Europa, a range of sharp limestone mountains. According to a story told for more than a thousand years, a nobleman named Pelayo gathered a small band of fighters there. Tradition dates their battle against Moorish forces to around the year 722. Legend has it that the Virgin Mary helped the outnumbered Christians win. Historians still debate how large that fight really was, but its symbolic weight in Spain is enormous.

In Asturias, people often call this Virgin by an affectionate nickname — La Santina, which means something like the little saint. Her small image sits inside the cave, above a waterfall and a pool. She is the patroness of Asturias, and for many Spaniards she stands for the very beginning of the long Christian reconquest of the peninsula. So when you see her name here, on the Cantabrian shore, you are seeing a piece of that mountain story carried to the sea.

Why would a fishing town honour a Virgin from a mountain cave? Dedications like this one often followed people. Families moved between the ports and valleys of northern Spain for work, for marriage, for the fishing seasons. Devotions travelled with them, the way recipes and songs do. A name like Covadonga could feel like a link to home, or simply a sign of respect for a figure the whole north of Spain shares.

Take a look at the building itself now. Notice the materials. Is it bare stone, or stone covered with pale render that has faded in the damp sea air? Look at the doorway, at the way rain and salt have marked the lower walls. On this coast, buildings take a beating from wind and moisture. Small repairs, patches of newer mortar and repainted woodwork are all part of how a place like this survives.

If the doors are open, step inside gently. Churches and chapels here are still living places of prayer, not museums. You may smell candle wax and old wood. Light from the windows often falls softly in this northern climate, grey one minute and golden the next. Look for the image of the Virgin herself. Many representations of Our Lady of Covadonga show her holding the child Jesus, dressed in a richly decorated robe.

Now think about the town outside. Santoña is one of the best known fishing ports of Cantabria, and it is famous across Spain for its anchovies. Here is something many visitors do not know. In the late nineteenth century, salting specialists from Italy, many of them from Sicily, came to this coast. They brought methods for curing anchovies, and local workers refined them. The anchovy fillet in oil, now a delicacy, owes a great deal to that meeting of traditions.

Here is a second surprise. Santoña claims as its son one of the great mapmakers of the age of exploration — Juan de la Cosa. He sailed with Christopher Columbus, and around the year 1500 he drew a world map that is among the oldest surviving maps to show the lands of the Americas. Scholars still discuss parts of his life, but the town holds his memory with real pride. You may notice his name on streets and monuments as you walk.

Above the town rises Monte Buciero, a rocky headland covered in green woods and cut by cliffs. For centuries its position made Santoña a natural stronghold guarding the bay. During the Napoleonic wars of the early nineteenth century, French troops occupied the town and strengthened its defences. Old forts and batteries still sit on the slopes and at the water's edge. A walk along the coast takes you past stone walls built for cannons, now quiet and overgrown.

On the other side of town, the land turns soft and flat. The marshes of Santoña form one of the most important wetlands on Spain's northern coast. Twice a day the tide fills and drains the channels. Migrating birds stop here to rest and feed, among them the elegant spoonbill, a white bird with a long, flat beak. If you have time, the marshes are a calm contrast to the stone of the old town.

So Santoña lives between two worlds — the sea and the mountain, the fishing boat and the cave. Its main patroness is the Virgin of the Port, honoured with her own feast, which tells you how closely faith and the sea are tied here. Our Lady of Covadonga adds a different note. She is a mountain Virgin in a sea town, a reminder that the whole north of Spain shares one long story.

Before you move on, take one more look. Notice the colour of the walls in this light, the sound of the street, the smell of salt drifting in from the harbour. People have stopped here to pray, to light a candle, or just to rest from the wind. As you walk away toward the port or the hills, listen for the gulls. They have been circling these rooftops far longer than any of us.
