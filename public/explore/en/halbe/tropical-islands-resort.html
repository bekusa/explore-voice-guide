The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere above you, very far above, there is a roof. Down here the air is warm and heavy, and it smells of damp earth, chlorine and green leaves. Your skin feels it first, a soft humidity that has nothing to do with the Brandenburg sky outside. You are in the flat pine country south of Berlin. Yet the light around you is filtered, pale and diffused, the way it might fall through cloud over a tropical coast.

Welcome to Tropical Islands Resort, near the small town of Halbe. Before we walk any further, look up. The structure that holds all of this was never meant for palm trees or swimming. It was built for airships. That single fact explains almost everything strange about this place. It explains the height, the curve, and the enormous open floor with no pillars at all.

The story begins with the land itself. This ground was once a military airfield, used by Soviet forces during the decades when this part of Germany belonged to the German Democratic Republic, the old East Germany. After reunification in 1990, the Soviet troops left, and the airfield stood empty. The long runways and open space made it attractive for a very ambitious idea.

Around the turn of the millennium, a German company called CargoLifter set out to bring back the airship. Not for passengers, but for freight. The plan was a giant, lighter-than-air craft that could lift very heavy loads, such as turbines or industrial machinery, and carry them to places without roads or ports. To build such a craft, they needed a hangar large enough to hold it. So they built this one.

The hall is usually described as one of the largest freestanding halls in the world. It runs roughly 360 metres long and more than a hundred metres high, with no internal columns to break the space. The frame is a series of steel arches covered with a membrane. Think of it as a huge half-cylinder lying on its side. From the motorway, it rises out of the forest like a silver loaf, and drivers see it from far away.

Here is the first surprise. The airship was never finished. CargoLifter ran into serious financial trouble and went into insolvency in 2002. The hangar stood complete, but empty. A building made for one purpose suddenly had no purpose at all. For a while, it seemed this giant shell might simply wait in the pine forest with nothing inside.

Then came a buyer from very far away. A Malaysian company, Tanjong, bought the hall and decided to fill it with a tropical world. The idea was simple, and a little bold: bring the climate of Southeast Asia to a region with long, grey winters. The resort opened at the end of 2003. Since then it has changed owners again, and today it belongs to a Spanish leisure group, Parques Reunidos.

Now turn your attention back to ground level. The interior is kept warm all year, roughly the temperature of a summer day by the sea. That is the second surprise. When it snows outside, people here walk barefoot in swimwear. Heating a space this size is a serious engineering task, and keeping the humidity right for the plants is just as important as keeping the air pleasant for people.

The heart of the hall is the rainforest. Thousands of tropical plants grow here, from tall palms to broad-leaved shrubs and climbing vines. Walk slowly along the paths and notice how the planting creates layers, a low ground cover, a middle level of bushes, and a high canopy. Some of the buildings among the trees take their style from Southeast Asian villages, with carved wood and steep roofs. They help turn one huge room into a sequence of smaller places.

Then there is the water. A wide sandy beach curves along a large, shallow pool, sometimes called the tropical sea. Beside it, a smaller lagoon area offers warmer water and a quieter mood. Above the beach, a tall tower carries water slides, and you will hear the splash and the shouts long before you see who is coming down. The sand under your feet is real sand, and it stays warm.

Outside the hall, the resort has added an open-air area for the warmer months, with more pools and green space. In summer, you can step out of the dome into real sunshine and real wind. It is worth doing once, just to feel the contrast. On one side is the controlled climate of the hangar. On the other side are the pines and sandy soil of Brandenburg, the landscape that was here long before any airship plans.

Many visitors also choose to stay overnight. There are rooms and lodges, and in some areas guests can sleep in tents inside the hall. Imagine lying awake under that vast roof, with the rainforest dim around you and the air still warm. At night the sounds change. The voices fade, the water stills, and the building feels even larger in the dark.

It is easy to treat this place simply as a pool day. But it is also a quiet lesson in how cities and regions reuse what history leaves behind. A Soviet airfield, then a failed dream of flying freight, then a tropical holiday under steel. Each chapter came from a different idea about the future. Very few buildings carry so many different plans inside one shell.

So before you dive back in, pause one more time. Look up at the curve of the arches and try to picture a great airship resting where the palms now stand. Then look down at the sand, the water, the children running toward the slides. As you leave later and see the pine forest again, glance back at the silver shape on the horizon. It was built to send something into the sky... and instead, it kept a small piece of the tropics here on the ground.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring your own towel and flip-flops for walking around.
Visit on a cold winter day for the strongest contrast.
Arrive early to claim a spot on the beach.
Try the outdoor pools in summer for real sunshine.
Consider an overnight stay to see the hall at night.
