The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, the wind moves across gravel and sand with a dry, steady hiss. The ground under your feet is a crust of small stones, pale and sharp-edged. Ahead of you, a long limestone ridge rises from the desert floor. In the morning its face looks almost white. By late afternoon it turns the colour of weak tea. This is Jebel Faya. At first it seems like an ordinary hill in the Sharjah interior... but it holds one of the oldest stories in Arabia.

The word jebel simply means mountain or hill in Arabic. Faya is the local name of the area. Together they describe a modest ridge, not a towering peak. What makes it remarkable is not its height. It is what lies at its foot. Along the base of the slope, archaeologists found rock shelters. These shallow overhangs gave shade and protection from the wind. For people crossing this land long ago, that shade could mean survival.

Here is the first surprise. Excavations at the foot of this ridge uncovered stone tools that are more than one hundred thousand years old. These were not made by farmers or herders. They were made by hunter-gatherers in the Old Stone Age, a period archaeologists call the Palaeolithic. The tools were found in deep layers of sediment beneath the shelters. Each layer is like a page in a very old book, with the oldest pages at the bottom.

The excavations were carried out over many years by a team working with the Sharjah authorities and German and British researchers. In 2011, the team published their findings in the journal Science. Their conclusion was bold. They argued that early modern humans may have reached southeast Arabia much earlier than many scholars had believed. The tools at Faya, they said, looked similar to tools made in East Africa during the same distant period.

Why does that matter? For a long time, a common view held that modern humans left Africa in one main wave, later and further north. Jebel Faya added weight to another idea — that people may have crossed into Arabia by a southern route, perhaps near the narrow mouth of the Red Sea. The debate is not fully settled. Scientists still argue about who made these tools and when. But this ridge sits right at the centre of that conversation.

Now look around and try to imagine a different landscape. The desert you see today was not always this dry. Climate in Arabia has swung back and forth over hundreds of thousands of years. In wetter periods, there were lakes, rivers, and grassland across parts of the peninsula. Animals moved through, and people followed them. In drier periods, the land emptied. The layers at Faya seem to record these rhythms, with signs of people arriving during greener times.

This is the second surprise. The sediments here do not show one single visit. They show repeated occupations across a very long span of time, with gaps in between. Later layers hold tools from much more recent periods, including the Neolithic, or New Stone Age, when people began herding animals. So the same patch of shade under this ridge drew human visitors again and again, separated by many thousands of years. The rock stayed. The people came and went.

In 2025, this area was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List as the Faya Palaeolandscape. The name is important. The value is not only in one shelter or one trench. It is in the whole landscape — the ridge, the plains, the old surfaces where tools still lie scattered. The listing recognised the region as a record of how early humans lived in harsh desert conditions over a vast stretch of time.

The nearest place to understand all this is the Mleiha Archaeological Centre, a short drive away. The centre explains the finds from Faya and from the wider Mleiha area, which also has later sites from the Bronze Age, the Iron Age, and the period before Islam. Visits to the excavation areas are usually arranged through the centre with a guide. That matters, because the sites are fragile, and the most important ground does not look important to an untrained eye.

The landscape around Faya is worth your attention on its own terms. To one side, the red-orange dunes of the Sharjah interior roll away toward the horizon. Close by, other limestone outcrops rise from the plain. One of them, often called Fossil Rock, holds the remains of ancient marine creatures. These fossils are far older than any human tool. They come from a time when this whole area lay under a shallow sea.

Think about those two layers of time together. First, the sea. Then, much later, the limestone lifted and weathered into ridges like this one. Then, only very recently in geological terms, human beings arrived and sat in the shade of that same stone. They knocked flakes from cobbles to make blades and scrapers. When you see a stone tool in a display case, remember that someone held it in their hand, right here, and used it to cut, scrape, and survive.

If you stand quietly, you will notice how open the view is. There are few trees, few buildings, and very little noise apart from the wind and the occasional car on a distant road. In the heat of midday, the light is flat and hard, and the ridge seems to lose its shape. Early morning and late afternoon bring long shadows. The texture of the rock face becomes clear, with its ledges, cracks, and hollows where the shelters lie.

Before you go, look once more at the base of the ridge. It is easy to miss — a line of shade, a few overhangs, some loose stones. Nothing here shouts for attention. Yet this quiet ground has changed how scientists think about the human journey out of Africa. As you walk back across the gravel, listen to it crunch under your shoes. People have crossed this same plain for a very long time... and you are now one of them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the Mleiha Archaeological Centre to understand the finds.
Arrange site visits through the centre with a guide.
Come early morning or late afternoon to avoid the heat.
Carry plenty of water and wear sun protection.
Wear closed shoes for loose gravel and uneven ground.
