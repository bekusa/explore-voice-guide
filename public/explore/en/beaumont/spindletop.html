The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The ground here is flat, the grass is short, and the air off the Gulf coast feels heavy and damp. Wooden buildings stand in a loose row, their boards weathered grey. Above them rise tall timber derricks, open lattice towers against a wide Texas sky. It looks quiet now. But on one winter morning more than a century ago, this patch of coastal prairie shook, roared, and turned black.

You are at Spindletop, just south of Beaumont, in southeast Texas. The name belongs to a low mound that once rose above the surrounding marsh and prairie. Beneath that mound sat a salt dome, a huge column of salt pushed slowly upward through layers of rock over millions of years. Geologists now know that salt domes like this one can trap oil and gas against their sides. In the late nineteenth century, very few people believed it.

One of the believers was a local man named Pattillo Higgins. He had no formal training as a geologist. Even so, he noticed things on the hill that others ignored. Gas seeped from the ground. Springs smelled of sulphur. Higgins became convinced that oil lay below, and he helped form a company to drill for it. He even planned a model industrial town and named it Gladys City, after a young girl from his Sunday school class.

The early attempts failed. The sandy ground collapsed into the drill holes, and many people in Beaumont laughed at the idea. Then Higgins found a partner. Anthony Lucas was a mining engineer born on the Adriatic coast, in what was then the Austro-Hungarian Empire. Lucas had worked on salt domes in Louisiana. He agreed with Higgins about the hill, and he brought fresh determination, along with backing from Pittsburgh oilmen.

The drilling crew included the Hamill brothers, experienced drillers who solved some of the problems with the loose sand. They used heavy mud to keep the hole open — a technique that would later become standard across the oil industry. Progress was slow and frustrating. The crew pushed deeper through the winter of 1900, while money and patience ran low.

Then came January 10, 1901. Mud began to bubble out of the hole. Pipe shot up through the derrick. For a few moments there was silence. Then oil burst out of the ground in a dark column that rose high above the top of the derrick. The crew had never seen anything like it. Neither had anyone else in America.

This was the Lucas Gusher. It flowed uncontrolled for about nine days before workers managed to cap it. Oil pooled across the prairie in lakes. Estimates from the time suggest the well produced far more oil each day than any well the country had seen before. Reporters, speculators, and the curious rushed in by train to see it with their own eyes.

Here is something many visitors miss. Before Spindletop, oil was mostly a product for lamps. Kerosene lit homes, and the supply seemed limited. The Lucas Gusher showed that oil could exist in enormous, cheap quantities. That changed the economics of fuel. Ships, railways, and later automobiles could run on petroleum. In many ways, the age of oil as the world knows it began on this coastal prairie.

Beaumont changed almost overnight. The town's population multiplied within months. Land prices soared. Derricks crowded the hill so closely that people said you could walk across the field by stepping from one platform to the next. Living conditions were rough. The boomtown filled with mud, saloons, gamblers, and fire risk. Fortunes appeared and vanished in weeks, sometimes in days.

Several major companies trace their roots to this field. Gulf Oil and The Texas Company, later known as Texaco, both grew out of the Spindletop boom. Production fell quite quickly after the first rush, because so many wells drained the same reservoir. Yet in the 1920s, deeper drilling on the flanks of the dome started a second boom. Later still, the salt dome was worked for sulphur.

That later activity led to a second surprise. All that extraction from beneath the ground caused the hill itself to sink. The mound that gave Spindletop its name has largely disappeared. Today the original site is low and marshy, and the land looks nothing like the rise that Higgins once walked across. The name, by the way, has its own story. Locals tell several versions, including one about heat shimmering over the hilltop like a spinning spindle — but these are popular tales, not documented fact.

The buildings around you belong to the Spindletop-Gladys City Boomtown Museum, on the edge of the Lamar University campus. It is a reconstruction, built in the 1970s, of the kind of town that sprang up during the boom. Walk inside the clapboard structures and you will find a general store, a saddlery, an oil company office, and other businesses of the era. Their tools, bottles, and furniture show daily life in a town built on hope and crude oil. Nearby stands a tall granite monument honouring the Lucas Gusher.

As you walk on, look up at the wooden derricks and imagine them packed shoulder to shoulder across the prairie. Smell the damp coastal air, and picture it thick with oil and smoke instead. Notice how ordinary this ground seems now. Under a field much like this one, a single well changed how the world travels, works, and lives. Take a last look at the timber towers before you leave — and think about the fuel that carried you here today.

Practical tips

Bring insect repellent for the humid coastal prairie.
Wear comfortable shoes for walking between the boomtown buildings.
Allow time to explore inside each reconstructed shop and office.
Ask staff whether a demonstration gusher is scheduled today.
