The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the Gardon river moves over smooth pale rocks, and the cicadas hum from the scrub on the hillsides. Then lift your eyes. Three rows of golden limestone arches stand across the valley, stacked one on top of another. The stone is warm in colour, rough under the hand, and marked with small pits and stains. For almost two thousand years, this bridge has stood right here, over the same water.

This is the Pont du Gard. It was never meant to be a bridge for people. It is part of a Roman aqueduct, a long artificial channel that carried fresh spring water to the city of Nîmes. In Roman times, Nîmes was called Nemausus, and it was a prosperous town in the province of southern Gaul. The aqueduct was built in the first century after Christ, most likely around the middle of that century.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. The structure in front of you is only a short section of a much longer system. The water began at springs near the town of Uzès, to the north. From there, the channel wound through hills and valleys for about fifty kilometres. Most of it ran close to the ground or underground, hidden from view. Only here, where the Gardon cut a deep valley, did the engineers need to build something this tall.

Now think about the slope. The Romans had no pumps for a job like this. Gravity did all the work. Across the whole route, the channel drops only about twelve metres in total. That is a gentle fall of a few centimetres for every hundred metres of distance. If the slope were too steep, the water would rush and damage the channel. If it were too flat, the water would stop. The surveyors had to hold that tiny gradient over fifty kilometres of uneven ground.

Look at the three levels. The lowest tier has six large arches, and it stands in the river itself. The middle tier has eleven arches. The top tier has many small arches, and it carries the water channel along its crown. Together, the three levels reach close to fifty metres above the river. This makes it the tallest aqueduct bridge the Romans are known to have built anywhere.

Now look closely at the stones. Many of the large blocks in the lower tiers were laid without mortar. They stay in place because of their weight and their careful cut. Some of these blocks weigh several tonnes each. The stone came from a quarry close by, along the river, so the builders did not need to haul it far. Workers lifted the blocks with wooden cranes. These cranes were powered by people walking inside large wheels, a little like a hamster wheel.

Here is the second detail worth noticing. Scan the faces of the piers and you will see stones that stick out from the wall, like small knobs or ledges. The builders left them there on purpose. They supported the wooden scaffolding and the frames that held the arches during construction. When the work was done, nobody removed them. You can also find marks and numbers carved by the Roman masons. These helped workers place each block in the right position.

Now imagine the water itself. The channel at the top is covered, and it is roughly the height of a person. By modern estimates, the aqueduct delivered tens of thousands of cubic metres of water to Nîmes each day. That water filled public fountains, baths, and private homes. Over time, minerals in the water built up thick crusts of limestone along the inside walls of the channel. Maintenance became harder, and over the following centuries the aqueduct slowly stopped working. By the early Middle Ages, the water no longer reached the city.

The bridge survived, but its purpose changed. During the Middle Ages, local people used the lower tier as a crossing over the river. To make room for carts and animals, some of the piers on the middle tier were cut back. This weakened the structure, and for a time it was in real danger. Some people also took stones from the aqueduct for other buildings. Large parts of the long channel elsewhere disappeared in this way.

In the eighteenth century, engineers built a new road bridge right against the lower tier, on the downstream side. You may be walking on it now. This addition finally gave travellers a safe crossing, and it took the traffic off the Roman structure. In the nineteenth century, the monument was restored, and the damaged piers were repaired. In 1985, the Pont du Gard was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

There is a human layer here too. Over many centuries, travellers and craftsmen carved their names, dates, and symbols into the soft stone. Among them were journeymen stonemasons from the French guild tradition, who travelled from town to town to learn their trade. For them, visiting the aqueduct was a kind of professional pilgrimage. Some of their carvings are still visible today. They are a record of respect from later builders for the work of the Roman ones.

Take a moment to see the aqueduct from different angles. From the riverbank below, the arches look heavy and tall. From farther upstream or downstream, the three tiers line up and the full rhythm of the arches becomes clear. In summer, people swim and sit on the rocks in the Gardon, just as people have done here for generations. In the late afternoon, the low sun turns the limestone a deeper gold.

Before you leave, look once more at the top row of small arches. That narrow line along the crown carried water for centuries, without a single motor. Listen to the river again, and the cicadas in the trees. The water that once flowed across the sky now runs only below. As you walk away, turn back once. Notice how the bridge sits low and steady in the valley, as if it has always belonged to it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk down to the riverbank for the full view.
Visit in late afternoon for warmer light on stone.
Bring water and sun protection on hot summer days.
Wear shoes with grip for the rocky riverside paths.
Check ahead if you want to walk the upper channel.
