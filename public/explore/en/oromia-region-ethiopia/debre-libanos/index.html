The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves up from the gorge below, carrying the call of birds and, now and then, the low murmur of chanting. Under your feet the ground is red earth and worn stone. Around you, pilgrims in white cotton shawls walk slowly, some barefoot, some pausing to kiss a doorframe or a wall. This is Debre Libanos, one of the most important monasteries in Ethiopia, and for many centuries a centre of the country's spiritual life.

The monastery sits on the high plateau north of Addis Ababa, in the Oromia Region. It occupies a shelf of land at the foot of a cliff, close to the edge of a deep gorge. Behind it, the rock rises steeply. In front, the land falls away into a valley whose streams eventually feed toward the Blue Nile. The setting matters. For centuries, Ethiopian holy men chose places like this — remote, high, and close to water — for prayer and retreat.

The story of Debre Libanos begins in the thirteenth century with a monk named Tekle Haymanot. He is one of the most honoured saints of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. That is the ancient Christian church of Ethiopia, which traces its roots back to the fourth century. Tekle Haymanot is remembered as a preacher and a founder of monastic life in this part of the highlands. Tradition holds that he came to this place and lived in prayer in a cave above the present church.

Now for the first surprise. Look at paintings of the saint inside the church or in the small shops nearby. You will often see a figure with wings, and with only one leg. According to a popular tradition, Tekle Haymanot prayed standing for so many years that one of his legs broke and fell away. Legend says God then gave him wings. It is a story of devotion, told and painted again and again, and you will soon learn to recognise him.

Over the centuries, Debre Libanos grew into far more than a single retreat. It became the head of an important network of monasteries. Its abbot held a powerful position in the Ethiopian church and at the royal court. Emperors and nobles sent gifts here. Some important figures chose to be buried here. For a long period, the monastery shaped religious education, politics, and the daily faith of many people across the highlands.

The building in front of you is not medieval. Earlier churches on this site were damaged or destroyed during wars over the centuries. The present church was built in the twentieth century under Emperor Haile Selassie, the last emperor of Ethiopia. It has a dome, a tall bell tower, and a strong, symmetrical shape. It looks very different from the small round churches you may see in Ethiopian villages. That contrast tells you something about the period — an emperor building a modern landmark for an old faith.

If you step inside, take a moment for your eyes to adjust. Light falls through stained-glass windows in deep blues, reds, and golds. The windows and wall images show saints, scenes from the Bible, and episodes from Ethiopian religious history. Look for Tekle Haymanot among them. Ethiopian religious art uses large eyes and strong outlines, and holy figures usually face the viewer directly. Bad characters are often shown in profile. This is the second surprise many visitors miss — once you know it, you can read the paintings like a code.

There is also a darker chapter here, and it should be told plainly. In 1937, during the Italian occupation of Ethiopia, there was an attempt to kill the Italian viceroy in Addis Ababa. A wave of brutal reprisals followed. The Italian authorities suspected that people at Debre Libanos had links to the attack. In May of that year, Italian forces killed a large number of monks, deacons, and pilgrims connected to the monastery. Historians still debate the exact number, but it was many hundreds, and some research suggests far more.

For a long time, this massacre was little known outside Ethiopia. In recent decades, historians and journalists have studied it more closely, and it is now recognised as one of the worst crimes of that occupation. When you walk the quiet paths here, remember that this peaceful place carries that loss. Many visitors stand silently for a moment. That simple act of attention is a good way to honour those who died.

Now look up at the cliff behind the church. A path climbs toward the cave that tradition links to Tekle Haymanot. Pilgrims make this walk to pray and to collect holy water from a spring. You will see people carrying plastic bottles and jerrycans, filling them, and sometimes washing their faces or arms. For believers, this water has healing power. The climb is steep in places, so go slowly, and watch your footing on loose stones.

Around the monastery, you will notice the human texture of a living holy site. There are priests with hand crosses, which believers kiss for a blessing. There are small stalls with candles, icons, and prayer books. On major feast days, especially those dedicated to Tekle Haymanot, very large crowds of pilgrims come here. The hillside fills with white shawls, singing, and drums. On an ordinary day, it is much calmer, and you can hear the wind again.

The landscape beyond the church is worth your time too. Not far away, an old stone bridge crosses a stream above the gorge. Locals often call it the Portuguese Bridge, although it is generally thought to be much later than the Portuguese presence in Ethiopia. From the edge of the gorge, you may see waterfalls after the rains. On the cliffs, look for gelada monkeys, a species found only in the Ethiopian highlands. The males have a bright red patch of skin on the chest.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Notice the light on the dome and the dark line of the cliff above it. Notice the pilgrims, the bottles of holy water, the soft sound of prayer from an open door. Debre Libanos has seen saints, emperors, war, and grief, yet it remains a working place of faith. As you walk away, listen for the wind rising from the gorge. It was here long before the first church, and it is still here now.

Practical tips

Ask permission before photographing pilgrims or priests.
