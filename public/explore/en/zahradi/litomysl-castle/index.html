The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment in the courtyard and let your eyes adjust. The walls around you are covered in a fine grey-and-white pattern, like stitching on a heavy coat. Up close, the surface feels slightly rough under the light. Arches open on every side, stacked in neat tiers. Sound behaves differently here. Footsteps soften, and voices drift upward into the galleries. This is Litomyšl Castle, on the hill above the town in eastern Bohemia, the historic western half of today's Czech Republic. The quarter at its foot is Záhradí.

The castle you see today is a Renaissance building, and that word matters here. The Renaissance was the period when European builders looked back to ancient Rome and Greece for ideas of order, symmetry and proportion. In Bohemia, this style arrived largely through Italian craftsmen. They brought with them a taste for open arcades, sunlit courtyards and balanced façades. Litomyšl is one of the clearest examples of that northern journey of Italian ideas. In 1999, UNESCO added the castle and its grounds to the World Heritage List.

Construction began in the 1560s and ran into the early 1580s. The patron was Vratislav of Pernštejn, a member of one of the most powerful noble families in the Czech lands. He spent time at the Habsburg court and travelled widely. He married a Spanish noblewoman, and he wanted a residence that showed his place in a wider European world. The design and building work are linked to the Aostalli family, Italian builders who worked in Bohemia. The result was less a fortress than a statement of taste.

Now look again at that grey-and-white pattern. It is called sgraffito, from the Italian word for scratching. Craftsmen laid down a dark layer of plaster, then covered it with a lighter one. While the top layer was still fresh, they scratched through it to expose the darker layer underneath. Here the main motif is a simple illusion. Each panel looks like a block of cut stone with a pointed, faceted face. From a distance, the walls seem to be built of carved masonry. In fact, it is all plaster.

This is the first surprise most visitors miss. The castle is often described as carrying around eight thousand of these panels. Locals like to say that no two are exactly alike. Walk slowly along one wall and test that claim for yourself. Some panels hold small figures, animals or plant shapes inside the faceted frame. Others are plain. The variation is quiet, and you only notice it once you start looking. The castle rewards patience more than speed.

The courtyard arcades are the heart of the building. The lower levels open in rounded arches on slender columns. The proportions feel light, almost airy, for such a large house. On a summer evening, the courtyard turns into an open-air stage. Each year the town hosts the Smetana opera festival, and much of it takes place right here. Orchestras play beneath the arches, and the galleries above fill with listeners. The architecture works as a concert hall, which suits a building made for display.

That festival name points to the second surprise. The composer Bedřich Smetana was born here, at the castle, in 1824. He was not born in the grand rooms, though. He was born in the castle brewery, where his father worked as the brewer. Smetana later became one of the founding voices of Czech national music. His cycle of symphonic poems called My Country is still played across the world. The brewery building near the castle now holds an exhibition about his life. A palace and a brewery, side by side, shaped one of the country's great composers.

After the Pernštejn family, the estate passed through other noble owners over the following centuries. Among them were the Waldsteins, an old Bohemian aristocratic family. Each generation adapted the interiors to its own fashion. The outer shell stayed largely Renaissance, but the rooms inside changed. That is why a tour of the interior can feel like a walk through several eras at once. Painted ceilings, later furniture and period decoration sit inside walls that are much older.

One of those later additions is a real treasure. Inside the castle there is a small theatre from the late eighteenth century. It is one of the few theatres of that age in Europe to keep much of its original stage machinery and painted scenery. The sets were painted by Josef Platzer, a respected stage designer of the period. These are flat painted panels that slide in and out to change a scene from a forest to a palace. Seeing them is a rare look at how theatre worked before electric light.

Step back outside and walk to the edge of the castle hill. From here the town opens below you. Litomyšl has one of the longest town squares in the Czech lands, lined with arcaded houses. You can trace a clear link between castle and town. The arcades in the courtyard echo the covered walkways along the square. In rain or strong sun, people still walk under those arches below. The hill also holds churches, a former monastery and gardens, so the castle is part of a larger ensemble.

Take a moment to notice the materials around you. The pale plaster changes colour with the weather. On bright mornings it looks almost white. Under cloud it turns soft grey, and the scratched pattern deepens. Late afternoon light from the side makes the faceted panels throw tiny shadows. That is when the illusion of carved stone works best. If you have a camera, try shooting along a wall at a low angle rather than straight on.

The building has also been carefully restored in recent decades. Conservation work on sgraffito is slow and delicate. Specialists clean, consolidate and sometimes recreate damaged areas by hand, using the same scratching technique. Look closely and you may notice that some panels seem slightly fresher than their neighbours. That difference is not a flaw. It shows a living building, maintained by people who still practise an old craft.

Before you leave, stand once more in the middle of the courtyard. Look up at the three tiers of arches and the square of sky above them. The sounds of the town are faint here, and the walls hold the quiet. Four centuries ago, a nobleman wanted this space to say something about who he was. Today it says something different. It shows how ideas travel, how craft survives, and how a brewer's son could grow up in the shadow of a palace. As you walk down the hill, glance back once. The grey-and-white walls will still be there, catching whatever light the day gives them.
