The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Footsteps echo off stone, and voices drift in from the narrow streets around you. The walls here are a warm, honey-grey sandstone, softened by the damp Asturian air. In the morning the light comes in low and pale. In the afternoon it slides across the courtyard and turns the stone almost gold. You are standing at the historic heart of the University of Oviedo, one of the oldest universities in Spain.

The story begins with one man and his will. Fernando de Valdés Salas was an Asturian churchman who rose to great power in sixteenth-century Spain. He served as Archbishop of Seville and as Inquisitor General, the head of the Spanish Inquisition. Near the end of his life, he left money in his will to found a place of learning in his home region. It was a gift from a powerful, controversial figure to a remote corner of the kingdom.

The road from bequest to classroom was long. A papal bull, a formal document from the Pope, approved the new university in 1574. But money, legal disputes and construction delayed the opening for decades. Teaching finally began in 1608. That gap is worth remembering. Valdés Salas never saw a single student walk through these doors. The building you see began as a promise that others had to keep.

Look at the architecture around you. The style is restrained and orderly, with clean lines and very little decoration. This sober approach was typical of Spanish building in the late Renaissance. The Renaissance was the period when European builders returned to the proportions of ancient Rome. Here, that idea appears in the rhythm of the arches and the balance of the façade. Nothing shouts. The building simply stands, solid and calm.

If you can, step into the cloister. A cloister is an enclosed courtyard surrounded by covered walkways, a design borrowed from monasteries. Here it creates a quiet square of sky in the middle of the city. Arches repeat around all four sides, and the walkways throw cool shadow on the stone floor. In the centre stands a statue of Valdés Salas himself. The founder finally keeps watch over the university he paid for but never visited.

For its first centuries, this was a small, regional university. Then, in the eighteenth century, it became linked with a remarkable thinker. Benito Jerónimo Feijoo was a Benedictine monk who taught here for many years. He wrote long series of essays that attacked superstition and false beliefs. He argued for observation, reason and common sense. In a Spain still cautious about new ideas, his books reached a huge audience. Many historians see him as a key figure of the Spanish Enlightenment.

Here is something many visitors miss. Feijoo was not from Asturias at all. He was born in Galicia, the region to the west. Yet Oviedo adopted him completely, and his name appears across the city today. If you walk the old streets after your visit, watch for his name on a square and on a monument. A monk from a quiet cloister became one of the city's proudest symbols.

In the late nineteenth century, the university became famous again. A group of professors, later called the Oviedo Group, taught here together. They believed knowledge should not stay inside university walls. Around 1898, they began a programme they called university extension. They gave free lectures and classes to workers, miners and ordinary townspeople. It was an early attempt in Spain to open higher education to everyone, not only the wealthy.

One of the most famous names connected with these halls is Leopoldo Alas, who wrote under the pen name Clarín. He taught law at the university. He is best known for a long novel called La Regenta, set in a fictional city named Vetusta. Readers have long recognised Oviedo behind that fictional name. So as you walk these streets, you are also walking through the pages of one of the great Spanish novels of the nineteenth century.

Now comes the hardest chapter. In October 1934, Asturias was shaken by a revolution led largely by coal miners. Fighting reached the centre of Oviedo. During those violent days, the university building was set on fire. The flames destroyed much of the interior. The library suffered terribly, and large parts of a collection gathered over centuries were lost. For a place built on books, it was a deep wound.

The building you see today is largely the result of reconstruction after that fire. The rebuilders kept the historic character, so the stone and the courtyard still feel old. That is the second surprise. Much of what looks original was carefully restored in the twentieth century. Look closely and you may notice small differences in the tone and finish of the stone. The surface carries both the old building and the repair.

Today, the university is far larger than this single building. Most teaching now happens on modern campuses in other parts of the city and the region. Thousands of students study medicine, law, science and engineering there. This historic building plays a different role now. It serves as the ceremonial and symbolic centre, the place for official events and important occasions. It is the university's memory, rather than its daily classroom.

The streets around you are worth a slow walk. The cathedral is only a short distance away, and its tall Gothic tower rises above the rooftops. To the other side lies a large green park called Campo de San Francisco, full of old trees and shaded paths. Students, families and older residents cross this neighbourhood all day. Cafés fill with conversation, and the sound of Spanish blends with the clatter of cups.

Before you leave, look once more at the stone around you. It has seen the will of an archbishop, the essays of a monk, the lectures of reformers and the smoke of a revolution. And still, students pass through, carrying bags and phones and coffee. Notice the courtyard light as it shifts, and the quiet under the arches. Then step back out into the street, and let the city carry you on.
