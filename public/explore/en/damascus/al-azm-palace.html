The guide

The story

Step through the doorway, and the noise of the market drops away almost at once. The air feels cooler here. Water splashes softly into a stone basin, and somewhere above you, birds move through the branches of a citrus tree. Underfoot, the courtyard paving is smooth and patterned, polished by many years of footsteps. You have walked only a few paces from the crowded lanes of old Damascus, yet this feels like a different world.

This is the Al-Azm Palace. It was built in the middle of the eighteenth century, around 1749 and 1750, for As'ad Pasha al-Azm. He was the Ottoman governor of Damascus, ruling the city on behalf of the sultan in Istanbul. The Azm family was powerful in Syria at that time, and several members held high office. This house was meant to show that power. But it shows it quietly — through stone, water and shade, not through a grand street front.

In fact, that is the first surprise. From the outside, a traditional Damascene house shows the street very little. The walls are plain, and the entrance is modest. All the beauty faces inward, toward the courtyard. This was a deliberate choice. It protected family privacy, and it kept the summer heat outside. Many old houses in this city follow the same idea. The palace simply takes it to a larger and richer scale.

Now look at the walls around you. Notice the bands of colour running across them — dark stone, pale stone, and warm honey-coloured stone in alternating rows. This striped style is called ablaq, a technique used across Syria and Egypt for centuries. The dark layers are usually basalt, a volcanic rock. The pale layers are limestone. Together they give the courtyard a calm, steady rhythm, almost like lines of music written across the walls.

The house is divided into separate parts, and this tells you a great deal about life here. One section was for receiving guests and conducting business. In Ottoman houses, this public area is often called the selamlik. Another, more private section belonged to the family. This is usually called the haramlik — the family quarters, where women and children lived away from visitors. There were also service areas for the cooks and staff who kept the household running.

As you move through the courtyards, look for the iwans. An iwan is a large, high, open room with one side facing the courtyard, like a stage set into the wall. Families sat here in the evenings, when the stone had cooled and a breeze moved through the house. The iwan usually faces north, away from the strongest sun. It is a simple idea, but it worked very well in a hot climate long before air conditioning.

The water is the second thing to notice. Fountains and pools sit at the centre of the courtyards. The sound of water was valued for its own sake, but it also helped cool the air. Fruit trees and climbing plants added shade and scent. If you stand still for a moment, you will hear how the sounds change from one courtyard to the next — a louder splash here, a quieter trickle there.

The palace also has its own private bathhouse, or hammam. A hammam is a steam bath with rooms of rising heat, a tradition in this region long before the Ottomans. Most Damascenes used public bathhouses in the city. Having a private one at home was a real luxury. Look up when you reach it. Small glass openings in the domed ceilings let in thin points of light, which once fell through the steam onto the bathers below.

Inside the reception rooms, the decoration becomes richer. You will see painted wooden ceilings, carved and inlaid panels, and coloured marble on the floors and walls. Damascus was well known for this kind of craftwork. Some accounts say that As'ad Pasha gathered materials from older buildings in the city for his new house. That story is often repeated, so treat it as tradition rather than firm record. Either way, the quality of the work is clear to see.

The palace has had a long and sometimes difficult life. It stayed with the Azm family for generations. In the early twentieth century, during the French mandate over Syria, it passed into French hands and housed a French institute. During the uprising against French rule in 1925, the old city came under heavy attack, and the palace was badly damaged. It was later repaired, and much of what you see today comes from that long work of restoration.

After Syrian independence, the building came under state care. It became home to a museum of popular arts and traditions. This is the third surprise for many visitors. The rooms are not empty. They hold scenes from everyday Damascene life — costumes, musical instruments, household objects, and figures that show weddings, coffee gatherings and traditional crafts. In this way, the palace of a governor now tells the story of ordinary city life.

The neighbourhood around you is part of the story too. The Umayyad Mosque, one of the oldest great mosques in the world, stands a short walk to the north. The spice and sweet sellers of the nearby market have worked these lanes for generations. As'ad Pasha also built a large merchants' inn close by, known as Khan As'ad Pasha. The palace, the market and the inn were all part of one living city centre.

Before you leave, find a quiet corner of the main courtyard and sit for a few minutes. Watch how the light moves across the striped stone. Notice where the shade falls, and how the water catches the sun. This house was designed for exactly this kind of slow time. When you step back into the lanes outside, listen for the change — the market voices returning, the narrow street closing in, and the calm of the courtyard staying with you a little longer.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with the nearby Umayyad Mosque.
Come early to enjoy the courtyards in quieter conditions.
Wear comfortable shoes for smooth and uneven stone floors.
Sit in a shaded iwan to rest between rooms.
Walk through the nearby spice market afterwards.
