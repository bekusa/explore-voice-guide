The guide

The story

Listen first. Car horns, the hiss of minibuses braking, a vendor calling out over the traffic. Then look up. In the middle of this noise stands an old stone arch, pale and worn smooth, with the warm afternoon light resting on its blocks. This is Bab Tuma, one of the ancient gates of Damascus. Cars circle it on all sides, so it sits like an island of the past in a modern stream. Just behind it, the narrow lanes of the Old City begin.

The name means the Gate of Thomas. For centuries this arch was one of several entrances through the walls that wrapped the old city. Each gate had its own name, its own neighbourhood, and its own story. Bab Tuma marks the northeastern corner of the walled city. Many visitors arrive here without knowing it. They only know that this is where the taxi stops, where the crowds gather, and where the old streets start.

The story of the gates reaches back to the Roman period, when Damascus was an important city of the eastern empire. Roman planners laid out a grid of streets inside a wall. According to a tradition repeated by many historians, each gate was linked to one of the planets known at that time. Bab Tuma is usually associated with Venus. It is a lovely idea... a ring of gates, each under its own sky. But the details of that system are not fully certain, so treat it as an old tradition rather than firm fact.

So who was Thomas? Here the stories split. Many local Christians connect the name with Saint Thomas the Apostle, one of the twelve followers of Jesus. Thomas is remembered in Christian tradition for his doubt, and for travelling far to the east to preach. Another explanation points to a Byzantine commander named Thomas. In this version, he was linked by marriage to the imperial family and helped defend the city. Both stories are told. Neither one can be proved beyond doubt from this spot.

That second story leads to a turning point. In the 630s, Arab Muslim armies reached Damascus, and the city came under their rule, ending centuries of Byzantine control. Byzantine here means the eastern Roman Empire, ruled from Constantinople. Accounts of the siege were written down long afterwards, and they mix memory with legend. Some of them say that fighting took place near this gate. Standing here, you can picture defenders on the walls. But keep in mind that the colourful details come from later chroniclers.

The arch you see today is not the Roman original. Like most of the city's gates, it was rebuilt in the Middle Ages. The work is generally dated to the thirteenth century, under the Ayyubids, the dynasty founded by Saladin. Later repairs followed in the fourteenth century under the Mamluks, a line of soldier-sultans who ruled Egypt and Syria. So this single structure carries the hands of several eras. Roman foundations, medieval stone, and modern repairs all sit together in one place.

Walk closer and look at the masonry. The gate is built from large, cut blocks of limestone, fitted tightly without much decoration. This was a working gate, built for defence and for control of traffic. Notice how the stones differ in tone. Some are pale and fresh, others are darker and pitted by weather. These differences show centuries of patching and replacement. Look also at the depth of the passage. A gate like this once held heavy doors that closed at night.

From here, sections of the old city wall run away from the gate. In some places the wall is clear and solid. In other places, houses have been built against it or on top of it, so the line almost disappears. This is common in Damascus. The city did not stop growing when its walls lost their military purpose. People simply used the old stone as a foundation. If you follow the wall with your eyes, you can see the city slowly absorbing its own defences.

Pass through the gate, and you enter the historic Christian quarter of the Old City. Damascus has had Christian communities since the earliest days of the faith, and many different churches are found in these lanes. Orthodox, Catholic, and Armenian congregations all have places of worship nearby. On Sunday mornings you may hear church bells mixing with the sound of traffic. Later in the day, the call to prayer from mosques in other quarters drifts across the rooftops. Both sounds belong to this city.

Here is something many visitors miss. Damascus appears in one of the most famous stories of the New Testament. According to the Book of Acts, Paul of Tarsus was travelling to the city when he had a vision that changed his life. He then stayed on a street called Straight. That street still exists, running east to west across the Old City. Not far from here, a small underground chapel is dedicated to Ananias, the man said to have restored Paul's sight. Pilgrims still visit it.

The lanes around Bab Tuma are full of old courtyard houses. From the street, they often show only a plain wall and a modest wooden door. Inside, many open onto a hidden courtyard with a fountain, citrus trees, and rooms facing inward. This design kept homes cool and private. In recent decades, a number of these houses became restaurants, cafes, and small hotels. In the evening, the quarter fills with families, students, and friends, sitting under vines and talking late into the night.

One more surprise. For people in Damascus, Bab Tuma is not only a gate. It is the name of a whole district, a bus stop, a meeting point. When someone says, meet me at Bab Tuma, they may mean the square, the shops, or the streets beyond. The ancient arch has become an everyday landmark. Children pass under it on the way to school. Delivery bikes weave through. The monument is still doing its old job — guiding people in and out of the city.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment beside the stone. Feel the cool surface under your hand, and notice how many feet have passed this way before you. Romans, Byzantine soldiers, medieval merchants, pilgrims, and today's commuters have all used this gate. As you walk into the lanes, watch how the noise of the roundabout fades behind you. The streets narrow, the light softens, and the old city of Damascus slowly closes around you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in late afternoon when warm light falls on the stone.
Cross the roundabout carefully; traffic circles the gate constantly.
Walk through the gate to explore the Christian quarter on foot.
Return in the evening for courtyard cafes and restaurants.
Dress modestly if you plan to enter nearby churches.
