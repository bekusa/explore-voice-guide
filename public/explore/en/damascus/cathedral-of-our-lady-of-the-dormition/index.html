The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere behind a wall, a motorbike coughs down a lane barely wider than its handlebars. A door creaks. Then, if you are lucky with your timing, a bell sounds over the flat roofs of the old Christian quarter of Damascus. The air smells of dust, cardamom coffee, and warm stone. Underfoot, the paving is uneven and polished by centuries of shoes. You are standing near the Cathedral of Our Lady of the Dormition, one of the quieter sacred places in this ancient city.

This church is the patriarchal cathedral of the Melkite Greek Catholic Church. That name needs a short explanation. The Melkites are Arabic-speaking Christians who follow the Byzantine rite, the old liturgy of Constantinople, while being in communion with the Pope in Rome. So the worship here looks Eastern. There are icons, incense, and chanted prayers. Yet the church belongs to the Catholic family. For many visitors, that combination is the first surprise of the day.

Now the second word in the name. Dormition means falling asleep. In Eastern Christian tradition, Mary did not simply die. She fell asleep, and was then taken into heaven. The feast of the Dormition falls in mid-August, and it is one of the great celebrations of the Eastern church year. If you look at the icons inside, you may find this scene. Mary lies on a bier, the apostles gather around her, and Christ stands above, holding a small figure that represents her soul.

Many locals do not use the formal name at all. They often call it after the lane it stands on, which carries the Arabic word for olive. So if you ask a shopkeeper for directions, the olive church may get you a quicker answer than the full title. It is a small sign of how this building lives inside the neighbourhood. It is not a monument set apart from the streets. It is a parish church, wedged between houses, workshops, and small grocery stores.

Take a moment to look at the neighbourhood itself. This is the eastern side of the walled Old City, near Bab Sharqi, the Eastern Gate. That gate dates back to Roman times, when Damascus was laid out around a long main street. That street still runs through the Old City today. In the Bible, the Book of Acts mentions a street called Straight in Damascus. It is linked with the story of Saint Paul, who came here after his conversion. That old road is only a short walk from where you stand.

The history of this quarter has not always been gentle. In 1860, violence swept through the Christian neighbourhoods of Damascus. Large parts of the quarter were burned, and many people were killed. Churches and homes had to be rebuilt in the years that followed. Much of what you see around you, including the fabric of the churches here, belongs to that period of rebuilding. So these walls carry a story of loss, but also a story of a community that stayed and started again.

Approach the entrance and notice the stone. Damascene builders have long used bands of light and dark stone, laid in alternating rows. You can see this striped pattern across the Old City, on mosques, houses, and churches alike. It is a shared local language of building. A church and a mosque a few streets apart may use the same materials, the same courtyards, the same heavy wooden doors. Here, faith communities have lived side by side for a very long time.

Step inside, if the doors are open, and let your eyes adjust. After the bright lane, the interior feels cool and dim. The first thing to find is the iconostasis. This is a screen or wall covered with icons, standing between the main hall and the altar area. In Eastern churches, it marks the boundary between the earthly and the heavenly. Priests pass through its doors during the liturgy. Worshippers stand before it and light candles.

Look closely at the icons. They are not painted to look like photographs. Faces are calm and long, eyes are large, and backgrounds often shine with gold. This is deliberate. Icons are meant as windows to the holy, not as portraits of everyday life. Many local Christians kiss an icon as they enter, or make the sign of the cross from right to left, in the Eastern way. If you watch quietly, you will see these small gestures repeated again and again.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. The prayers in this church are sung largely in Arabic. Some people arrive expecting Greek or Latin, and are surprised. But Arabic has been the everyday language of Christians in this region for well over a thousand years. Listen during a service and you may hear Arabic words that also appear in Muslim prayer, such as Allah, the Arabic word for God. In Damascus, a shared language crosses the lines between religions.

Notice the candles near the entrance. Thin tapers stand in trays of sand, and people light them for family members, for the sick, and for those who have died. The smell of wax and incense hangs in the air. Over the past years of war and hardship in Syria, many families from this quarter have scattered abroad. For those who remain, a candle here can be a way to remember relatives who now live in Beirut, Stockholm, or Montreal.

This cathedral is also a place of authority. As the seat of the Melkite patriarch, it is where major ceremonies of the church take place. The patriarch's full title links him to Antioch, the ancient city where, according to the Book of Acts, followers of Jesus were first called Christians. So when you stand here, you stand at a centre of a church whose memory reaches back to the first generations of Christianity in the eastern Mediterranean.

Before you leave, step back outside and look up. Above the lane, cables cross the narrow strip of sky. Laundry may hang from a balcony. A cat may be asleep on a step. A child may run past with bread from the corner bakery. The cathedral does not demand attention from this street. It simply belongs to it.

As you walk away, listen once more. The motorbikes, the voices, perhaps a distant call to prayer from a minaret, perhaps a bell. These sounds have overlapped in this part of Damascus for generations. Let them follow you down the lane toward Bab Sharqi, and toward the long, straight street that has carried travellers through this city for two thousand years.
