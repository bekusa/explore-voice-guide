The guide

The story

Start with the light. On high ground at the western edge of Damascus, the sun hits wide surfaces of pale stone and bounces straight back at you. The air up here is often drier and windier than in the old city below. Traffic hums somewhere down the slope. In front of you, or perhaps far off behind a security fence, a long, heavy building sits on the hill. This is the People's Palace, known in Arabic as Qasr al-Shaab.

The name sounds open and welcoming. For most of its life, the building was anything but. It was built as a ceremonial seat of the Syrian presidency, a place for state receptions, visiting heads of state, and formal photographs. Ordinary Syrians saw it mostly on television, as the backdrop to handshakes and signed agreements. That gap, between a name about the people and a building closed to them, is a good place to begin.

Here is the first surprise. The architect was not Syrian, and he was not from the Arab world at all. The palace was designed by Kenzo Tange, one of the most celebrated Japanese architects of the twentieth century. Tange designed the Peace Memorial Park in Hiroshima and the curved Olympic gymnasium for the 1964 Tokyo Games. In 1987 he received the Pritzker Prize, often described as the highest award in architecture.

The commission came from President Hafez al-Assad, who ruled Syria from 1971 until his death in 2000. Construction took place during the 1980s, and the building was finished around the start of the 1990s. It was a period when Syria wanted to project permanence and strength. A modern palace on a hill, designed by a famous name, sent a clear message. The state was here, it was solid, and it was watching over the capital.

Now look at the shape of the building. Tange worked in a style often called late modernism. In simple terms, this means bold geometric forms, very little decoration, and a strong sense of mass. The palace is built from large blocks and long horizontal lines. It does not rise like a tower. Instead it spreads out across the hilltop, low and wide, so that it feels rooted into the ground.

The position on the hill matters as much as the walls. From below, the building looks down over the city. From the palace grounds, the view runs across a huge spread of Damascus toward Mount Qasioun, the bare ridge that rises along the northern side of the city. In many countries, rulers placed their palaces in the old centre, among the people. Here the palace stands apart, above and outside the dense streets.

That separation becomes clearer when you think about the old city. A short drive away is the walled heart of Damascus, one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world. There you find the Umayyad Mosque, covered markets, narrow lanes, and courtyard houses built around fountains and citrus trees. That world is close, shaded, and inward-looking. The People's Palace is the opposite. It is open to the sky, exposed, and built to be seen from a distance.

And here is the second surprise. Despite its scale, the palace was not mainly a family home. The Assad family is generally reported to have lived in other residences in the city. The People's Palace worked more like a stage. It was used for official ceremonies, diplomatic meetings, and events meant for the cameras. In a way, it was a building designed to be photographed rather than lived in.

Then history turned. In December 2024, the government of Bashar al-Assad, the son of Hafez, fell after nearly fourteen years of civil war. The Assad family's long hold on power came to an end. In those days, crowds entered several presidential properties across Damascus. Places that had been closed for decades were suddenly filled with ordinary people, walking through rooms they had only seen on screens.

Since then, the People's Palace has taken on a new role. Syria's new leadership has used it to receive foreign delegations and to hold official meetings. So the building is again a backdrop for handshakes, but the faces in the photographs are different. The stone has not changed. What has changed is the meaning people attach to it. For many Syrians, the name People's Palace now carries a sharper question about who the state is really for.

A practical note. Whether you can come close to the palace depends on the current security situation, and that situation can change quickly. Parts of the grounds may be closed, guarded, or only open for special events. Treat this as a working government site, not a museum. Follow the instructions of guards, and do not point cameras at checkpoints or security staff. Often the best experience is simply a respectful view from a distance.

If you can see the building clearly, notice how it reacts to the time of day. In the strong midday sun, the pale surfaces look flat and almost white, and the shapes become harsh. In the early morning or late afternoon, shadows fall across the facade and the geometry becomes easier to read. You can see the deep recesses, the edges of each block, and the way the building steps across the hill.

So take a last look. You are standing between two versions of Damascus. Behind you, or below you, is a city of courtyards, markets, and centuries of daily life. In front of you is a palace built by a Japanese master for a Syrian president, now used by a different government in a changing country. As you walk away, listen to the traffic rising from the valley. The city is still moving, and the hilltop is still waiting to see what comes next.
