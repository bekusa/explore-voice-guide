The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, under a long iron roof, the covered market hums with voices, footsteps and the clatter of shop shutters. Ahead, the roof simply ends. Light pours in. And there, in the open air, a row of tall stone columns rises above the crowd. Their tops are carved with curling leaves. A broken piece of an ancient gateway still sits across them, high above the street. You are standing at the edge of the Temple of Jupiter.

Most people walk straight past these columns on their way to the great mosque beyond. They glance up, take a photo, and keep moving. But these stones are a doorway in more than one sense. They mark the entrance to one of the oldest continuously sacred places in the world. For thousands of years, people have come to this same patch of ground to pray. Only the names of the gods have changed.

Long before the Romans arrived, Damascus was a city of the Aramaeans, an ancient people of the Near East whose language spread across the region. Here they worshipped Hadad, a god of storms and rain. In a dry land at the edge of the desert, a god who brought rain mattered enormously. Historians believe a temple to Hadad stood on this site in the first millennium before the common era. Nothing of it survives above ground. But the idea of this place as holy ground was already very old.

Then came Rome. When the Romans took control of Syria, they did something they often did across their empire. They matched local gods with their own. Hadad, the storm god, became linked with Jupiter, the Roman king of the gods, who also ruled the sky and thunder. The result was a blended deity, often called Jupiter of Damascus. Over the following centuries, a vast temple rose here, built and expanded in stages during the Roman period.

It is hard to picture the scale from where you stand. The columns in front of you belonged to the western gateway of the temple complex. A gateway like this is called a propylaeum, which simply means a grand monumental entrance. Beyond it lay a huge walled enclosure, and inside that, an inner sacred court. The whole complex covered an area far larger than the mosque you see today. In its time, this was one of the largest temples in Roman Syria.

Look closely at the column tops. These are Corinthian capitals, the most decorative of the classical styles, carved with layers of acanthus leaves. The stone is weathered and pitted. Some edges are worn soft. Now look at the fragment resting above them. Part of it curves, the trace of an arch set inside a triangular gable. This was a fashionable design in the Roman East. It gave the gateway a sense of height and drama for anyone arriving from the city.

Here is something many visitors miss. The temple never really disappeared. It was absorbed. In the late fourth century, as Christianity became the official faith of the Roman Empire, the old pagan sanctuary was turned into a church. That church was dedicated to Saint John the Baptist. According to tradition, a relic believed to be the head of Saint John was kept here. The same sacred ground now served a new faith.

The next great change came in the seventh century, when Arab Muslim armies took Damascus. The city became the capital of the Umayyad Caliphate, the first great Islamic dynasty, which ruled an empire stretching from Spain to Central Asia. For a time, sources say, Christians and Muslims shared the site. Then, in the early eighth century, the caliph al-Walid the First ordered a grand new mosque to be built inside the old temple enclosure.

That building is the Umayyad Mosque, which stands just ahead of you. Its builders reused the Roman enclosure walls, and you can still see ancient stonework in its outer fabric. So when you walk from these columns toward the mosque, you are following the same path a Roman worshipper would have taken. The gods changed, the rituals changed, but the direction of the walk stayed the same. That is the second surprise of this place. It is one continuous story.

Now turn around and look back into the market. This is Souq al-Hamidiyah, a covered bazaar from the Ottoman era, named after an Ottoman sultan. Its arched metal roof lets in tiny dots of daylight, like stars on a dark ceiling. Locals often say these holes came from gunfire during the uprising against French rule in the 1920s. It is a popular story, and it may be true, but treat it as local memory rather than settled fact.

Inside the souq, listen for a rhythmic thudding. That sound often comes from Bakdash, a long-running ice cream parlour. Workers pound thick, stretchy ice cream with wooden mallets, then roll it in crushed pistachios. The texture is chewy, almost elastic, because it is made with mastic, a resin from a Mediterranean tree. Around you, shops sell fabric, perfume, sweets and household goods. The ancient gateway has always stood at the meeting point of trade and faith.

The Old City of Damascus was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979. This recognises the layers you can see right here. Roman stone, Byzantine memory, Umayyad grandeur and Ottoman commerce all meet within a few steps. Damascus is often described as one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities on Earth. Standing between these columns, that claim feels less like a statistic and more like something you can touch.

Before you move on, take one more look upward. The broken gable hangs there against the sky, held up by columns that have outlasted empires. Pigeons settle on the ledges. Shoppers pass beneath without looking. Somewhere nearby, the call to prayer may rise from the mosque. As you walk toward it, notice the ground under your feet. People have walked this way to pray for perhaps three thousand years. Today, for a few steps, you are one of them.
