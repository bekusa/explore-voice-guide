The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere above your head, a pendulum is swinging. It makes a soft, steady tick, the sound of an old house clock. But this clock does not only keep the hours. It moves the planets. You are standing in a modest brick house in Franeker, a small canal town in the Dutch province of Friesland. Inside this house is the oldest working planetarium in the world.

Look up at the ceiling of the front room. It is painted a deep blue, the colour of a clear night. Golden spheres hang from it. Each one is a planet, and each one sits in its own curved slot. Look very closely and very patiently. They are moving right now, slowly, at the same pace as the real planets in the real sky.

The man who built this was not an astronomer by profession. Eise Eisinga was a wool comber. He prepared raw wool for spinning, and he ran a small family business from this house. He taught himself mathematics and astronomy from books as a boy. He grew up in the Frisian countryside, far from any university chair. Yet he understood the motions of the solar system well enough to build this machine.

The reason he built it is a story of fear. In 1774, several planets and the Moon were due to appear very close together in the sky. A local preacher wrote that this meeting of planets could bring the end of the world. People in the region became frightened. Eisinga thought this fear was unreasonable. He wanted to show his neighbours that the planets follow fixed paths and simply cannot crash into each other.

So he decided to show them, not just tell them. He started building a moving model of the solar system in his own living room. The work took about seven years, and the planetarium was finished in 1781. Think about that for a moment. The predicted danger passed long before he finished. But by then, the project had become something much larger than an answer to one rumour.

Now think about the scale of what is above you. The Sun sits at the centre. The planets known in his time circle around it — Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. Each one moves at its true speed. Mercury goes around fairly quickly. Earth takes one full year. Saturn, on the outer ring, needs about twenty-nine years for one orbit. A child who sees Saturn here today will be an adult before it returns to the same place.

Here is something many visitors miss. The ceiling shows only the faces of the planets. The real machine is hidden above it. If you go up to the attic, you can see the works. There are large wooden rings and gear wheels. Thousands of small hand-forged nails act as teeth on the wheels. A pendulum clock drives everything. It is the work of a craftsman using simple materials and very careful arithmetic.

The fact that it is wood and iron, not brass, matters. Eisinga was not a rich man. He used what a skilled Frisian householder could make or buy. His father helped with some of the work. The result has kept running for well over two centuries, with maintenance and repair over the years. That is a remarkable record for a machine built by hand in a private home.

The ceiling also carries other information. Around the planets, you will see dials and markings. They show things like the date, the position of the Moon, and the signs of the zodiac, the twelve star patterns along the Sun's yearly path. In the room, you can also find clocks and indicators that track time in more ways than one. Take your time here. The details reward a slow eye.

Eisinga's own life was not calm. In the 1780s, the Dutch Republic was divided by political conflict. Eisinga supported the Patriots, a reform movement that opposed the ruling powers. When the Patriots lost, he was forced to leave Friesland for several years. His planetarium stayed behind and kept turning without him. Later, he was able to return home and continue his work.

In the early nineteenth century, the Dutch state bought the planetarium. Eisinga was allowed to keep living in the house and to look after his machine. After his death, other caretakers took over this task. The house became a museum, and today it also tells the wider story of astronomy and space. In 2023, the planetarium was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List, the international register of places of outstanding value.

Step outside for a moment and look at the street. Franeker once had its own university, and it was one of the oldest in the Netherlands. That university is long gone. Its closing came in the early nineteenth century. But the town still has its brick façades, quiet canals and a historic town hall nearby. In winter, Franeker is also known as one of the eleven Frisian towns on the famous skating route across the province.

When you go back inside, stand in the front room once more. Imagine it as a living room, with a family eating supper under a turning sky. That is exactly what it was. Eisinga did not build this for a palace or an observatory. He built it for his neighbours, to calm their fear with knowledge they could see with their own eyes.

So before you leave, look up one last time. Find the small golden Earth on its slot. It is the same planet you are standing on, and it is moving at the same speed. Listen again for the tick of the pendulum overhead. Then walk out into the street, under the real sky, and remember that it is turning too — just as Eisinga showed.
