The guide

The story

Listen first. Waves wash over flat grey stone, then drain back through cracks with a soft, hollow gurgle. A gull calls somewhere overhead. The air is cool and salty, even in summer, and it smells of seaweed drying in the sun. Under your feet the rock is pale and rough, almost like old concrete. All around you, strange stone figures stand along the shore. Some look like faces. Some look like giant flowerpots. Welcome to the Mingan Archipelago, on the North Shore of the Gulf of Saint Lawrence in Quebec.

This national park reserve is a long chain of islands and islets. Parks Canada counts around a thousand of them, scattered for roughly 150 kilometres along the coast. Some are large, with forests and bogs. Others are little more than a bare ledge that disappears at high tide. The main gateways are the small towns of Longue-Pointe-de-Mingan and Havre-Saint-Pierre. From there, boats carry visitors out to the islands. The protected area was created in the 1980s, and today it is one of the most unusual coastlines in Canada.

The word reserve in the name matters. A national park reserve is land that Canada protects as a park while Indigenous land claims are still being negotiated. Here, those rights belong to the Innu, a First Nations people whose territory covers a vast part of eastern Quebec and Labrador. The Innu community of Ekuanitshit, also known as Mingan, sits on this coast. For thousands of years, Innu families travelled these waters to hunt seals, fish, and gather eggs. Parks Canada works with the community in managing the islands.

Now look closely at the stone. It is limestone, and it is extremely old. It formed at the bottom of a warm, shallow sea roughly 450 million years ago. At that time, this part of the earth lay much closer to the equator. Layer after layer of mud, shell, and tiny marine creatures settled and hardened into rock. Today you are standing on that ancient seabed. It is the same material that sits under your shoes, just lifted, cracked, and carved by everything that came after.

Here is the first surprise. Bend down and look at the flat surfaces near the water. You may see fossils right in the rock. Look for curved shapes, small spirals, branching patterns, and round coral-like forms. These are the remains of sea life from that warm, ancient ocean. Many visitors walk straight past them, because their eyes go to the big monoliths. Please look, and photograph, but leave everything in place. Collecting rock or fossils is not allowed in the park.

So how did those famous stone figures form? The story starts with ice. During the last ice age, massive glaciers covered this region. Their weight pressed the land down. When the ice melted, the land slowly rose again, like a cushion after you stand up. As the coast lifted, new rock came within reach of the waves. Water, frost, salt, and wind attacked the softer layers first. Over thousands of years, the harder pieces were left standing alone as monoliths, or sea stacks.

This process has not stopped. Every winter, water seeps into cracks, freezes, and expands. Every storm throws waves against the base of the pillars. That is why many monoliths are wider at the top than at the bottom. The sea eats away at their feet first. Some have already collapsed, and others will fall in the future. In other words, the shapes you see today are temporary. A visitor a few centuries from now will meet a different set of stone characters.

The second surprise is in the plants. Despite the southern origin of the rock, the climate here is cool and harsh. The limestone soil is thin and rich in lime. Together, these conditions support small plants usually found much farther north, in arctic or alpine places. One of the most special is the Mingan thistle. It grows only in a few places around the Gulf of Saint Lawrence. Look for low cushions of flowers clinging to the rock, shaped by wind into tight little mats.

The islands are also important for birds. Seabirds nest on several of the more remote islets, where foxes and people rarely reach them. The best known are the Atlantic puffins, with their black backs, white faces, and bright orange-striped beaks. Terns, eiders, gulls, and razorbills also use these waters. Boat tours approach some nesting islands, but visitors cannot land on the most sensitive ones. If you have binoculars, use them. Watch for small birds flying fast and low over the water, wings beating very quickly.

Keep an eye on the sea itself. The cold waters of the Gulf are rich in food, and whales come here to feed. Minke whales are often seen, and larger species pass through in some seasons. Seals also rest on the rocks at low tide. In Longue-Pointe-de-Mingan, a long-running research group studies the whales of this coast. On a calm day, listen for a sudden puff of breath. That sound often comes before you see anything at all.

People from far away have also left traces here. Long before most of Quebec's towns existed, European fishermen and whalers worked in the Gulf. Basque whalers, from the region between Spain and France, hunted in these waters centuries ago. Archaeologists have found signs of European activity on some islands. Later, in the nineteenth century, Acadian families from the Magdalen Islands settled the coast and founded Havre-Saint-Pierre. Their descendants still live here, and you will hear their French in the shops and on the docks.

Visiting the islands is a slow, simple experience. Boats leave from the mainland and drop visitors on islands with trails, small shelters, and interpretive signs. Some islands offer basic camping, so you can stay overnight and watch the light change on the monoliths. Paths are often short, but the ground is uneven and the weather can turn quickly. Fog may roll in without warning. Bring layers, good shoes, water, and food. Services on the islands are very limited, and boats keep to a schedule.

The light here changes everything. In the middle of the day, the stone can look flat and grey. Early in the morning, or late in the evening, the low sun brings out yellows, oranges, and soft pinks in the limestone. Shadows show every crack and ledge. Tides matter too. At low tide, you can walk farther along the shore and see pools full of seaweed and small creatures. At high tide, the monoliths stand directly in the water and look even stranger.

Before you leave, take one more slow look around you. Notice the wide sky, the low islands on the horizon, and the stone figures standing at the edge of the sea. Feel the rough rock under your hand. Hear the water moving through the cracks. These shapes took thousands of years to form, and they are still changing. As you walk back toward the boat, look over your shoulder once more. The tide, the light, and the stones will already look slightly different.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book boat shuttles from Havre-Saint-Pierre or Longue-Pointe-de-Mingan.
Bring warm layers since fog and wind arrive quickly.
Visit near low tide to explore pools and fossils.
Come early morning or evening for warm light on stone.
Pack binoculars for puffins, seals, and passing whales.
