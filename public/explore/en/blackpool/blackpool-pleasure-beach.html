The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you look at anything, there is the sound. A low wooden rumble rolls across the site, followed by a rising chorus of screams, then the clatter of wheels on old timber. Salt wind comes in off the Irish Sea, carrying the smell of frying doughnuts and warm sugar. Above you, steel and wood twist against a pale northern sky. You are standing on what was once open sand at the southern end of Blackpool's seafront.

That sand is the key to the name. In the late nineteenth century, this stretch of shore south of the town was little more than dunes. Travelling showmen, fortune tellers and stallholders set up here, close to the crowds that the railways were bringing to the coast. Blackpool was becoming the holiday town for the mill and factory workers of northern England. When the mills closed for their summer weeks, whole towns seemed to arrive at once.

In 1896, a businessman named William George Bean saw something bigger. He had seen American amusement parks and wanted to bring that idea to the Lancashire coast. With a partner, John Outhwaite, he began to turn the dunes into a permanent park. The early attractions were simple by today's standards — a carousel, a switchback railway, gentle rides for people in their Sunday clothes. But the ambition was clear from the start.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. This park has stayed in the same family for well over a century. Bean's daughter, Doris, married Leonard Thompson, and the Thompson family has run the Pleasure Beach ever since. Generation after generation, they have added rides, rebuilt buildings and argued over what to keep. Very few parks of this size anywhere in the world can say the same. That continuity is part of why the place feels layered, rather than designed in one go.

Now look for the oldest survivor. Sir Hiram Maxim's Flying Machines have turned here since 1904. Maxim was an American-born inventor, better known for designing a machine gun. He also dreamed of powered flight. When his flying experiments did not succeed, he built this ride instead — small carriages that swing outward on long arms as the central tower spins. More than a century later, the ride still turns, and it is one of the oldest working amusement rides anywhere.

Not far away is another early piece of the park. The River Caves opened in the early twentieth century. Small boats drift slowly through dim, painted scenes of distant lands. It is quiet, a little strange, and very old-fashioned — and that is the point. In a park built around speed and height, this is a ride from an age when simply floating through the dark felt like an adventure.

Then came the great wooden coasters. The Big Dipper opened in the 1920s, and its white timber frame still runs along the edge of the park. Wooden coasters move differently from steel ones. The structure flexes slightly under the train, and you feel every joint and board through the seat. Riders often say these old coasters feel faster than they really are. That shaking, rattling sensation is exactly what the builders intended.

The second surprise is the Grand National, which opened in 1935. It is a racing coaster with two tracks side by side, so two trains leave together and race each other. But the layout has a trick. The tracks cross over, so the train that starts on the left finishes on the right. Coaster fans call this kind of design a Möbius loop. Very few of these exist in the world, and this one is among the oldest.

As you walk, notice the buildings, not only the rides. In the 1930s, the architect Joseph Emberton designed a series of buildings here in the Art Deco and modernist styles. These styles favoured clean white surfaces, smooth curves and strong horizontal lines. The round, white Casino building near the entrance is the best-known example. Against the noise and colour of the rides, these calm white forms give the park a surprising sense of order.

After the war, the park kept growing, and the scale changed. The most visible result rises above everything else. The Big One opened in 1994, and for a time it was the tallest roller coaster in the world. Its first drop falls steeply towards the ground, and from the top, riders can see along the coast and out to sea. On a clear day, its long red and blue lift hill is visible from far along the promenade.

The park did not stop there. In 2018, it opened Icon, a launched steel coaster. Instead of a slow climb, the train is pushed forward at high speed by powerful motors. So within one walk, you can see more than a century of thinking about thrills. There is the spinning arm of 1904, the shaking timber of the interwar years, the tall steel of the 1990s and the electric launch of today.

Take a moment to watch the people, too. Grandparents point out rides they queued for as children. Teenagers compare which coaster scared them most. Families from the old mill towns have come here for generations, and for many of them, a day at the Pleasure Beach is a family tradition. The rides change, but the shared feeling of a seaside day out has lasted much longer than any single attraction.

Before you go, find a quiet spot and look north along the shore. You will see the line of the promenade and, in the distance, Blackpool Tower. Behind you, the old wooden track of the Big Dipper creaks as another train climbs. The sea air is still there, the sugar smell, the distant screams. This is the same sand where showmen set up their stalls more than a century ago. As you walk away, listen once more for that wooden rumble.

Practical tips

Ride the Grand National with a friend in the other train.
