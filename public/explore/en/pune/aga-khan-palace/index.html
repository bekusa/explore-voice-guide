The guide

The story

Listen first. Beyond the gate, the traffic of Pune softens into birdsong and the scrape of a broom on stone. The air under the trees feels a degree or two cooler. Ahead of you, a long pale building rises behind green lawns. Its arches repeat, one after another, like a row of open doorways. Stand still for a moment and let your eyes travel along them. This is the Aga Khan Palace. It looks like a summer retreat, but it was once a prison.

The palace dates from 1892. It was commissioned by Sultan Muhammed Shah, the Aga Khan the Third, the spiritual leader of the Nizari Ismaili Muslim community. The Aga Khan was the hereditary title of that leadership. In the 1890s, the countryside around Pune suffered from severe famine. According to the account most often told here, the Aga Khan built the palace partly as an act of charity. The construction work gave paid employment to hundreds of people from the surrounding villages when food was scarce.

Look at the shape of the building. It is long and low, with deep verandas wrapped around it. The arches are tall and rounded, and they carry a clear European influence. Many writers describe the style as Italianate. The design is practical as well as elegant. The verandas shade the inner rooms from the hard Deccan sun. The Deccan is the high plateau of central and southern India, where Pune sits. Breezes move through the open arcades, and the thick walls hold back the heat of the afternoon.

For its first half century, the palace was a private residence in a quiet corner of the city. Then, in August 1942, history arrived at the gate. The Indian National Congress launched the Quit India Movement, a mass campaign demanding an immediate end to British rule. The British authorities responded quickly. They arrested the leaders of the movement within hours. Mahatma Gandhi was among them. He was not sent to an ordinary jail. He was brought here, to this palace, and held under guard.

Gandhi was not alone. His wife, Kasturba Gandhi, was detained with him. So was his long-serving secretary, Mahadev Desai, a man who had recorded Gandhi's words and days for years. The poet and political leader Sarojini Naidu was also held here for a time. The palace became a gilded cage. The rooms were comfortable compared with a prison cell, but the doors were locked. Outside, the country was in upheaval, and the prisoners received news only in fragments.

The imprisonment brought grief almost immediately. Mahadev Desai died here in August 1942, only days after the arrests. He suffered a sudden heart attack. For Gandhi, the loss was deeply personal. Desai had been close to him, almost like a son. Then, in February 1944, Kasturba Gandhi also died here, still in detention. She had been Gandhi's companion since they were married as teenagers. Both deaths took place within these walls, and both are remembered in the grounds today.

Walk out into the garden and follow the paths to the memorials. These are samadhis. A samadhi is a memorial shrine that marks a place of cremation or remembrance. The memorials to Kasturba Gandhi and Mahadev Desai stand here in simple stone. They are quiet places. Visitors often stop, lower their voices, and leave flowers. Here is a detail many people miss. A portion of Mahatma Gandhi's own ashes was also placed in these grounds after his death in 1948. So in this garden, Gandhi rests close to two of the people he loved most.

Gandhi was released in May 1944, mainly because his health had weakened. He never returned to live here. But the palace kept its meaning. In 1969, Prince Karim Aga Khan, the Aga Khan the Fourth, donated the palace to the people of India. He gave it as a tribute to Gandhi and his philosophy. The building now serves as a memorial and museum. The Gandhi National Memorial Society cares for it. Later, the Indian government recognised it as a monument of national importance.

Now step inside. The rooms are bright and plain. You will find photographs from the independence struggle, written panels, and objects connected with Gandhi's life here. Some rooms show the spaces where he and Kasturba lived during their detention. Notice how ordinary the personal items are. A few utensils. Simple clothing. Sandals. Gandhi believed in living with very little, and these small things say that more clearly than any speech. The contrast with the grand arches outside is part of the experience.

Here is a second surprise. This palace was not Gandhi's only prison in Pune. A few kilometres away stands Yerwada Central Jail, where he was held during the 1930s. In 1932, from that jail, he began a famous fast. It led to the Poona Pact, an agreement about political representation for India's so-called untouchable communities. Poona is the older English spelling of Pune. So this city holds two chapters of Gandhi's captivity, and each one shaped modern India in a different way.

Before you leave, look for the khadi shop on the grounds. Khadi is hand-spun, hand-woven cloth. For Gandhi, it was far more than fabric. Spinning your own thread was a protest against imported British textiles. It was also a statement about self-reliance and dignity of labour. The spinning wheel became a symbol of the whole independence movement. You can still buy khadi cloth and simple goods here. It is a living link to the ideas that once filled these rooms.

Notice, too, how families use the gardens today. Students come with notebooks. Grandparents walk slowly with children and point to the photographs. Couples sit in the shade of the old trees. The lawns are wide, and the sound of the city stays mostly outside the walls. The place works as a memorial, but it also works as a park. That mix feels honest. Gandhi spoke often about ordinary life, and ordinary life continues here every day.

Take one last look along the arcade. The arches still frame the light the same way they did in 1944. Somewhere behind those walls, a man in a simple cotton shawl once waited, wrote letters, and mourned. Now the doors stand open. As you walk back toward the gate, listen again for the broom on stone and the birds in the trees. Then listen for the traffic of Pune returning. You are carrying a little of this quiet back out into the city.
