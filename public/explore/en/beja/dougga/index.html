The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. Under your shoes, the old paving stones are worn smooth and slightly uneven, warm from the sun even in the cooler months. Around you, olive trees run down the hillside in long silver-green rows. The wind moves through them with a dry, steady hiss. Somewhere below, a dog barks in a farm. And above it all, pale honey-coloured columns stand against the sky, as if a Roman town simply stopped one afternoon and never started again.

This is Dougga, known in antiquity as Thugga. It sits on a slope above the fertile valleys of northern Tunisia, not far from the modern town of Téboursouk. What makes it unusual is how much survives. Streets, temples, baths, houses, a theatre, and a market all remain in place across the hillside. UNESCO added the site to its World Heritage list in 1997. Many experts consider it one of the best-preserved Roman towns in North Africa.

But Dougga is older than Rome here. Before the Romans arrived, this was a town of the Numidians — the Berber-speaking people who ruled much of this region. Their kings allied with, and sometimes fought against, both Carthage and Rome. So the story of this hill is not one empire but several layers. Numidian, Punic, Roman, and later Byzantine and local life all left marks on the same stones.

The clearest sign of that earlier world stands a little below the main town. Look for a tall, slender tower with a pyramid on top. This is the Libyco-Punic Mausoleum, built around the second century before Christ. It is one of the rare surviving monuments of royal Numidian architecture. Its style mixes local, Greek, and Egyptian ideas. Stacked levels, carved columns, and small sculptures once decorated the tomb of an important local figure.

Here is the first surprise. The tower you see is largely a reconstruction. In 1842, a British consul named Thomas Reade had the monument partly taken apart to remove its inscription. That stone carried text in two scripts — Libyan, the ancient writing of the Berbers, and Punic, the language of Carthage. The inscription went to the British Museum in London, where it remains. French archaeologists rebuilt the mausoleum in the early twentieth century, using the fallen blocks.

That bilingual inscription mattered far beyond Dougga. Because the two languages sat side by side, scholars could use the Punic text to help understand the ancient Libyan script. In a small way, this hillside played a role like the Rosetta Stone did for Egyptian writing. So when you look at the tower, you are looking at a place that helped people read a lost alphabet.

Now walk up toward the heart of the Roman town. The Capitol is the building that dominates the skyline. Its tall columns support a triangular pediment, and behind them stands the inner chamber. It was dedicated in the second century to the three chief Roman gods — Jupiter, Juno, and Minerva. An inscription links it to the reign of the emperors Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus. Look up at the pediment. A worn relief shows a figure carried away by an eagle.

Just beside the Capitol, look down at the paving. This is the Square of the Winds, and it holds the second surprise. Carved into the stone is a large circle, a kind of compass, with the names of twelve winds cut around its edge in Latin. Romans thought of winds as distinct forces, each with a name and a character. Many visitors walk straight across it without noticing. Take a moment to find the letters under your feet.

Next, find the theatre, set into the hillside. Its stone seats curve in a half circle and face out toward the open countryside. It held several thousand spectators. The Romans built it in the second century, and a local benefactor paid for it — a common way for wealthy citizens to win public honour. Sit on the upper rows if you can. The view across the valley shows why the builders chose this spot.

The theatre is not only a ruin. In modern times it hosts summer performances during a local festival, so music and voices still fill the stone bowl on some evenings. Try a simple test while you are here. Have a friend stand in the middle of the stage and speak in a normal voice. From the seats, you may hear them more clearly than you expect.

Dougga also shows the daily life of ordinary residents. Wander the narrow streets and you will find large public baths, with their furnace rooms and hot pools. Near one of the bath complexes is a public latrine. A row of stone seats curves around the wall, side by side, with no walls between them. For Romans, this was a social space. People chatted here as they would in a café. You will also see the remains of private houses, many of which once held fine mosaics. Most of the best mosaics are now displayed in the Bardo Museum in Tunis.

Further along stands the Temple of Juno Caelestis, a Roman form of the old Carthaginian goddess Tanit. Its courtyard has a rare semicircular shape. This temple is a good example of how religion here blended traditions. Local gods did not simply disappear under Rome. They often took on Roman names and kept their worshippers. Nearby, two triumphal arches mark old entrances to the town, honouring emperors from the Severan dynasty, a family with African roots.

The site has a more recent human story too. Until the twentieth century, a small village lived among these ruins, with homes built on and around the ancient walls. In the mid-twentieth century, its residents were moved to a new village nearby so that excavations could continue. Some local families still have ties to this land. You may meet people from the area working at the site or selling small goods near the entrance.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute. Notice the shadows the columns cast across the paving. Listen to the wind in the olive trees and the birds that nest in the old walls. Thousands of people once walked these same streets to the market, the baths, and the theatre. As you head back down the slope, look over your shoulder at the Capitol one more time. It will stay on the horizon long after the rest of the town drops out of sight.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for steep slopes and uneven Roman paving.
Visit early morning to avoid the strongest midday sun.
Carry water and a hat — shade is limited on site.
See the site's mosaics later at the Bardo Museum in Tunis.
Walk downhill to the mausoleum last for the best views.
