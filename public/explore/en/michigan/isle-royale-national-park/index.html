The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps against dark volcanic rock. A loon calls somewhere out on the bay, a long rising note that seems to hang in the air. The breeze off Lake Superior is cool, even in high summer, and it carries the smell of spruce, balsam fir and wet stone. There are no engines here, no traffic, no road. You have arrived on an island that most people in America will never see.

This is Isle Royale National Park, a remote archipelago in the northwest corner of Lake Superior. It belongs to Michigan, but look at a map and you will notice something odd. The island sits much closer to the shores of Minnesota and Canada than to the Michigan mainland. The main island is roughly forty-five miles long and only a few miles wide. Around it lie hundreds of smaller islands, rocky ridges that break the surface of the lake like the backs of sleeping animals.

Getting here takes effort, and that is part of the story. There are no bridges and no cars on the island. Visitors come by ferry or by seaplane, crossing open water that can turn rough in minutes. Because of this, Isle Royale is one of the least visited national parks in the lower forty-eight states. But here is the first surprise. People who do come tend to stay for days, not hours. Many return again and again, drawn back by the quiet.

The shape of the land comes from very old geology. Long ago, enormous lava flows spread across this region. Over time the layers tilted, and glaciers later scraped across them. The result is the ridge-and-valley pattern you see today. Long ridges of hard rock run parallel to one another, with narrow bays and swamps filling the low ground between them. When you walk the trails, you are walking along the edges of these ancient tilted layers.

People came to this island long before it became a park. Thousands of years ago, Indigenous peoples travelled here to mine copper. The metal lay close to the surface, sometimes in pure veins. They worked it with stone hammers and fire, leaving behind shallow pits that can still be found in the forest today. The Ojibwe, whose homelands surround the lake, know the island as Minong. Their connection to this place continues, and the Grand Portage Band in Minnesota maintains close ties to it.

In the nineteenth century, commercial copper mining companies arrived with bigger ambitions. They dug shafts, built small settlements and cleared land. But the island was hard to reach and hard to work. Most of these ventures failed within a few decades. Fishing proved more lasting. Families of Scandinavian descent set up small fisheries along the shore, catching lake trout and whitefish. They lived through short summers, cold storms and long isolation. Some of their cabins and fish houses still stand along the harbours.

The island became a national park in 1940. Later, in 1976, most of its land was designated as wilderness, which means it is managed to stay as wild as possible. In 1980 it was also named an international biosphere reserve. So when you notice the lack of signs, buildings and noise, that is not neglect. It is the plan. The park was set aside so that nature could run its own course here.

And nature has done something remarkable. Moose are not originally from the island. They arrived in the early twentieth century, most likely by swimming across from the mainland or crossing on winter ice. With no predators, their numbers grew quickly. Then, around the late 1940s, wolves crossed an ice bridge from the mainland during a hard winter. From that moment, the island became a natural laboratory for how predators and prey shape one another.

Here is the second surprise. Scientists have studied the wolves and moose of Isle Royale every year since 1958. It is the longest continuous study of a predator and its prey anywhere in the world. Over the decades, the wolf population rose and fell. By the late 2010s, inbreeding and isolation had left only a couple of wolves alive. The National Park Service then brought new wolves to the island from the mainland, and the study goes on, watching what happens next.

The island holds a few smaller wonders too. Look for a small stone with a green, turtle-shell pattern. This is Isle Royale greenstone, also called chlorastrolite, and it is the official state gem of Michigan. Collecting it inside the park is not allowed, so enjoy it where it lies. There is also a fun puzzle of geography. In Siskiwit Lake there is an island called Ryan Island. It is often described as the largest island in the largest lake on the largest island in the largest freshwater lake by surface area.

The waters around Isle Royale also hold history below the surface. The surrounding reefs and storms have claimed many ships over the years. Several wrecks lie in the cold, clear water near the island, kept in good condition because Lake Superior is so cold. Experienced divers come with proper training and gear to see them. From the shore, lighthouses mark the safe passages and remind you how dangerous these waters have always been for sailors.

On land, the way to experience the park is on foot or by paddle. Trails run along the ridges and down to quiet coves. The longest follows the Greenstone Ridge, the spine of the main island. Canoeists and kayakers move between inland lakes, carrying their boats across short portages. Campgrounds are simple. Water from the lakes must be filtered or boiled. In early summer, insects can be fierce, so long sleeves and a head net make a real difference.

You do not need to hike far to feel the island's mood. Sit on a rock near the harbour in the early morning. Mist may lift off the water. You might hear the soft splash of a moose feeding in a shallow bay, or the slow creak of spruce trees in the wind. In late summer, look for thimbleberries along the trails, their soft red fruit like a flattened raspberry. Small moments like these are what visitors remember most.

Before you move on, take one more look around you. Notice the dark rock under your feet, the deep green of the forest and the endless grey-blue of the lake. This island has been changed by lava, ice, copper miners, fishers, moose and wolves. Yet in this moment, it is mostly just quiet. As you walk away, listen for the loon one more time... and carry a little of that silence with you.
