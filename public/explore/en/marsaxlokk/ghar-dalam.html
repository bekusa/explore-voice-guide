The guide

The story

Cool air meets you first. Step from the Maltese sun into the mouth of the cave and the temperature drops, the light turns grey and soft, and the sound of traffic on the road behind you fades. The floor is damp underfoot. Somewhere ahead, water drips from the rock. You are standing at the entrance of Għar Dalam, a cave a short drive from the fishing harbour of Marsaxlokk, and you are walking into one of the oldest records of life on these islands.

The name is Maltese, and it means the Cave of Darkness. It is an honest name. The passage runs back into the limestone well beyond the reach of daylight, and only the first part is open to visitors. A walkway leads you in, past walls the colour of old honey and rust. The rest of the cave continues into the hill, closed off, as dark as it has always been.

Here is the first surprise. This cave was carved by water. Long ago, a river ran through the valley outside, which locals call Wied Dalam. Over a very long time, that water dissolved the soft limestone and opened this tunnel. Look at the ceiling and walls as you walk. The rounded, scooped shapes in the rock come from flowing water. Today the valley is dry, but the shape of the river is still here, in stone.

That river did something else. It carried mud, pebbles, and bones into the cave, and it laid them down in layers, one on top of another. Over many thousands of years, those layers built up on the cave floor. When scientists began to dig here in the nineteenth century and through the twentieth, they found that each layer held a different world. The floor of this cave is a stack of time, with the oldest at the bottom.

And those worlds are strange. The deep layers hold the remains of dwarf elephants and dwarf hippopotamuses. Yes, elephants, on Malta. Above them, in younger layers, come large numbers of deer bones. Researchers also found remains of animals like bears, wolves, and foxes, and of giant dormice, rodents far bigger than the ones alive in Europe today. None of these animals lives wild on Malta now.

So how did elephants reach a small island in the middle of the sea? The usual explanation goes back to the Ice Ages. When huge ice sheets locked up water, sea levels fell. At times, the gap between Malta and Sicily became much narrower, and animals could cross. Later, when the sea rose again, they were trapped. On a small island with limited food, many species slowly became smaller over generations. Scientists call this island dwarfism. Big animals shrink. Some small ones, like those dormice, grow larger.

The second surprise sits higher in the layers. Near the top, the bones of wild animals give way to traces of people. Archaeologists found pottery, tools, and the bones of farm animals. These finds are among the earliest evidence of human life on Malta, from roughly seven thousand years ago. The first farmers likely came across the sea from Sicily. Their pottery looks very similar to styles found there.

The cave gave its name to that moment. Archaeologists call the earliest stage of Maltese prehistory the Għar Dalam phase. Think about the scale of that. Long before the great stone temples of Malta were built, long before the knights or the British, people sheltered and worked around this very opening. The temples that visitors travel to see came later — centuries later — from the descendants of those first farming communities.

Not every story from this cave went smoothly. In the early twentieth century, a few human teeth found here were thought to belong to Neanderthals, the ancient human relatives of Ice Age Europe. It made headlines in scientific circles. Later study showed the teeth were from modern humans after all. It is a good reminder that science corrects itself, and that the soil here has been read, and reread, many times.

The cave also has a more recent chapter. During the Second World War, Malta was bombed heavily, and people across the island looked for safety underground. This cave served as a shelter in those years. So the same rock that sheltered early farmers also sheltered families listening to air-raid sirens. Stand still for a moment in the cool dark, and that long line of human use feels close.

Before or after the cave, spend time in the small museum on the site. One hall keeps an old-fashioned display, with glass cases packed with bones, teeth, and antlers, row upon row. It looks like a cabinet from an earlier age of museums, and that is part of its charm. A more modern gallery explains the layers, the animals, and the science in clearer terms. Look closely at the teeth. Their size tells you more about dwarf elephants than any label can.

Outside, the grounds are planted with trees and shrubs native to the Maltese islands. In the heat, the shade feels welcome, and the air smells of dry earth and resin. Beyond the site lies the southern coast. Marsaxlokk is close, with its brightly painted fishing boats, called luzzu, each with an eye painted on the bow. Nearby Birżebbuġa looks out over a wide bay, with the large cranes of the freeport on the horizon.

As you leave, look back at the cave mouth. It is a plain opening in a limestone hillside, easy to miss from the road. Yet behind it lies a record of hippos and elephants, of shifting seas, of the first people to farm this land. Feel the warmth return to your skin as you walk out. The valley is quiet and dry now. But under your feet, the story of the island is still being kept, layer by layer.
