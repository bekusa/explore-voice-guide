The guide

The story

Listen first. Above the stone streets of Split, the noise of scooters and café chairs fades quickly. In its place comes the dry, steady buzz of cicadas, the creak of pine branches, and the smell of warm resin rising from the ground. The path underfoot is pale limestone, polished smooth by many feet. This is Marjan, the green hill that fills the western end of the Split peninsula. For the people of this city, it is part park, part forest, and part open-air chapel.

Marjan is not a high mountain. Its highest point, called Telegrin, rises less than two hundred metres above the sea. But height is not what matters here. What matters is position. The hill sits between the old town and the open Adriatic. From its slopes you can look east over red roofs and church towers, and west toward islands that sit low and blue on the horizon. Few cities have a forest this close to their historic core.

Many visitors start from the old neighbourhood of Varoš, just west of the Roman palace walls. Here, narrow lanes climb toward a long series of stone steps. The climb is short but steep, and the steps are worn in the middle from generations of use. Take it slowly. Every pause gives you a wider view. Within a few minutes, the lanes and laundry lines below you turn into a pattern of roofs, and the harbour opens out beneath.

At the top of these steps is Vidilica, a viewpoint with a café terrace. This is the place most people photograph. From here, the shape of Split becomes clear. You can see the harbour, the ferries moving in and out, and the bell tower of the cathedral rising from the old palace. On a clear day, the islands of Brač and Šolta appear across the water. The view explains the city better than any map.

Now, a detail many visitors walk straight past. Close to Vidilica, behind a wall, is an old Jewish cemetery. It dates back several centuries, to the period when a Jewish community lived and traded in Split. It is one of the older Jewish burial grounds in this part of Europe. The gravestones are simple and weathered, set on a slope that looks toward the sea. It is a quiet place, and it carries a part of Split's story that is easy to forget.

Here is a second surprise. The thick pine forest around you looks ancient, but much of it is not. In earlier centuries, the hill was far more bare and rocky. Grazing animals and wood cutting had stripped much of the slope. Around the turn of the twentieth century, local residents organised to replant it. They planted Aleppo pines, a hardy Mediterranean tree that can grow in thin soil. The green hill you see today is largely the result of that long, patient effort.

As you walk further west, the paths divide. A wide road runs along the hill, while smaller trails cut through the trees. Along the way you will meet small churches. One of the closest to the city is the little church of Saint Nicholas, a modest stone building from the medieval period. Its walls are plain and thick. It does not try to impress. Instead, it shows how faith was built here: small, solid, and close to the landscape.

On the southern side of the hill, the cliffs hold something stranger. The church of Saint Jerome sits below a rock face, and in the cliff above it are shallow caves and hermit cells. For centuries, people seeking solitude came to Marjan to pray and live simply. Some cells are cut directly into the stone. Standing beneath them, you can imagine the silence a hermit chose here — the sea below, the city close, and yet a world away.

Marjan is also linked to Marko Marulić, a writer from Split often called the father of Croatian literature. He lived in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, and wrote in Latin and in Croatian. Tradition connects him with the area around Saint Jerome, where he is said to have spent time in retreat. Whether every detail of that story is exact, the link matters to locals. It ties the hill to the moment when Croatian became a literary language.

At the foot of the southern slope, near the sea road, stands the Meštrović Gallery. Ivan Meštrović was one of the most important Croatian sculptors of the twentieth century. The gallery is housed in a large villa he built for himself, and today it shows his work in bronze, stone, and wood. If you are walking down from the hill toward the water, it is worth noticing. Its formal gardens and columns are very different from the wild pines above.

For local families, Marjan is not a monument. It is a weekend habit. You will see runners on the main road early in the morning, and cyclists climbing slowly in the heat. Grandparents walk dogs. Teenagers sit on rocks above the sea. Near the western tip, small beaches such as Bene and Kašjuni draw swimmers through the summer. The water here is clear and the shore is mostly pebbles and rock, so people come with sandals and towels.

The hill also changes with the light. In the morning, the eastern view over the city is soft and slightly hazy. By midday, the limestone glares white and the shade under the pines becomes precious. In the evening, the sun drops toward the western islands, and the sea turns from blue to silver and then to bronze. Many people climb to the high points at this hour simply to watch the sun go down over the water.

So pause here for a moment. Feel the uneven stone under your shoes. Listen to the cicadas, and to the faint sound of the city below. Look at the pines, and remember that people planted many of them by hand. As you walk on, notice the small things — a chapel door, a worn step, a view between two trees. Marjan does not ask for attention. It simply waits above Split, as it has for generations.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the Varoš steps early to avoid the midday heat.
Carry water; shade and taps are limited on the trails.
Bring swimwear for the pebble beaches near the western tip.
Wear sturdy shoes; the limestone steps get slippery.
Return to the high points for sunset over the islands.
