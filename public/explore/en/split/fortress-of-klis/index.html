The guide

The story

Listen to the wind first. Up here, on this narrow spine of limestone, it rarely stops. It comes off the bare mountains behind you and pushes across the walls toward the sea. The stone under your feet is pale, worn smooth in places, rough and sharp in others. Below, the land falls away toward the coast, and Split spreads along the shore like a spill of white and terracotta. You are standing on one of the oldest guard posts on the Adriatic.

Welcome to the Fortress of Klis. It sits on a ridge in the gap between two mountains, Kozjak to the west and Mosor to the east. That gap matters. For thousands of years, it was the easiest route from the coast into the hinterland behind it. Whoever held this rock could watch every trader, shepherd and army moving between the sea and the interior. So almost everyone who wanted this region tried to hold it.

Look down toward the town of Solin, at the foot of the slope. That is where the Roman city of Salona once stood, the capital of the Roman province of Dalmatia. Its ruins are still there today. People had fortified this ridge even before the Romans arrived, and later the Romans used the height to protect the road toward their city. From the walls, you can see why. A lookout here could see trouble coming hours before it reached the plain.

After Rome fell, the fortress became important to the early Croatian rulers. In the medieval centuries, it was linked with the dukes and kings of Croatia, and with the powerful noble families who served them. Croatian historians often describe Klis as one of the early centres of royal power in this region. So when locals talk about this place, they speak about more than a military site. For many Croatians, it is part of the story of how their state began.

Now look at the shape of the defences. The fortress does not sit in one compact block. It stretches along the ridge in a long, thin line, with walls stepping up the rock in several levels. If attackers broke through one gate, they found another wall and another climb ahead of them. Every level gave the defenders a higher position. As you walk, notice how the path keeps turning. Those turns were deliberate, because a straight road is easier to storm.

The most famous chapter here belongs to the sixteenth century. The Ottoman Empire was pushing west across the Balkans, and fortress after fortress fell. Klis held out. Its defence was led by a captain named Petar Kružić, together with fighters known as Uskoks. The Uskoks were irregular soldiers, many of them refugees from lands the Ottomans had already taken. They fought hard and they raided often. For years, this small garrison on a bare ridge resisted one of the strongest empires in the world.

The siege did not end well for the defenders. In 1537, after a long struggle, the fortress fell to the Ottomans, and Kružić was killed. A popular local tale says that his head was displayed by the victors, and that his sister paid a ransom to bring it home for burial. It is a story Croatians still tell. Whatever the exact details, the surviving Uskoks moved north, to the coastal town of Senj, and continued their fight from there for many decades.

Here is something many visitors walk right past. Inside the walls stands a small church dedicated to Saint Vitus. Under Ottoman rule, this building served as a mosque. When the Venetians took the fortress in the seventeenth century, they turned it into a church. Look closely at its form and you may notice that it does not quite match a typical Dalmatian chapel. One building carries the memory of two faiths and two empires, side by side on the same rock.

The Venetians captured Klis in 1648. After them came other rulers, including the Austrians, who held the fortress in the nineteenth century. Each new owner repaired walls, added cannon positions and changed the buildings to suit newer weapons. That is why the masonry around you is so mixed. Some stones are rough and irregular. Others are cut neatly into blocks. You are looking at many centuries of repair, layer upon layer, built by people who never met each other.

Now a surprise of a very different kind. If the walls seem familiar, there may be a reason. The fortress was used as a filming location for the television series Game of Thrones. It appeared as part of the city of Meereen, a slave city ruled by force in the story. Fans still come up here to find the exact spots from those scenes. It is a strange modern twist for a place that saw so many real sieges.

Take a moment at the highest point you can safely reach. On a clear day, the view runs over Split, across the channel, and out to the islands lying low on the water. Turn around, and the scene changes completely. Behind you is a hard, dry country of grey rock and scrub. This is the edge between two worlds, the soft Mediterranean coast and the tough inland highlands. The fortress was built exactly on that line.

When you leave, take time for the village of Klis itself. The houses climb the slopes below the walls, and life here moves at its own pace. The area has a strong local tradition of lamb roasted slowly on a spit, and you may smell wood smoke near the roadside restaurants. Families still gather here on weekends for long lunches. It is a good reminder that this place belongs to the people who live under it today, as well as to its past.

Before you walk down, stand still for one more minute. Feel the wind again, the same wind that pushed against Roman lookouts, Croatian nobles, Uskok fighters and Ottoman soldiers. Look at the pale stone, warm in the sun, cracked and patched by so many hands. Then look down at the road winding toward the coast. Everyone who held this ridge watched that road. Now, for a short time, you are watching it too.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for steep, uneven limestone paths.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed ridge.
Come on a clear day for views to islands.
Carry water because the upper walls offer little shade.
Try spit-roasted lamb in Klis village afterward.
