The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the open paving of the square. The stone under your feet is pale and smooth, worn by more than a century of evening crowds. Above you, the theatre's long facade catches the light. In the morning it looks almost cream. By late afternoon it turns a warm honey colour. Pigeons settle on the cornices, and the noise of the old town feels a few streets away.

This is the Croatian National Theatre in Split. Locals simply call it the HNK, after the initials of its Croatian name. It is the city's main home for drama, opera and ballet. It also sits outside the walls of Diocletian's Palace, the Roman complex that forms the heart of old Split. That position matters. When the theatre was built, standing outside the ancient core was a statement about a growing, modern city.

The building opened in 1893, at the end of the nineteenth century. This was a tense, hopeful period on the Dalmatian coast. Dalmatia is the long coastal region of southern Croatia, and at that time it belonged to the Austro-Hungarian Empire. Italian was the language of much of the local elite. A strong Croatian national movement was pushing back. For its supporters, a theatre that performed in Croatian was a public act of identity, not only a place for entertainment.

The square in front of you carries the name of Gajo Bulat. He was a mayor of Split in the late nineteenth century and a leading figure in that Croatian revival. So the theatre and the square belong to the same story. Together they mark the moment when Split began to describe itself, in stone and in public life, as a Croatian city. It is worth keeping that in mind as you look up at the facade.

Now look at the architecture itself. The style is broadly Neo-Renaissance. That means the builders borrowed the calm symmetry, columns and rounded arches of the Italian Renaissance, the great revival of classical forms in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Notice how the facade is divided into clear horizontal bands. The ground level feels heavy and solid. The upper levels feel lighter, with tall windows and decorative frames. The whole front is built to look ordered and dignified.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. For its time, this was a very large theatre for a city of Split's size. It was planned for an audience far bigger than the town strictly needed. That ambition was deliberate. The people behind it wanted a cultural house that could stand beside the theatres of larger European cities. The scale you see today is a sign of how much confidence, and how much civic pride, went into the project.

The second surprise is less happy. In 1970, a fire broke out and badly damaged the building. For several years, the city's main stage stood largely ruined. Reconstruction took most of the decade. The restored theatre reopened in 1979, the same year Split hosted the Mediterranean Games, a large international sporting event. The city was renewing itself for that moment, and the theatre was part of the effort. So the building you see is both original and carefully rebuilt.

When you look closely at the stone and the plaster, you may notice that some surfaces look crisper than others. That difference is a quiet trace of the restoration. Restorers tried to return the theatre to its nineteenth century appearance rather than modernise it. The goal was continuity. For the people of Split, the old theatre was a familiar landmark, and they wanted it back as they remembered it, not replaced by something new.

Inside, the auditorium follows the classic horseshoe shape of European opera houses. Tiers of seating curve around the stage, so the audience can see one another as well as the performers. This design made a night at the theatre a social event. People came to watch the stage, but also to be seen. If you attend a performance, take a moment before the lights go down. Look up at the tiers and the ceiling, and listen to the room fill with voices.

The theatre has separate companies for drama, opera and ballet. On a single week you might find a Croatian play, an Italian opera and a classical ballet on the programme. Many drama performances are in Croatian, while operas are usually sung in their original language. The theatre is also closely linked to the Split Summer Festival. During the summer, productions often move outdoors, into the old town and the courtyards of Diocletian's Palace, where ancient stone becomes a stage set.

Step back now and look at the square as a whole. It is one of the more open spaces in central Split. Cafés line parts of it, and in the evening people gather on the steps and benches before a show. Across the square you will also notice a church with a strikingly modern exterior. The contrast is part of the character of this place. A nineteenth century theatre and a twentieth century church face each other across a single public space.

This square is also a useful pause between two very different parts of the city. Behind you, a short walk leads into the narrow lanes of the Roman palace. In the other direction, the streets open toward newer neighbourhoods. Many locals pass through here every day without looking up. For them, the theatre is simply part of the route to work, to the market or to coffee with friends. That everyday quality is part of its charm.

Before you move on, look once more at the facade. Find the line where the warm stone meets the sky. Listen to the sounds around you, the scrape of café chairs, a scooter in the distance, footsteps on the pale paving. This building has survived empire, fire and reconstruction, and it still opens its doors most evenings. As you walk away, you are crossing the same square that Split's theatregoers have crossed since 1893.
