The guide

The story

Listen first. Before the engine settles, before anyone speaks, there is a dry rattle of teak leaves overhead. The leaves are huge, some as wide as a dinner plate, and they fall onto the track with a papery crack. The air smells of dust and warm bark. Somewhere ahead, a bird calls in a sharp, repeating note. You are inside Tadoba Andhari Tiger Reserve, in the Chandrapur district of eastern Maharashtra. This is a forest that rewards patience, and it asks you to use your ears as much as your eyes.

The reserve carries two names, and each one points to something real on this land. Tadoba comes from Taru, a figure honoured by the Gond people, the Indigenous community whose villages have stood in and around these hills for generations. Andhari is the name of the river that runs through the southern part of the forest. Put together, the name joins a local deity and a moving body of water. That is a fair summary of what keeps this place alive — belief and water.

Locals tell a story about Taru. According to one popular tale, he was a village chief who fought a tiger near the lake and died in the struggle. People later built a shrine in his memory, and they still visit it. Nobody can confirm the story as recorded history. It is folklore, passed down by word of mouth. But it carries a truth everyone here understands. In this forest, people and tigers have always shared the same ground, and not always peacefully.

The protected history is easier to trace. Tadoba was declared a national park in the 1950s, which makes it one of the oldest national parks in Maharashtra. The Andhari Wildlife Sanctuary was added decades later, in the 1980s. In the mid-1990s, the two were joined into a single tiger reserve. Today it is the largest national park in the state, and it stands among the best-known tiger landscapes in central India.

Now look at the trees around you. This is dry deciduous forest. That means most trees drop their leaves in the hot months to save water. Teak dominates much of the reserve, with its broad leaves and straight grey trunks. Clumps of bamboo lean over the tracks like green fountains. In the hottest weeks, the forest turns gold and brown, and the ground fills with fallen leaves. Here is the first surprise. That bare, open look is good news for visitors, because it makes animals far easier to see.

Watch for one tree in particular. Its bark is cracked into small, square plates, like the skin of a crocodile. Locals often call it the crocodile bark tree. Look too for the mahua tree, with its thick, spreading crown. Its pale, fleshy flowers fall to the ground in spring. People collect them, and so do bears, deer and birds. When the mahua flowers fall, the sweet smell drifts across the track, and half the forest comes to feed.

At the heart of the reserve lies Tadoba Lake. Water is the reason this forest holds so much life. In the dry season, the lake and the scattered waterholes become meeting points. Deer come down to drink with nervous, jerky steps. Wild boar dig in the soft mud at the edge. And in the shallows, you may notice what looks like a floating log. Look again. Mugger crocodiles live in these waters, and they are very patient hunters.

The animal everyone hopes to see is, of course, the tiger. Tadoba has a strong reputation for tiger sightings, and many visitors leave with a story. But the forest holds much more. Leopards move through the same terrain, usually keeping out of the tigers' way. Sloth bears shuffle between termite mounds, sucking up insects with a loud, wet snort. Packs of dhole, the reddish wild dogs of India, hunt together and whistle to each other. Even without a tiger, a single drive here can be extraordinary.

Here is the second surprise. The best tiger spotters are often not human. Spotted deer, called chital, give a sharp, high alarm call when a predator moves nearby. Sambar, the large brown deer, give a deep, honking bark. Langur monkeys shout from the treetops, because they can see far across the forest. When your driver suddenly stops the vehicle and switches off the engine, this is usually why. The forest is talking, and the guides know how to listen.

The largest animal you may meet is the gaur, sometimes called the Indian bison. It is a massive wild cattle species, dark brown, with pale stockings on its legs like white socks. A big bull looks almost too heavy for its own legs. Gaur often graze in calm groups near the meadows. Give them space. They look gentle, but they are strong, and even tigers usually think twice before facing a healthy adult.

The reserve is divided into a core zone and a surrounding buffer zone. The core is the most protected area, with the strictest rules. The buffer zone includes villages, farmland and forest edges, where people and wildlife live close together. Visitors enter through several gates, and each one leads into a different part of the forest. Safaris run in open vehicles with a trained local guide. The vehicles keep to marked tracks, and the rules are strict for good reason. The animals come first here.

This closeness between people and wildlife is the real story of Tadoba. Families in the nearby villages grow crops, graze cattle and collect forest produce like tendu leaves and mahua flowers. Tigers and leopards sometimes wander beyond the forest edge. Living alongside large predators brings real risks and real losses. Many local people now work as guides, drivers and naturalists, and tourism brings income to the area. The future of the reserve depends on these neighbours as much as on any fence or law.

Before you leave, take a moment of stillness. Ask your driver to cut the engine, if it is safe. Listen to the leaves crack under some unseen footstep. Notice the light as it moves through the bamboo, turning the dust into a soft haze. Look at the track in front of you, and you may see fresh pugmarks, the paw prints of a big cat, pressed into the dust. Something passed this way not long ago. It may be watching you now, quietly, from the shade.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book your safari permit well before you travel.
Choose early morning drives for cooler air and active animals.
Wear muted earth-toned clothing to blend into the forest.
Bring binoculars to spot animals far across meadows.
Stay quiet during alarm calls so everyone can listen.
