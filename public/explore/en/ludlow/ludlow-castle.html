The guide

The story

Listen first. Rooks call from the high walls, and somewhere below, the River Teme runs over stones. Underfoot there is grass where floors once stood, and the air carries a faint, cool smell of old stone. Ludlow Castle does not rise on its own like a fortress in a painting. It grows out of the rock itself, on a ridge above the river. That pairing of castle and cliff is the first thing to notice. Everything else here grew from it.

The castle began in the late eleventh century, in the years after the Norman Conquest of 1066. The de Lacy family, Norman lords given land along this unsettled border with Wales, raised the first walls. This was frontier country. The castle was not built to impress a court. It was built to hold ground. The thick masonry, the high position, and the steep drop to the river all served one purpose, which was defence.

Now look at the tall building near the entrance to the inner bailey, the enclosed heart of the castle. This is the Great Tower. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The tower did not start life as a keep. It began as a gatehouse, a fortified passage into the castle. Later builders blocked the old entrance and turned the gatehouse into a solid tower. If you look closely at the stonework, you can sometimes see where openings were filled in.

Inside the inner bailey stands something unusual. It is a round chapel, dedicated to Saint Mary Magdalene. Very few churches in England have a circular nave, the main space where worshippers gather. The round shape copies the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem, the site Christians honour as the tomb of Christ. Crusaders and pilgrims carried that memory home. Here, on a Welsh border hill, a small echo of Jerusalem still stands, open to the sky.

The castle changed hands over the centuries. In the fourteenth century it passed to the Mortimer family, among the most powerful lords of the Welsh border, which the English called the Marches. Under the Mortimers, the castle became less of a raw fortress and more of a grand residence. They added comfortable private rooms, a great hall for feasting, and chambers with large windows. You can still trace those later buildings along the north side of the inner bailey.

Walk toward that range of buildings. Here you will find the shell of the great hall, with the private apartments beside it. Look up at the window openings. They are wider and more generous than the narrow slits of the early defences. That change in window shape tells a simple story. When a lord feels safe enough to let in light, the castle has started to become a home. Picture fires in the hearths, tapestries on the walls, and the noise of a full household.

In time, Ludlow became a royal castle. Through the Mortimer line, it passed to the House of York, and so to the English crown. In the later fifteenth century, King Edward the Fourth sent his young son here to be raised. When the king died in 1483, that boy became Edward the Fifth. He was at Ludlow when the news arrived. He travelled toward London, and he never ruled. He is remembered as one of the Princes in the Tower, whose fate is still debated.

A second royal story is just as poignant. Prince Arthur, the eldest son of King Henry the Seventh, came to Ludlow with his young bride, Catherine of Aragon. They lived here as the Prince and Princess of Wales. Arthur died at Ludlow in 1502, only months after the wedding. Catherine later married his younger brother, who became Henry the Eighth. Their marriage, and its collapse, helped lead England to break with the Church of Rome. A turning point in English history has a quiet link to these rooms.

For almost two centuries, Ludlow was also a seat of government. The Council of Wales and the Marches met here. This body ran the administration and the courts for Wales and the border counties. Judges, clerks, and petitioners came and went through these gates. The castle was busy with law and paperwork as much as with soldiers. The Council was finally abolished in 1689, and after that the castle slowly lost its purpose.

Here is a surprise for lovers of literature. In 1634, the poet John Milton wrote a masque, a kind of courtly drama with music and verse, known today as Comus. It was first performed here at Ludlow Castle, for the family of the Earl of Bridgewater, who was then President of the Council. Young members of the family took roles in the performance. Theatre has returned many times since. In summer, open-air plays have often filled the outer bailey.

During the English Civil War in the 1640s, the castle was held for the king and later surrendered to Parliament. In the years that followed, it fell into decay. Roofs were stripped, timber and lead were taken, and the walls were left to the weather. In 1811, the castle was bought by the Earl of Powis, and it has remained in that family's care. Because of that, the ruin was stabilised and kept open rather than lost completely.

Step back into the wide outer bailey and look toward the town. Ludlow grew up at the castle gate. Its streets were laid out in a planned pattern, running down the slope toward the river. Castle Square, just outside, still hosts a regular market, and the town is known for its food. Food festivals have used the castle grounds as their stage. So the space between town and castle is still shared, as it has been for close to a thousand years.

Before you leave, find a spot near the walls where you can see the river and the hills beyond. This was the view the guards watched, looking west toward Wales. Notice the colour of the stone, warm in the sun and grey in the shade. Notice how the round chapel, the tall tower, and the empty windows sit together inside one enclosure. As you walk back through the gate and down into the town, listen again for the rooks... still keeping watch from the walls.
