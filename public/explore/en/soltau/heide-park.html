The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you look up. Somewhere above the pine trees, a long mechanical rattle climbs slowly. It goes quiet, and then a wave of screams rolls down the track and fades into the forest. Under your feet the paths are smooth and wide, and the air smells of resin, sandy soil and frying sugar. This is Heide Park, on the edge of the small town of Soltau in northern Germany. It is one of the largest theme parks in the country, set in pine forest and open heathland.

To understand this place, start with the land around it. Soltau sits in the Lüneburg Heath, a wide region of sandy soil, juniper bushes, birch and pine. For centuries this was poor farming country. Shepherds grazed a hardy local breed of sheep called Heidschnucke on the open heath. In late summer the heather flowers, and whole hillsides turn a soft purple. If you drove here along the motorway between Hamburg and Hanover, you crossed some of that landscape. The park took its name from it. Heide simply means heath.

Heide Park opened in 1978 as a modest leisure park, far smaller than what you see today. Over the following decades it grew ride by ride and themed area by themed area. The forest was cleared in places, but much of it stayed. That is why so many paths still run under tall pines. Today the park belongs to Merlin Entertainments, the British company that also runs the Legoland parks and Madame Tussauds. Since the takeover, the park has added hotel stays and short-break packages, and now calls itself a resort.

Now look for the wooden giant. Colossos is a huge wooden roller coaster, and it opened in 2001. For a time it ranked among the tallest wooden coasters in the world. Its frame is a dense lattice of timber beams. From below it looks almost like a forest built by people. Wooden coasters ride differently from steel ones. They rattle, they sway a little, and the noise is part of the experience. After several years out of service, Colossos returned in 2024 with a new theme about a battle of giants.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. Wood is a living material, even after it is cut. It swells in wet weather and shrinks in dry heat. A wooden coaster therefore needs constant care. Maintenance crews check the track, replace worn planks and tighten bolts again and again. The long closure of Colossos was largely about this kind of work. So when you hear that deep, rolling roar, you are hearing timber and steel wheels in a long and careful partnership.

Not far away, water and steel meet. Krake, which means octopus in German, is a dive coaster that opened in 2011. The train climbs, stops for a long moment at the edge of a steep drop, and then falls almost straight down. At the bottom it rushes into a lake and throws up a wall of spray. Many people stand at the railing just to watch that splash. On a warm day, the people who stand closest often leave with wet shirts and big smiles.

The park has many other thrill rides, and each one offers a different sensation. On Limit, riders hang below the track with their legs swinging free. Desert Race does not climb a lift hill. Instead it launches its train forward at high speed in a few seconds. Flug der Dämonen, the Flight of the Demons, is a wing coaster. Riders sit on seats that stick out to the sides of the track, with nothing above or below them. It feels close to flying.

If you prefer to keep your feet on the ground, watch the riders instead. The faces at the top of a lift hill are often more interesting than the drop itself. Some people laugh nervously. Some close their eyes. Some lift both arms high. Down at the exits, groups of friends crowd around small screens to find their ride photos. The shared joke afterwards is a large part of the day. A theme park is a kind of stage, and the audience plays the main role.

Heide Park is not only for adrenaline seekers. Between the large coasters there are gentler rides, play areas for small children, and themed zones with their own buildings and music. Families with young children often spend whole afternoons in these quieter corners. Wooden bridges, painted facades and small lakes break up the large spaces. Benches wait under the trees. When the queues are long, these shaded spots are a good place to rest and plan the next ride.

Here is a second surprise. Germany's best-known parks are often in the south, near the Alps or the Black Forest. Yet this park, on flat northern heathland, holds some of the country's most powerful rides. Flat land has a quiet advantage for builders. There is plenty of space, and the open sky lets tall structures stand out from far away. On the approach road you may see the top of a coaster above the treeline long before you reach the gate.

The park follows the seasons. It closes for the cold winter months and opens again in spring. In autumn it often decorates for Halloween with pumpkins, fog and costumed actors. The feel of the place changes with the weather. On a sunny summer day the paths are crowded and loud. On a cool, grey morning the pines drip, the queues are short, and the steel tracks shine with damp. Each version has its own character.

Outside the park, the town of Soltau is worth a short visit. It is a small heathland town with red-brick buildings and a calm centre. The wider region invites slow travel too. You can cycle quiet lanes, walk across open heath, and meet shepherds with their flocks. Many visitors come only for the rides and leave without seeing any of this. If you have an extra day, the heath offers a very different kind of quiet.

Before you move on, stop for a moment. Look up at the nearest track and follow its line against the sky. Listen to the chain lift ticking slowly upward. Smell the pine resin and the sweet food stalls. Feel the sandy ground under your shoes, the same soil that shepherds walked for centuries. All around you, people are choosing to be a little afraid, together, for fun. As you walk on, notice the trees between the rides. The heath is still here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the biggest coasters first, before queues grow.
Bring a light rain jacket for northern German weather.
Stand near the Krake lake railing for the splashdown.
Rest in shaded forest benches between big rides.
Add a day to walk or cycle the nearby heath.
