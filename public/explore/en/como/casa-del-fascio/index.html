The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look at the white face in front of you. The surface is pale marble, smooth and cool, almost chalky in the Lombard light. A grid of openings runs across it, some filled with glass and some left open to the sky. Behind you rise the dark stone and the dome of Como's cathedral. In front of you is a building that looks like a diagram drawn with a ruler. These two buildings stand only a few steps apart, but they come from completely different worlds.

This is the Casa del Fascio, the former local headquarters of Mussolini's National Fascist Party. The architect was Giuseppe Terragni, a young man from the Como area. He designed it in the early 1930s, and it was finished in the middle of that decade. Today many architects call it one of the most important buildings of twentieth-century Italy. That is an uncomfortable fact for many people. It is a beautiful building made for an ugly political purpose, and both truths stand here together.

Let us begin with its shape, because the shape is the whole idea. The building is close to a perfect half-cube. Its footprint is a square, and its height is half the width of that square. Terragni worked with careful ratios and repeated proportions. You can feel this order even without measuring anything. Your eye moves across the facade and finds rhythm everywhere: frames inside frames, solid beside empty, light beside shadow.

Now look again at the four sides as you walk around the block. Here is the first surprise. No two facades are the same. One side is a deep open grid, almost like a cage. Another side is mostly flat wall with a few careful cuts. Terragni treated each face as a separate composition. He responded to what each side looks at: the cathedral, the street, or the old city behind it.

The style is called Rationalism. In simple words, this was the Italian branch of modern architecture between the two world wars. Rationalist architects rejected decoration for its own sake. They believed in clean geometry, reinforced concrete, large windows and honest structure. Terragni and a group of young colleagues pushed these ideas in Italy in the late 1920s. Como, a quiet city on a lake, became one of the places where this new architecture took shape.

The building carries a political idea in its glass. A phrase is often linked to it: that fascism should be a house of glass, open for every citizen to see inside. Terragni used this image to explain his design. The building is full of transparency. There is glass in the walls, glass in the doors, and glass blocks in the ceilings. Many historians point out the obvious irony. A dictatorship is not transparent at all. The building made a promise that the regime never kept.

The ground floor holds the second surprise. The main entrance has a row of glass doors facing the square. Terragni designed them so they could open together. When the doors opened, the crowd in the piazza could flow directly into the building. The street and the interior would become one space. It was architecture designed for mass gatherings, for speeches and rallies. Standing here now, in a calm square, it is hard to imagine those crowds.

Inside, the heart of the building is a tall central hall. It was the meeting space, and the offices stood around it on the upper floors. Above the hall, a ceiling of glass blocks lets soft daylight fall into the centre. Terragni also designed much of the furniture, including tubular steel chairs and desks. The painter Mario Radice created abstract panels for the interior. Every detail, from the stairs to the lamps, belonged to one total design.

A quick practical note. After the Second World War, the building passed to new owners. For many decades it has been used by the Guardia di Finanza, Italy's financial police. This means the interior is usually not open to the public. Special openings and guided visits sometimes happen, but you should not expect them. For most visitors, the experience is the exterior and the square, and that is still a great deal.

Now think about the man who drew all of this. Giuseppe Terragni was born in 1904 and died in 1943, when he was only thirty-nine. His career lasted less than fifteen years. During the war he served at the front, including in Russia. He came home exhausted and ill, and he died soon after in Como. In that short time he produced a small group of buildings that architects still study closely today.

If you have time, you can follow his work through the city. Down near the lakefront stands the Novocomum, an apartment block from the late 1920s. Its rounded glass corners shocked many locals when it appeared. Near the stadium, by the water, stands the war memorial built from a sketch by the futurist architect Antonio Sant'Elia, another son of Como. Terragni completed the design. Together these buildings form a quiet modernist trail through the town.

Before you leave, step back toward the edge of the square. Look at the Casa del Fascio and the cathedral apse in the same view. The cathedral took centuries to build. It is layered, carved and full of figures. The modern building is flat, white and silent. Terragni clearly thought about this view. He placed his clean geometry in a direct conversation with the old stone. Neither building feels weaker because the other is there.

So take a last look at the marble grid. Notice how the shadows inside each opening change as clouds move across the sun. Listen to the scooters, the church bells, the voices crossing the square. This is now an ordinary corner of daily life in Como. As you walk away, you may see the city a little differently... as a place where old stone and new ideas still stand side by side.

The row of glass entrance doors facing the square.
Glass-block ceilings glimpsed through the ground-floor glass.
The cathedral dome reflected in the building's windows.
Before you go

Practical tips

Walk all four sides to compare each different facade.
Stand by the cathedral apse to frame both buildings.
Watch for occasional special openings of the interior.
Combine with the Novocomum on the lakefront nearby.
Come in morning light for the sharpest facade shadows.
