The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Insects hum in the long grass. Somewhere below, a river moves slowly through green lowland. The ground under your feet feels like an ordinary low hill, soft with red earth and covered in pasture. It is not ordinary. You are standing on one of the oldest centres of power in the Americas.

This is San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán, an archaeological zone in the southern part of the state of Veracruz. Many visitors come here from Villahermosa, the capital of neighbouring Tabasco, after a long drive west across the Gulf Coast lowlands. The name can confuse people. This Tenochtitlán has no link to the Aztec capital under modern Mexico City. Tenochtitlán is simply the name of the nearby village, and the archaeological zone takes its name from the local communities around it.

The people who built this place are known today as the Olmec. That name was given by modern scholars. We do not know what these people called themselves. Their culture grew in the hot, wet lands along the southern Gulf of Mexico. Many researchers describe the Olmec as one of the earliest complex societies in Mesoamerica. Mesoamerica is the cultural region that later produced the Maya and the Aztecs.

San Lorenzo reached its peak roughly three thousand years ago. Most archaeologists place its main period of power somewhere between about 1200 and 900 BC. That is many centuries before the great Maya cities of the classic period. When you think about the age of this hill, remember this detail. The famous Olmec site of La Venta, whose stone monuments you may see in Villahermosa, became powerful mostly after San Lorenzo began to decline.

Now look again at the landscape around you. Here is the first surprise. Much of this plateau is shaped by human hands. Archaeologists have shown that the Olmec moved enormous amounts of earth to level and extend the natural hill. They built terraces and platforms on its slopes. What looks like a natural rise in the land is partly an ancient construction project, carried out without metal tools, wheels, or pack animals.

The most famous objects from San Lorenzo are its colossal heads. These are huge human faces carved from basalt, a hard, dark volcanic stone. Each head wears a close-fitting headdress, a little like a helmet. The faces have full lips, broad noses, and heavy eyelids. Many scholars believe they show individual rulers, because each face is different. Archaeologists have recovered around ten of these heads from San Lorenzo alone.

Here is the second surprise. There is no basalt here. The nearest good source of this stone lies in the Tuxtla Mountains, a volcanic range many kilometres to the northwest. The Olmec moved blocks weighing many tonnes across swamp, river, and forest to reach this plateau. Researchers still debate exactly how they did it. Many think rafts on the rivers played a part, along with a great deal of human muscle and careful organisation.

The story of discovery is also part of this place. In the 1940s, the American archaeologist Matthew Stirling worked here and brought the colossal heads to international attention. In the 1960s, Michael Coe led further excavations and helped establish how old the site really was. Later, the Mexican archaeologist Ann Cyphers directed long-term research projects here. Her teams found more monuments and studied how ordinary people lived around the elite centre.

Not every discovery is a giant face. Excavations here uncovered a system of carved basalt channels. These stone troughs fit together end to end, and they appear to have carried water. Some scholars connect them with ritual, perhaps linked to water and fertility. Others stress their practical role. Either way, they show that the Olmec planned their centre with care, and that they invested heavy stone in more than portraits.

Many of the monuments found here were damaged or buried in ancient times. Faces were broken, and some sculptures were deliberately placed in the ground. For many years, people read this as violent destruction. Today, several researchers suggest another idea. They think some monuments may have been recycled or ritually retired. One popular theory says some colossal heads were recarved from older stone thrones. This remains an interpretation, not a settled fact.

You may notice that most of the great sculptures are not standing in the open here. Over the decades, many were moved to museums, including the anthropology museum in Xalapa and the national museum in Mexico City. Some pieces stay closer to home, in a small local museum near the site. Seeing a head in its home landscape, even in a modest room, feels very different from seeing it in a city gallery.

The villages around the zone are part of the experience too. You will see farmers, cattle pastures, and simple houses with tin or palm roofs. Children often walk the same paths that lead across the old plateau. People in these communities have lived beside the monuments for generations. Some local residents help with excavations and care for the museum. Their knowledge of the land is part of how this past survives today.

As you prepare to leave, look out from the edge of the plateau once more. Notice the flat green land below, the line of trees along the water, the heavy, warm air. Three thousand years ago, people stood on this same height and looked over these same rivers. They moved mountains of earth and carried stone from distant volcanoes. Take one last look at the soil under your shoes... and then walk slowly back down the hill.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan a full day trip westward from Villahermosa into Veracruz.
Bring water, sun protection, and insect repellent for open pasture.
Visit the small local museum to see sculptures near their origin.
Wear closed shoes for uneven, muddy paths across the plateau.
Pair the trip with La Venta monuments in Villahermosa.
