The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind moves across open grass. Somewhere a farm vehicle hums along a narrow road, and a crow calls from a line of trees. Under your feet the ground is flat and soft, and around you the sky feels very wide. It does not look like much at first. But you are standing in what was once the heart of Japan's first planned capital city, Fujiwara-kyō.

Turn slowly in a full circle and look at the horizon. Three low, rounded hills sit around this plain. To the east is Mount Kagu. To the west is Mount Unebi, the tallest of the three. To the north is Mount Miminashi. Together they are called the Yamato Sanzan, the Three Mountains of Yamato. The palace was placed here, in the space between them, and this choice was not an accident.

In the late seventh century, the Japanese court looked hard at China. The Tang dynasty ruled a powerful state, with written law, a large bureaucracy and enormous cities laid out on a grid. Japanese rulers wanted the same order and the same authority. Planning for a fixed, permanent capital began under Emperor Tenmu. After his death, his widow continued the work. She took the throne as Empress Jitō, and in 694 the court moved here.

This was a real change in how Japan was governed. Before this time, the court often moved its palace when a new ruler came to the throne. Each reign had its own residence, and the buildings were usually simple. Fujiwara-kyō was different. It was meant to last. Streets ran north to south and east to west in a regular grid. Officials, temples and ordinary households all had their place inside this pattern. The city was a statement — a picture of a centralised state, drawn on the land.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. In Chinese capitals such as Chang'an, the palace usually stood at the northern edge of the city. At Fujiwara-kyō, archaeologists found that the palace stood close to the centre of the grid. Why the planners did this is still debated. Some scholars connect it to older Chinese classical texts that describe an ideal city with the ruler at its middle. Others see a local adaptation. Either way, this capital was not a simple copy.

The buildings themselves were also new for Japan. The main halls of the palace stood on stone foundations, and their roofs were covered with fired clay tiles. Until then, tiled roofs were used mainly for Buddhist temples. Palace buildings had thatch or wooden shingles. Putting heavy tiles on government halls was a deliberate choice. It made the palace look solid, permanent and modern, in the style of the continent. Huge numbers of tiles had to be produced, and kilns in several regions supplied them.

Now look for the low raised ground and the markers on the site. At the centre stood the Daigokuden, the Great Hall of State, where the ruler appeared for major ceremonies. Today its location is shown by a raised earthen platform and a stone marker. In parts of the site you can also see tall vermilion pillars. They are not original. They are modern markers, placed where excavations found the positions of ancient posts, so that visitors can picture the scale of the walls and halls.

Some of the most important things found here are small. Archaeologists have dug up many mokkan. These are thin strips of wood with writing in ink. Officials used them as labels for tax goods, as notes and as practice sheets. They record shipments of rice, salt and seafood sent from provinces across Japan. These humble pieces of wood show the new state working day to day. They show a government that could count, record and collect.

That government had a legal framework. In 701, while the court was still here, the Taihō Code was issued. It was a body of criminal and administrative law, based on Chinese models. It set out ranks for officials, ministries, land rules and tax duties. The code shaped Japanese government for a long time. So this quiet field connects to a very large idea — the first full attempt to rule the islands through written law.

There is a second surprise. For many years, people assumed Fujiwara-kyō was smaller than the capital that followed it at Nara. But excavations, especially from the 1990s, found traces of city streets far beyond the area that scholars had expected. Many researchers now believe the city was larger than Heijō-kyō, the Nara capital. Yet it lasted only sixteen years. In 710, under Empress Genmei, the court moved north to Nara. The reasons are still discussed, including drainage, the city's layout and new knowledge of Tang design.

The hills around you also have a place in poetry. The Manyōshū is the oldest surviving collection of Japanese poems, compiled in the eighth century. One famous poem is attributed to Empress Jitō. In it, she notices that spring has passed and summer seems to have come, because white robes are hung out to dry on Mount Kagu. Look east at that same hill. The poem is a small, domestic picture — washing in the sun — from the ruler of a brand-new capital.

Today the site is protected as a Special Historic Site, and much of it is open land. Local people and the city plant flowers across parts of the field through the year. In spring you may see yellow rapeseed blossoms. In summer, ponds and beds of lotus open in the morning. In autumn, wide areas of cosmos flowers move in the wind. These flowers are modern, but they make the outline of the old palace easier to feel, because nothing tall blocks the view.

Before you leave, look again at the three hills. They have not moved since Empress Jitō saw them. The grid streets are under the soil now, and the tiled halls are gone. But the space is still here — the same plain, the same sky, the same low mountains. As you walk away, notice how quiet it is. Thirteen centuries ago, this ground held the noise of officials, carts and builders. Now it holds wind, birds and flowers.
