The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see much of anything, you hear water. It runs in thin channels, drops over stone lips, and splashes into basins somewhere above you on the hill. The air under the plane trees is cooler than the street outside, and it smells of wet moss and clipped box hedge. This is Villa Lante, in the small town of Bagnaia, a few kilometres from the centre of Viterbo. And almost everything here was designed around that sound.

Villa Lante is not really a house with a garden. It is a garden with two small houses placed politely to one side. It was created in the sixteenth century as a summer retreat for Cardinal Giovanni Francesco Gambara, who was the bishop of Viterbo. The design is usually attributed to Jacopo Barozzi da Vignola, one of the leading architects of the Italian Renaissance. The documents are not complete, so scholars speak of an attribution rather than a signed contract. But the hand of a great designer is clear in every terrace.

The style here is often called Mannerist. In simple terms, that is the late phase of the Renaissance, when artists took the calm, balanced forms of earlier decades and began to play with them. They added surprise, wit, and a little theatre. Here that playfulness is expressed through water. Every fountain is a small performance, and the whole hillside is arranged as one long sequence that you are meant to walk, slowly, from one scene to the next.

The sequence has a story. Many historians read the garden as a journey from wild nature to human civilisation. At the top of the slope, water comes out of a grotto as if from the raw earth. As it travels downhill, it passes through more and more shaped and controlled forms. By the bottom, it sits in a perfectly square pool inside a geometric parterre. Nature at the top, order at the bottom. You can follow that idea simply by following the water.

So let us start where the water starts. At the highest point is the Fountain of the Deluge, a rough, mossy grotto flanked by small pavilions. The stone here is left deliberately rugged. It recalls the great flood of ancient myth, when the world was washed clean and began again. Just below it sits the Fountain of the Dolphins, an octagonal basin where carved sea creatures spray water. The scene is still wild, but already a little more organised.

Now look down the slope at the long stone channel running between the steps. This is the water chain, and it holds one of the best jokes in the garden. Its curling sides are shaped like the claws and legs of a crayfish. In Italian, a crayfish or shrimp is called a gambero. That is a pun on the patron's name, Gambara. The cardinal's emblem appears all over the villa, but here it is turned into running water you can follow with your eyes.

The chain empties into the Fountain of the Giants. Two large reclining figures rest on either side of a basin. They represent river gods, and they are commonly identified as the Tiber and the Arno, the great rivers of Rome and Florence. Water spills from the vessels they hold, cascading into a pool below. It is a reminder that this water, coming down from the hills above Viterbo, is joining a much larger world of rivers, cities, and power.

Just below the giants is something many visitors walk past without a second look. It is a long stone table, and down its centre runs a narrow channel of water. This is known as the Cardinal's Table. The idea was simple and very civilised. Guests dining outdoors on a hot summer evening could set their bottles of wine in the running water to keep them cool. Picture the candlelight, the conversation, and the steady sound of water at the centre of the table.

Continue down to the Fountain of the Lights. This is a curved set of terraces lined with many small basins, each with a little jet of water rising from it. In the right light, the jets look like rows of tiny candle flames. It is one of the gentlest moments in the garden. The water no longer crashes or cascades here. It simply rises and falls, quietly and evenly, like breathing. The wildness of the grotto above now feels very far away.

Now turn to the two buildings. They look like twins, square and modest, standing on either side of the slope. But they were not built at the same time. The first was built for Cardinal Gambara. The second was completed later for Cardinal Alessandro Peretti Montalto, a nephew of Pope Sixtus the Fifth, who took over the property. Their placement keeps the central axis open, so the water stays the main subject. Inside the Gambara building, frescoes in the loggia show views of the villa and its gardens.

At the bottom of the hill lies the square parterre, with its low hedges cut into precise patterns. At its heart is the Fountain of the Moors. Four bronze youths stand in the centre, holding up a heraldic emblem topped by a star. That star and the little mountains beneath it belong to the Montalto family. So the final, most orderly scene in the garden carries the name of the second cardinal. The journey that began in a rough grotto ends here in perfect geometry.

The villa takes its name from the Lante family, who held the property for a long period from the seventeenth century onward. Today it is cared for by the Italian state. Locals also like to tell a story about Cardinal Gambara and the cost of all this splendour. According to one popular tale, a fellow churchman scolded him for spending so lavishly on a pleasure garden. True or not, the story fits the place. Very few men of the Church built anything so purely for delight.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment wherever you are on the slope. Look up toward the grotto, then down toward the square pool. You can see almost the entire design in one line. Listen again to the water, which still arrives by gravity from the hills, as it did four centuries ago. As you walk back through the gate into the streets of Bagnaia, notice how quickly the sound fades... and how quiet the town feels without it.

Practical tips

Walk the garden from the top down to follow the story.
Arrive early to hear the fountains before groups gather.
Wear shoes with grip for damp stone steps and slopes.
Combine your visit with a walk through Bagnaia's old centre.
Check ahead whether the casino interiors are open that day.
