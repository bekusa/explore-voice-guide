The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look at the ground in front of you. A low ridge runs through the grass or the woodland floor. It is soft at the edges and almost too modest to notice. Birds move in the trees. A tractor works somewhere in a distant field. This quiet line in the Bavarian countryside was once the edge of the Roman Empire.

You are on the Upper Germanic-Rhaetian Limes. Limes is the Latin word for a frontier, a boundary path. This frontier ran for about 550 kilometres. It stretched from the Rhine in the west to the Danube in the east. It was one of the longest continuous structures the Romans ever built in Europe. Today very little of it stands upright. Most of it survives as a bump in the earth, a line of stones, or a crop mark seen from the air.

In 2005, this frontier became a UNESCO World Heritage Site. It forms part of a larger listing called the Frontiers of the Roman Empire. That listing also includes Hadrian's Wall in northern England. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. In length, the German frontier is far longer than its famous British cousin. It is simply much harder to see, because so much of it lies under farmland, forest, and village streets.

The frontier did not appear all at once. It grew in stages from the late first century onward. At first it was a patrolled road with timber watchtowers. In the second century, under the emperor Hadrian, soldiers added a wooden palisade, a fence of sharpened tree trunks. Later the wooden towers were replaced in stone. The line was not a fortress wall built to stop armies. It was closer to a controlled border, a way to watch movement and to collect tolls on trade.

The frontier had two names because it crossed two Roman provinces. Upper Germania lay to the west. Raetia lay to the east, covering much of what is now southern Bavaria. The two sections looked different. In Upper Germania, the barrier became a palisade with a deep ditch and an earth bank behind it. In Raetia, the timber fence was eventually replaced by a stone wall. That Rhaetian wall is the line that runs through this part of Bavaria.

The stone wall was not very tall, and it was not meant to be. It was a statement as much as a defence. Behind it, every few hundred metres, stood a watchtower. Across the whole frontier there were roughly nine hundred of these towers. Soldiers in them could see the next tower along the line. They could pass signals, watch a valley, or warn a fort of trouble. Life in a tower was probably long hours, cold nights, and very little excitement.

The real power sat in the forts behind the line. Bavaria has several well-known ones. At Weißenburg, the Romans called the fort Biriciana. A gate has been reconstructed there, and the remains of a large Roman bathhouse lie under a protective roof. At Pfünz, in the Altmühl valley, another gate has been rebuilt. At Ruffenhofen, a museum called the Limeseum sits beside a fort site that is marked out with plants in the open field.

Here is the second surprise. In 1979, a man digging in his garden in Weißenburg struck metal. He had found a hoard of Roman objects, including bronze statuettes of gods and silver votive pieces. Someone had buried them, probably in a time of danger, and never returned. Today this find is known as the Weißenburg Roman treasure. It is one of the richest Roman discoveries in southern Germany, and it came from an ordinary back garden.

The frontier was a meeting place, not only a barrier. Soldiers came from across the empire. Some served far from home for many years. Merchants, craftspeople, and families settled in villages outside the forts. Germanic peoples on the other side traded goods across controlled crossing points. Coins, pottery, and jewellery moved in both directions. The line separated two worlds, but it also connected them every single day.

The end came in the third century. Pressure from Germanic groups grew, and the empire faced crisis on many fronts. Around 260, Rome gave up this frontier and pulled back to the Rhine, the Danube, and the Iller rivers. Historians call this the fall of the Limes. The towers emptied. The wall was left to the weather. Over the following centuries, local people took its stones for houses, churches, and field walls.

When the Romans left, the stories began. In Bavaria, people called the remains of the stone wall the Devil's Wall. According to one popular tale, the Devil asked God for as much land as he could enclose with a wall in a single night. He failed to finish before dawn, and in his anger he smashed what he had built. Locals said that was why only broken pieces remained. It is folklore, of course, but it shows how strange the wall seemed to later generations.

The eastern end of the frontier lies at Eining, near the Danube. There the Romans had a fort called Abusina, and its stone foundations are still visible today. From there, the river itself became the border. If you follow the line on a map from the Rhine to this point, you trace a long, slightly bending path. Today a cycle route and hiking paths follow much of it, and small information boards mark many tower sites along the way.

As you walk on, try to read the landscape as a Roman soldier might have done. Notice where the ground rises and where you could see far. Notice the edge of the forest, and the open field beyond it. A tower may once have stood on the small mound near you. A path may follow an old patrol road. The frontier is not a single monument. It is a long thread through the countryside, and you can join it at hundreds of points.

So before you leave, look once more at the low line in the earth. Nearly two thousand years ago, someone stood here on watch. They looked north, into land that Rome never fully controlled. Now the same ground carries walkers, cyclists, and cows. The empire is gone. The line is still here, quiet and patient, waiting for the next person to notice it.
