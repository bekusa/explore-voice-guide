The guide

The story

Listen first to the ground around you. In this western edge of Varna, the sound is not birdsong or temple bells. It is traffic, the hum of warehouses, and the low wind that comes in off the lake. Nothing here looks ancient. That is the first surprise. Under this ordinary industrial ground, archaeologists found one of the most remarkable burial sites in the prehistoric world.

The story begins in 1972. An excavator operator named Raycho Marinov was digging a trench for a cable during construction work. His bucket turned up something that glinted. It was gold. Marinov collected the small objects and reported them. Local archaeologists came quickly, and they soon understood that this was not a lost coin purse. It was a cemetery, and it was very, very old.

How old? The graves date to the Copper Age, sometimes called the Chalcolithic. This was the period when people first learned to work metal, before bronze came into use. Radiocarbon dating places these burials in the fifth millennium before Christ, roughly six and a half thousand years ago. To put that in perspective, these people were laid to rest long before the first pyramids rose in Egypt.

Excavation went on for many years, led by archaeologists from the Varna museum. Ivan Ivanov directed much of the early work. In the end, the team recorded around three hundred graves. Together they held thousands of gold objects, with a combined weight of several kilograms. This gold is often described as among the oldest worked gold yet found anywhere on Earth. That one claim is why scholars around the world know the name of Varna.

But the gold is only part of the picture. Most graves were modest. People were buried with pots, flint blades, stone tools, and simple ornaments. A small number of graves, however, held enormous wealth. That contrast matters. It is some of the earliest clear evidence we have of social rank. Here, perhaps for the first time on this scale, some people were treated as far more important than others, even in death.

The most famous burial is known simply as Grave 43. It held a man of middle age, buried on his back. Around him lay more gold than had been found in all the other graves of the period anywhere else. There were bracelets and rings, beads and plaques, and a stone axe with a gold-covered handle that may have been a sceptre of power. Some archaeologists suggest he was a chief, or a priest, or both.

Here is a second surprise. Some of the richest graves contained no body at all. Archaeologists call these symbolic graves, or cenotaphs. A cenotaph is a tomb for someone whose body is not there. A few of them held clay masks, shaped like human faces, with gold pieces placed where the eyes, mouth, and ears would be. Nobody knows for certain who these empty graves honoured. Perhaps people lost at sea, perhaps something more spiritual.

The people who made all this belonged to what is now called the Varna culture. They lived around the lakes and the coast of this part of the Black Sea. They farmed, kept animals, and worked copper from mines further inland. They also traded over long distances. Ornaments made from the shell of the spondylus, a spiny sea mollusc from warmer southern waters, appear in these graves. Those shells travelled a long way to reach Varna.

So why did such wealth gather here, of all places? The honest answer is that scholars still debate it. Varna sat at a meeting point of sea, lake, and land routes. Salt, copper, and gold may all have passed through. Communities that controlled that trade could grow rich. But this is reasoned interpretation, not certainty. Much about these people, including their language and their beliefs, remains unknown.

Now a practical note, and an honest one. If you are standing at the site itself, there is very little to see on the surface. The graves were excavated and the area returned to its modern use. The real treasures were taken to safety. Today they are displayed at the Varna Archaeological Museum in the city centre. If you want to look at the gold with your own eyes, that is where you need to go.

In the museum, the objects sit behind glass under soft, careful light. Many are surprisingly small. Some beads are no bigger than a grain of rice. The gold has a warm, buttery colour, slightly softer than modern jewellery. Look closely at the edges and you can see how the metal was hammered and shaped by hand. Every piece was made without any modern tools at all.

It is worth slowing down in front of those cases. Think about the hands that made these objects. Think about the families who stood at the edge of each grave. They chose what to place beside the people they loved, or the people they feared. The skeletons, the pots, and the gold were all part of a ceremony. We see only what survived, and we do not know the words that were spoken.

As you leave, look again at the flat, everyday landscape of this side of Varna. Roads, fences, the grey shine of the lake in the distance. It is easy to walk past and feel nothing. Yet beneath ground just like this, a buried society waited for thousands of years. Somewhere under your feet, in places like this, there may still be stories no one has found.
