The guide

The story

Listen first. Beyond the trees there is a soft, steady hush of waves on the shore. Closer in, gravel crunches under your shoes, and the air carries a mix of salt and cut grass. Tall trees throw cool shade across the paths. This is Euxinograd, the former summer palace of Bulgaria's royal family. It sits on the Black Sea coast, a short drive north of central Varna.

Start with the name, because it holds a small clue. In ancient times, Greek sailors called the Black Sea the Euxine, which roughly means the hospitable sea. The ending, grad, is a Slavic word for town or city. So Euxinograd means something like the town on the Euxine. It is a name that joins two worlds, the classical Greek past of this coast and the Slavic language of modern Bulgaria.

The palace was not always a royal retreat. Before the royals came, this land belonged to a monastery. The story of the palace begins in the 1880s, only a few years after Bulgaria became an independent principality. Its first ruler was Prince Alexander of Battenberg, a young German-born prince. He took an interest in this quiet stretch of coast, and construction of a residence began here during his short reign.

At first, the estate carried the name Sandrovo, taken from Sandro, a familiar form of Alexander. But Alexander did not stay long on the Bulgarian throne. He gave up power in 1886. His successor, Ferdinand, completed the work and later gave the estate the name you hear today. Here is a detail many visitors miss: the elegant name Euxinograd is younger than the palace itself.

Now look at the building. It does not look Balkan at all. The steep slate roofs, the tall chimneys and the pale walls come from the style of French country châteaux. Think of the palaces of the Loire Valley in France, where noble families built graceful homes surrounded by gardens. Bulgaria's new rulers had close ties with Western Europe. They wanted a summer home that showed those ties clearly, here on the eastern edge of the continent.

This was a deliberate choice. In the late nineteenth century, Bulgaria was a young state building its identity after nearly five centuries of Ottoman rule. A royal palace in the French manner sent a message to visitors and diplomats. It said that this small country belonged to modern Europe. So when you look at these rooflines, you are looking at a political statement made in stone and slate.

Yet for many people, the real treasure of Euxinograd is not the palace. It is the park around it. Over decades, gardeners and landscape designers from abroad shaped this ground into a large botanical garden. They planted trees and shrubs brought from many parts of the world. Some species were new to this coast. Today, broad lawns open between groups of old trees, and paths curve gently toward the water.

Walk slowly here and look up. The trees vary in shape, bark and leaf. Some are tall conifers with dark needles. Others spread wide, leafy crowns. The design follows the idea of the English landscape garden, which aims to look natural rather than geometric. There are few straight lines. Instead, each turn of the path opens a new view, a lawn, a pond, or a glimpse of blue sea between the trunks.

Here is a second surprise. Euxinograd is also known for its wine and brandy. The estate has a long tradition of winemaking, which began in the royal era. Vineyards and cellars were part of the property, and the royal household served its own wines. That tradition continued long after the monarchy ended. Bottles with the Euxinograd name are still produced, and many Bulgarians know the name from the label before they ever see the palace.

The royal chapter of the story closed in the middle of the twentieth century. After the Second World War, Bulgaria became a communist state, and the monarchy was abolished in 1946. The young Tsar Simeon left the country with his family. Euxinograd passed to the state. During the communist decades, it served as a residence for the country's leaders, and ordinary citizens had little access to the grounds.

Today the estate is still a government residence. It hosts official meetings, visiting delegations and state events. This is why access can feel more controlled than at a typical museum. Visits usually happen on organized tours, and some areas may close when official guests are present. That quiet, slightly formal atmosphere is part of the experience. You are walking through a working state property, not only a historic site.

As you move between the palace and the shore, notice how the place changes. Near the building, the space feels ordered and calm. Closer to the sea, the wind grows stronger and the light becomes brighter and wider. In summer, the shade of the old trees offers real relief from the heat. In spring and autumn, the colours of the park shift from fresh green to gold and rust.

Before you leave, stop for a moment on one of the paths. Look back at the steep roofs rising above the trees. Then turn toward the water and listen again to the waves. Princes and tsars once walked these same paths to escape the city and the weight of politics. Now the same breeze moves through the branches above you, and the same sea stays restless beyond the lawn.
