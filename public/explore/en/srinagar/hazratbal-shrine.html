The guide

The story

Listen first. Wings beat overhead as a flock of pigeons lifts from the courtyard and wheels out over the water. The marble under your bare feet is cool, even when the sun is bright. Somewhere nearby, a voice rises in prayer and then falls quiet. In front of you, a white dome sits against the mountains, and behind you the long, flat shine of Dal Lake runs toward the hills. This is Hazratbal, on the northern shore of the lake.

The name itself tells you what this place means to Kashmir. Hazrat is a title of deep respect, used for revered figures in Islam. Bal, in the Kashmiri language, means a place, often a place beside water. So the name means something close to the place of the revered one. Locals also call it Dargah Sharif, the noble shrine. For many Kashmiri Muslims, this is the most sacred site in the valley.

The reason is a single relic. Inside the shrine is the Moi-e-Muqaddas, which means the sacred hair. Believers hold that it is a hair from the beard of the Prophet Muhammad. According to the traditional account, it came to Kashmir in the seventeenth century. It passed from a family of descendants of the Prophet to a Kashmiri merchant, and then into the care of this shrine. It has remained here, and at the centre of local devotion, ever since.

The relic is not on permanent display. It is shown to the public only on special days in the Islamic calendar, such as the Prophet's birthday and certain other holy dates. On those days, the scene here changes completely. Crowds fill the courtyard, the lawns, and the lakeside paths. People travel from villages across the valley. When the relic is raised before them, the sound of the crowd... is something visitors remember for a long time.

Now look again at the building itself, and notice something unusual. Most old mosques in Kashmir do not have domes at all. They have layered, sloping roofs of wood, often topped with a spire, closer in shape to a pagoda than to a Middle Eastern mosque. Hazratbal is different. Its white marble dome is often described as the only domed mosque in Kashmir. That is the first surprise here: the building looks familiar to outsiders, and unusual to locals.

The white marble shrine you see today is modern. It was built in the second half of the twentieth century, replacing an older and simpler structure on the same spot. Construction began in the late 1960s and finished in the late 1970s. The project was closely tied to Sheikh Abdullah, the most important Kashmiri political leader of his era. The design mixes Mughal ideas with Kashmiri tradition. You can see this in the arched windows, the single tall minaret, and the clean, symmetrical front.

The story of this site is older than the marble, though. In the seventeenth century, during the rule of the Mughal emperors, this lakeshore held a garden and a pleasure house. The Mughals loved Kashmir. They came here to escape the heat of the plains, and they built terraced gardens along the lake. Historical sources say that the building here was later turned into a place of prayer. So the shrine stands on ground that was first shaped for royal leisure, and then for faith.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a dramatic one. In the last days of December 1963, the sacred hair disappeared from the shrine. The news spread fast. Huge numbers of people came out into the freezing streets of Srinagar, and protests spread across Kashmir and beyond. For several days, the region was in turmoil. Then, in early January 1964, the relic was recovered. After religious leaders examined it, they confirmed it was genuine. The episode is still remembered as a turning point in Kashmiri politics.

That history helps explain the care you will see around you. Visitors remove their shoes before entering the marble areas. People speak softly. Some sit for a long time with their backs against the walls, saying nothing. Others touch the doorframe as they pass. You do not need to share the faith to feel the weight of attention here. The best approach is simple: move slowly, watch what others do, and follow their lead.

Step back toward the edge of the courtyard and look across the water. Dal Lake is a working lake, not just a view. Small wooden boats called shikaras move across it, paddled by a single person with a heart-shaped oar. Some carry tourists. Others carry vegetables, flowers, or fishing nets. From the shrine, you can see the lake's surface broken by floating gardens and reed beds, with the Zabarwan mountains rising beyond.

The lanes around the shrine have their own life. On ordinary days, small stalls sell prayer caps, rosaries, dried fruit, and simple snacks. On Fridays, after the midday prayer, the whole area fills with families and the smell of food cooking over open flames. Many people come here not only to pray, but also to sit on the lawns, talk, and look at the lake. This mix of devotion and ordinary daily life is part of the character of the place.

Light changes the shrine through the day. In the early morning, mist often lies on the lake, and the dome looks soft and grey. By midday, the marble turns a hard, bright white that can be difficult to look at directly. In the late afternoon, the stone takes on a warm, pale gold, and the reflection of the minaret stretches out on the water. In winter, snow on the mountains makes the white of the shrine look almost blue.

Before you leave, take one more slow look. Notice the pigeons settling back onto the courtyard floor. Notice the boatmen waiting at the water's edge, and the line of the mountains above them. Feel the cool marble under your feet one last time. For centuries, people have come to this shore carrying hope, grief, and gratitude. As you walk back toward the road, you are walking the same path they took... and the dome will stay in view behind you for a long way.
