The guide

The story

Listen for the small sounds first. Gravel crunches under your shoes. Water moves quietly across a long reflecting pool. Somewhere nearby, a sprinkler ticks across a lawn kept very green in dry Southern California light. Then you see a modest white wooden farmhouse on the edge of the grounds. It looks almost out of place beside the polished museum. The whole story here starts in that small house.

First, a note on where you are. The library sits in Yorba Linda, a quiet city in Orange County a short drive northeast of Anaheim. Many visitors pair the two, which makes sense. But this land has its own story. Here, in the early twentieth century, families planted citrus groves across the hills. The scent of orange and lemon blossom was once the smell of this whole valley.

Richard Nixon was born in that white farmhouse on January 9, 1913. His father, Frank, built the house himself from a kit of pre-cut materials. That was a common way to build a family home in rural America at the time. Frank tried to make a living from a lemon grove on the surrounding land. The grove failed, and in 1922 the family moved to nearby Whittier. The boy who left this house would later become the thirty-seventh President of the United States.

Walk up to the birthplace and look closely. It is small, with plain wood, simple windows, and a narrow porch. The rooms inside are close together, the scale of a working family with little money. This is the first surprise for many visitors. The museum does not open with power and politics. It opens with a kitchen, a piano, and a bedroom. The contrast between this house and the White House is the quiet argument of the whole site.

The library itself opened in July 1990. Nixon attended the dedication himself, and so did Presidents Ford, Reagan, and the first President Bush. For its first years, a private foundation ran the site. In 2007 it joined the presidential library system of the National Archives, the federal agency that cares for official government records. That change matters. It meant historians and archivists, not only admirers, would shape how the story is told.

Inside, the galleries move through a long and complicated career. You will meet the young Navy officer of the Second World War. You will see the congressman and senator, then the vice president under Dwight Eisenhower. Nixon lost the presidential race of 1960 to John Kennedy. Two years later he lost a race for governor of California. Many people thought his career was over. In 1968, he won the presidency.

The foreign policy galleries draw many visitors. In 1972, Nixon travelled to China. It was a remarkable moment. For more than twenty years, the United States and the People's Republic had no formal relations. The visit opened a door that changed the shape of the Cold War, the long rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union. That same year, Nixon also went to Moscow and signed arms agreements with Soviet leaders.

It is easy to forget how much domestic history passes through these rooms too. The Environmental Protection Agency was created during his administration. The first landing of people on the Moon happened in his first year in office, in July 1969. Nixon spoke by telephone with the astronauts on the lunar surface. Moments like these show that his presidency was larger than its ending.

And then there is the ending. The museum includes a gallery on Watergate, the political scandal that began with a break-in at Democratic Party offices in Washington in 1972. The cover-up that followed led toward impeachment. In August 1974, Nixon resigned. He is still the only American president to leave office that way. After the National Archives took over, this gallery was rebuilt to give a fuller and more critical account. Give it time. It is one of the most serious rooms here.

Now go outside to see the large green-and-white helicopter. This is the aircraft that carried Nixon away from the White House lawn on the day he resigned. Many people know the photograph. He stands at the top of the steps, arms raised, fingers in a V. Here, you can stand next to the real machine. You can walk up and look inside. It is a strange feeling to be this close to an object from that morning.

The museum galleries were redesigned and reopened in 2016 with more interactive displays. One space is a full-scale copy of the East Room of the White House, a formal hall with chandeliers and pale walls. It is often used for public events and lectures. Stand in the middle and look up. You get a real sense of the formal space where presidents host state dinners and sign laws.

Find the gardens near the birthplace before you leave. Richard Nixon and his wife, Pat, are buried here, a short walk from the house where he was born. Pat Nixon died in 1993. Her husband died the following year, in April 1994. His funeral took place here on these grounds. Five living presidents attended, including President Bill Clinton, who spoke at the service. The graves are simple, and flowers grow nearby.

Pat Nixon deserves her own moment. She grew up in modest circumstances not far from here, in Southern California. As First Lady, she travelled widely and opened more of the White House to the public. She also encouraged volunteer work across the country. Visitors sometimes walk past her story quickly. If you have time, look for the displays about her life, her clothing, and her travels. She shaped this presidency more than many people remember.

Before you go, stand once more by the reflecting pool and look toward the little white farmhouse. Notice the distance between the two buildings — only a few minutes on foot. One life covered that distance, with great achievements and a deep fall. The grounds do not tell you what to think. They put the beginning and the end side by side, under the same wide Orange County sky. Take that quiet with you as you walk back to the gate.
