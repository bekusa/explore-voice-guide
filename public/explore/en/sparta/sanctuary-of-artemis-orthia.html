The guide

The story

Listen first. A few cars pass on a road nearby. Reeds move in the low ground towards the river. Under your feet the earth is soft and grey, and the grass grows long between old stones. This quiet corner of Sparta does not look like a famous place. Yet for more than a thousand years, people came here to watch something that shocked visitors from across the ancient world.

You are standing on the west bank of the Eurotas, the river that runs through the Spartan plain. In ancient times this low, damp district was called Limnai, which means the marshes. The name fits. Even today the ground here feels wetter and greener than the hills around it. The Spartans chose this place for a goddess they called Artemis Orthia. The word Orthia probably means upright, or standing straight, although scholars still debate it.

Artemis was the Greek goddess of the wild, of hunting, and of the passage from childhood into adult life. At Sparta she seems to have joined an older local goddess, Orthia, who was worshipped here before the two names merged. That passage into adulthood is the key to this sanctuary. In Sparta, boys left their families young and entered a hard state education. Many of its most important rituals took place on the ground in front of you.

Now look at what remains. You can see the foundations of a temple, the base of a long altar, and curved rows of stone seating around them. The pieces come from very different centuries. The first shrine here was very simple, probably just an altar in an open space. A stone temple came later, in the archaic period, and it was rebuilt more than once. The curved seating is much later again. It belongs to the Romans.

Here is the first surprise. Early in the twentieth century, British archaeologists dug this site, in excavations that ran from 1906 to 1910. Deep in the soil they found a thick layer of clean river sand. Someone in antiquity had spread it across the whole sanctuary, perhaps during a major rebuilding. For the excavators, that sand became a clock. Everything below it was older, everything above it was younger. It helped archaeologists date Spartan objects in a way that few sites in Greece allowed.

And the objects were extraordinary in number. The excavators recovered tens of thousands of tiny lead figurines, many no bigger than a thumb. They show warriors, winged goddesses, animals, wreaths, and musicians. These were cheap offerings, cast in simple moulds. Ordinary people could afford them. Picture the crowds who once pressed into this space, each one leaving a small grey figure for the goddess. Their gifts lay in the mud of the marsh for many centuries.

The second surprise is stranger. The diggers also found many clay masks, painted and full of expression. Some show calm young faces. Others show old, wrinkled, grotesque faces with open mouths and deep lines. Most were too small to wear, so they were probably copies of real masks, given as offerings. They suggest that dance, drama, and perhaps mockery played a part in the worship here. Sparta, so often imagined as silent and stern, also had a theatrical side.

Now to the ritual that made this place known. The Greek writer Xenophon describes a contest in which boys tried to steal cheeses from the altar of Orthia. Older youths stood guard with whips. The boys had to grab the cheese and take the blows. It was a test of cunning, speed, and endurance, all qualities that Sparta valued in its future soldiers.

Over time the game changed into something harsher. In the Roman period, the ritual became an endurance trial. Young Spartans were whipped at the altar, and the winner was the one who held out longest without crying out. Ancient writers report that some boys died rather than give in. Cicero and Plutarch both wrote about it, and Plutarch said he had seen it himself. This was no longer a quiet local rite. It had become a spectacle.

That is why those Roman seats are here. Sometime in the third century, the Romans built a small amphitheatre around the temple and the altar. Visitors came from across the empire to watch the whipping, much as tourists today come to watch a festival. Stand near the curve of the seating and look towards the altar. You are seeing the view that those Roman spectators paid to see. The old Spartan ritual had turned into a show about the Spartan past.

The sanctuary also had its legends. According to the travel writer Pausanias, the ancient wooden statue of the goddess was the one that Orestes and Iphigenia carried away from the land of the Taurians, on the Black Sea. Locals told another tale. They said that a quarrel once broke out at a sacrifice here, that blood was spilled at the altar, and that the goddess then demanded human blood. In this story, the lawgiver Lycurgus replaced human sacrifice with the whipping of boys. These are myths, not history, but Spartans believed them.

The boys who won contests here sometimes left a lasting mark. Archaeologists found stone slabs with inscriptions from victors in singing and other competitions. Some slabs had small hollows, where iron sickles were once fixed as prizes and offerings. The sickle was a humble farm tool. Here it became a sign of honour. Many of these finds are now in museums, so the site itself feels bare. That bareness is honest. It asks you to imagine the rest.

Take a moment before you leave. Look past the stones to the line of trees along the Eurotas, and beyond them to the mountains that close the Spartan valley. The same slopes looked down on the shepherds who left lead figures, the boys who ran for the cheese, and the Roman crowds on these seats. As you walk away, notice the softness of the ground again, and the smell of wet grass. The marsh is still here. So is the goddess's quiet corner of the plain.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, overgrown ground around the ruins.
Bring water and sun protection in summer; shade is limited.
Come early in the day to avoid heat and glare.
See the excavated finds in Sparta's archaeological museum afterwards.
Combine your visit with a walk along the Eurotas river.
