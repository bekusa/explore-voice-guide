The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here, the loudest sound is often the wind moving through cypress trees and wild fig. Under your feet, the path is a mix of worn stone and loose gravel, polished smooth by centuries of sandals and boots. Below you, the plain of Laconia stretches out in a patchwork of olive groves and orange trees. Behind you, the slopes of the Taygetos mountains rise steeply. And all around you stand the brick and stone shells of a city that was once one of the last bright centres of the Byzantine world.

Welcome to Mystras. This hillside lies only a few kilometres west of modern Sparta, but its story is very different from the Sparta of warriors and Thermopylae. Mystras is medieval. It is a city of churches, palaces, monasteries and narrow lanes, all stacked on a steep cone of rock. Today it is an archaeological site and a UNESCO World Heritage Site. For about two centuries, though, it was a capital. Many historians argue that some of the most important ideas of the late Middle Ages took shape right here.

The story begins with a castle — and, surprisingly, not a Greek one. In the middle of the thirteenth century, a French knight named William of Villehardouin ruled much of southern Greece. His family had arrived with the crusaders who seized Constantinople in 1204. Around 1249, William built a fortress on the summit above you. He wanted to control the plain below and the restless mountain people of the Taygetos. You can still see the castle walls on the very top of the hill.

William did not keep his castle for long. In 1259 he was defeated and captured by the Byzantine emperor's forces at a battle in northern Greece. To win his freedom, he had to give up several fortresses, and Mystras was one of them. So, only a few years after a crusader built it, the castle passed into Byzantine hands. Greek-speaking families began to settle on the slopes beneath the walls. They wanted protection, and the old settlement of Sparta in the plain felt too exposed.

From there, the town grew downhill, layer by layer. In the fourteenth century, Mystras became the seat of the Despotate of the Morea. A despot was a senior prince, usually a close relative of the emperor. The Morea was the medieval name for the Peloponnese, the large peninsula of southern Greece. While the rest of the Byzantine Empire was shrinking under pressure from the Ottoman Turks, this corner of Greece became surprisingly prosperous and confident.

As you walk, notice how the city is arranged in tiers. Near the top is the castle. Below it is the upper town, with the Palace of the Despots. Further down is the lower town, with most of the great churches and monasteries. The streets are not laid out on a grid. They follow the shape of the mountain, twisting and doubling back. Gates and arches divide one zone from another. Even today, getting from one church to the next can feel like a small climb.

The Palace of the Despots is one of the rare surviving examples of a Byzantine palace anywhere. Its long main wing has rows of windows and a great hall where rulers once received guests and held court. For a long time the building stood as a ruin. After a major restoration in recent years, its walls, roof and interior spaces are once again easier to read. Stand in front of the façade and imagine the envoys, priests and merchants who once waited here.

Now to the churches, the real treasure of Mystras. The oldest major church is the Metropolis, dedicated to Saint Demetrios. It was the cathedral of the town. Inside, look down at the floor. Set into the paving is a marble slab carved with a double-headed eagle, a symbol of the Byzantine imperial family. According to a popular tradition, this slab marks the place where Constantine Palaiologos became emperor in 1449. He would be the last emperor of Byzantium.

That detail surprises many visitors. The last Byzantine emperor began his reign not in Constantinople, but here, on this Peloponnesian hillside. Only four years later, in 1453, Constantinople fell to the Ottomans, and Constantine died defending its walls. Mystras itself held out a little longer. It surrendered to the Ottoman sultan in 1460. The courtyard of the Metropolis also holds a small museum, with carved stone, jewellery and everyday objects from the medieval town.

Mystras was not only a political centre. It was also a place of learning. In the early fifteenth century, a philosopher named George Gemistos Plethon lived and taught here. He studied the ancient Greek thinker Plato with real passion. When he travelled to Italy for a church council in the late 1430s, his lectures on Plato excited Italian scholars. Some historians see this as one small spark for the Italian Renaissance. Think about that as you look across these quiet ruins.

The painted walls of the churches show the same creative energy. In the Peribleptos monastery, built partly into the rock, the frescoes are delicate and full of movement. Faces turn, robes flow, and small scenes are full of human gesture. Further along, the Hodegetria church, also called the Aphentiko, and the Pantanassa monastery show how Byzantine builders mixed domes, arches, brick patterns and tall bell towers. Many of the walls carry colours that are still deep blue, red and gold.

Here is a second surprise. Mystras is not entirely empty. The Pantanassa is still a working convent, and a small community of nuns lives there. Their gardens, flowers and quiet routines bring the past into the present. After the Ottoman conquest, the town continued for centuries, with Venetian rule for a short period too. It was badly damaged in fighting in the late eighteenth century. In the nineteenth century, a new Sparta was built on the plain, and people slowly moved away.

Before you leave, find a spot with a view of the valley. Look at the red tiles, the broken arches, the green slopes and the plain beyond. Listen again to the wind, the bells of goats, perhaps a distant car on the road to Sparta. This was once a capital where emperors, monks and philosophers walked. Now it is mostly silence and stone. As you walk down, keep an eye on the small details — a carved doorway, a patch of old plaster, a painted face looking back at you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes with grip for steep, uneven stone paths.
Start at the upper entrance and walk downhill.
Carry plenty of water, especially on summer afternoons.
Cover shoulders and knees before entering the Pantanassa convent.
Come early in the day to avoid heat and tour groups.
