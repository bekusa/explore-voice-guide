The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Out here, south of Sparta, the loudest sound is often the wind moving through the olive trees. Beneath your feet the ground is dry and stony, scattered with fragments of old foundation. To the west, the long wall of Mount Taygetos rises over the valley, often streaked with snow in winter. To the east, the Eurotas River runs through its green belt of reeds. You are on a low hill. For more than a thousand years, people climbed it to honour a god and to mourn a boy.

This is Amyclae, the sanctuary the ancient Greeks called the Amyklaion. It stands on a rise known today as Agia Kyriaki, named after the small white chapel at the top. The hill lies a few kilometres south of the centre of modern Sparta. That distance mattered. In classical times, Amyclae was counted as one of the villages that made up Sparta itself. It sat a little apart, but it belonged to the Spartans completely. Some of their most important religious life took place on this hill.

The story of this place begins with a myth. Hyacinthus was a beautiful young prince, and the god Apollo loved him. One day the two of them threw the discus together. Apollo's throw went wrong, and the heavy disc struck Hyacinthus and killed him. In some versions, the jealous wind god turned the disc in the air on purpose. From the boy's blood, the myth says, a flower grew. The Greeks named that flower after him, and the word still lives on in English as hyacinth.

Ancient writers said that Hyacinthus was buried here, on this hill. That detail shaped everything that came after. The sanctuary honoured Apollo, but it also held the grave of a young man who died too early. Joy and grief shared the same ground. It is unusual to find a Greek sanctuary where a god and a mortal hero are bound so closely together. Many visitors arrive expecting a simple temple of Apollo. In fact, they are standing at a place of mourning as well.

Archaeology shows that this hill was important long before classical Sparta. Excavators have found material here from the Mycenaean age, the Bronze Age world that later Greeks remembered through the stories of Homer. Small clay figurines and pottery suggest that people made offerings on this summit in that very early period. Later generations kept returning. So the worship you are imagining here did not start with Sparta. Sparta arrived, found an old holy place, and made it its own.

The great event of the year was a festival called the Hyakinthia. It took place in early summer and lasted several days. According to ancient accounts, the first day was solemn. People held back from feasting and singing, out of respect for the dead youth. Then the mood changed. The following days brought music, processions, choirs of young people, and shared meals. The festival moved from grief to celebration, a little like the seasons moving from drought to harvest.

Here is something many visitors do not know. The Spartans took this festival extremely seriously. Ancient writers say that Spartan men made great efforts to come home for the Hyakinthia, even during military campaigns. For a city famous above all for its soldiers, that tells you a lot. This hill was not a side attraction to Spartan life. It was close to the centre of how Spartans saw themselves, their gods, and their place in the valley.

Now picture what once stood in front of you. The travel writer Pausanias visited in the second century after Christ and described the sanctuary in detail. He wrote about a colossal bronze statue of Apollo. It was very old even in his day, and very simple in form. He said it looked almost like a bronze column, with a face, hands and feet added. The god held a spear and a bow and wore a helmet. It must have been strange and powerful to see.

Around this statue stood a huge structure that Pausanias called the Throne of Apollo. He named its designer as Bathycles of Magnesia, a craftsman from the Greek cities of Asia Minor, the region that is now western Turkey. The throne was covered with carved scenes from myth. Pausanias listed many of them, one after another. Beneath the statue, he said, was an altar shaped like a tomb. People believed Hyacinthus lay inside it. Offerings to the hero passed through a bronze door into the altar before the sacrifice to Apollo.

Pausanias also recorded a quieter ritual. Each year, he wrote, the women of Sparta wove a new garment, a tunic, for the statue of Apollo. They worked in a special building set aside for the task. It is easy to overlook this detail beside the giant throne and the bronze god. Yet it shows ordinary hands and patient work behind the festival. Every year, someone sat at a loom and made clothing for a god on this hill.

Almost all of that splendour is gone. The bronze statue disappeared long ago, and the throne survives only in scattered stone pieces and foundations. Archaeologists began digging here in the late nineteenth century. More excavations followed in the early twentieth century, and modern research teams have returned in recent decades. Their work has traced foundations, recovered architectural fragments, and found many small offerings. Some carved pieces are kept in museums. What remains on the hill is mostly the outline of the sanctuary, not its full shape.

There is also an old Greek saying linked to this place. According to one popular tale, the people of ancient Amyclae heard false alarms about approaching enemies so often that they banned anyone from speaking of them. Then the enemy really came, and nobody gave a warning. The town fell in silence. This is a legend, not a recorded event. But the saying, that Amyclae was destroyed by its silence, lived on for centuries as a warning about ignoring danger.

Today, the chapel of Agia Kyriaki keeps the hilltop in religious use. This is a pattern you see across Greece. A Christian church rises on the same high ground where older gods were honoured. The chapel is small and modest, with whitewashed walls against the blue sky. Around it, the ancient stones lie low among dry grass and wildflowers. Take a moment to walk the edges of the site. From different points, the views open toward the mountains and down toward the valley farms.

Before you leave, look once more across the Eurotas valley. The same mountain stood behind every procession that climbed this slope. The same river watered the fields below. The bronze god is gone, and so is the throne. But the hill still faces the same horizon. As you walk back down, notice the olive branches, the warm stone, the quiet. Somewhere in the grass, perhaps, a wild hyacinth is waiting for spring.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid strong midday heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for the stony, uneven hilltop ground.
Bring water and sun protection, as shade is limited.
Pair the visit with Sparta's archaeological museum for carved fragments.
Respect the chapel as an active place of worship.
