The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The wind moves across the open desert, dry and steady, and then it seems to drop away into the ground in front of you. A dark, round mouth opens in the rock. The air near the edge feels different, cooler, heavier. You cannot see the bottom. Your eyes search for a floor, a reflection, anything, and find only shadow.

This is the Well of Barhout, a vast natural pit in the desert of Al Mahrah, in far eastern Yemen. It is not a well in the usual sense. Nobody dug it, and nobody draws water here. It is a sinkhole, a shaft that formed naturally in the limestone. Over a very long time, water slowly dissolved the rock below and left a hollow that eventually opened to the sky. The word well is simply the oldest name people gave it.

The opening is wide, roughly thirty metres across according to the explorers who measured it. That is about the length of a few buses parked end to end. The walls drop almost straight down. Look at the rim and you will see pale, layered stone, broken in places, with darker stains where water and time have marked it. The ground around the edge is loose. Keep a generous distance, and let your eyes do the travelling instead of your feet.

For centuries, local people kept their distance too, but for a different reason. Many in the region know this pit as the Well of Hell. Legend has it that Barhout is a prison for jinn, the spirits of Arabian and Islamic belief. Jinn are beings made of smokeless fire, neither human nor angel, and able to do good or harm. Locals say that anything thrown into the pit disappears, and that anyone who goes too close may be pulled inside.

There is an older layer to the story. Traditional Islamic sayings mention a place called Barhut, a valley in Hadramawt with the worst water on earth. In some traditions, it is where the souls of unbelievers gather. These are religious and folk teachings, not geography, and scholars have long debated what they meant. But the name stayed with this dark shaft. For many people in the region, Barhout became a place you spoke about quietly, and rarely visited.

The stories also carry a physical detail. Some visitors have reported an unpleasant smell rising from the depths. Folklore explains it as the breath of spirits. A simpler explanation sits at the bottom of the pit. Animals that fall in cannot climb out again, and they decay in the still air below. In a closed space with little wind, the smell rises slowly toward the surface.

Here is the first surprise. Until recently, almost nobody had been to the bottom. For generations, the depth of Barhout was a matter of guesswork and rumour. Some said it had no floor at all. That changed in 2021, when a team of cave explorers from neighbouring Oman decided to go down. They lowered themselves on ropes into the darkness, carrying lights, cameras, and safety equipment, while others waited at the rim.

What they found was not a gateway to anywhere. It was a cave. The team reported that the shaft runs more than one hundred metres deep, deeper than a thirty-storey building is tall. At the bottom, they found snakes, the remains of dead animals and birds, and pools of water fed by water seeping down the walls. No jinn. No endless fall. Just the quiet, slow work of geology and the animals that had wandered too close.

The second surprise is more beautiful. On the walls and floor, the explorers found cave pearls. These are small, smooth, rounded stones that form in caves when dripping water deposits thin layers of mineral around a tiny grain, again and again. They grow very slowly, in still pools, out of sight. So in the place that legend called the mouth of hell, the explorers found something closer to treasure. Their photographs showed green-tinged water and pale, rounded stone.

The descent made news across the Arab world and beyond. Some people felt relief. Others felt that the explorers had disturbed something better left alone. Both reactions say something real about this place. A pit like Barhout sits in two worlds at once. One is the world of rock, water, and measured depth. The other is the world of stories told by grandparents, passed down through generations who never needed to see the bottom to respect it.

Lift your eyes now from the shaft to the land around it. Al Mahrah is one of the most remote corners of the Arabian Peninsula. It sits close to the border with Oman, far from Yemen's large cities. The landscape is wide and sparse, with low ridges, gravel plains, and a sky that feels very large. In this kind of space, a sudden hole in the ground is impossible to ignore. It is no surprise that people built stories around it.

Notice the light as it falls into the opening. When the sun is high, it reaches part of the way down the walls, then stops, and the lower shaft stays in deep shadow. When the sun is low, the rim glows and the inside turns almost black. Birds sometimes circle above, and a few nest in the rock face. Their calls echo slightly, which can make the pit feel even deeper than it is. Sound here behaves strangely, so take a moment to listen.

Before you leave, look once more at that dark circle in the ground. You now know what lies at the bottom: water, stone, snakes, and small pearls growing slowly in the dark. And yet the pit still feels like a place of stories. Both things can be true. As you walk away across the open ground, notice how quickly the opening disappears behind you. A few steps, and the desert looks whole again, as if nothing were there at all.
