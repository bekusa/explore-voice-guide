The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through tall grass, and somewhere a crow calls across the open field. Under your feet the ground is soft and slightly uneven. In front of you, thatched roofs rise from the earth like low hills, and a frame of huge wooden pillars stands against the sky. This is Sannai-Maruyama, on the southwestern edge of Aomori. People lived on this exact patch of ground for well over a thousand years, long before anyone wrote anything down.

The people who lived here belonged to the Jomon culture. Jomon means "cord-marked", after the patterns they pressed into their pottery with twisted cords. The Jomon were hunters, gatherers and fishers. They lived across the Japanese islands for many thousands of years. This village was occupied from roughly 5,900 to 4,200 years ago. That makes it older than the great pyramids of Egypt. It is also one of the largest Jomon settlements ever found in Japan.

Here is the first surprise. This place was almost lost to a baseball stadium. In the early 1990s, Aomori Prefecture planned to build a sports ground on this land. Archaeologists began a routine survey before construction. What they found was far bigger than anyone expected... houses, graves, storage pits, and enormous wooden posts. In 1994 the prefecture cancelled the stadium and decided to preserve the site. Today it is protected as a Special Historic Site of Japan.

Look now at the tallest structure, the frame of six great pillars. The posts stand in two rows of three, evenly spaced. Archaeologists found six large holes in the ground here. At the bottom of the holes were the remains of chestnut wood posts, each roughly a metre across. The tower you see is a modern reconstruction. Nobody knows how tall the original stood, or whether it had a roof. It may have been a watchtower, a ritual platform, or a landmark.

That uncertainty is worth holding on to. Many guides will tell you one confident story about this tower. The honest answer is simpler. The spacing between the holes is very regular, which suggests the builders used a shared unit of measurement. Raising posts of that size required many people working together. So whatever the tower was for, it shows a community that could plan, organise and share labour on a large scale.

Next, find the long building with the thatched roof. This is a reconstruction of a large pit dwelling, much bigger than an ordinary house. Its floor sits slightly below ground level, as Jomon floors usually did. Step inside if it is open. The air is cooler, and the light comes in low through the doorway. Notice the smell of dry grass and wood. Some researchers think a space this big was used for gatherings, shared work, or winter shelter.

Around the large house, smaller pit dwellings dot the field. These were family homes. Each one had a shallow pit, a hearth inside, and a roof of wood and plant material. Archaeologists have found the traces of hundreds of dwellings across the site. Not all of them stood at the same time, of course. The village grew, shrank and was rebuilt again and again over many generations, like layers of one long family story.

Here is the second surprise. These people were not simply wandering hunters. Studies of plant remains suggest that the villagers managed chestnut trees around the settlement. They may have tended them deliberately, almost like an orchard. Chestnuts gave them food they could store through the long northern winter. They also gathered walnuts and other nuts, caught fish, and hunted small animals. This steady food supply helps explain how such a large village could last so long.

The villagers also traded over remarkable distances. Archaeologists found jade that came from the Itoigawa area, far to the southwest in Niigata. They found obsidian, a black volcanic glass used for sharp blades, from Hokkaido across the sea to the north. They found amber from elsewhere in northern Japan. So this was not an isolated village at the end of the world. It was connected to a wide network of people, boats and paths.

Walk toward the burial areas and the mood changes. Adult graves were dug in lines along a path that led out of the village. Young children were often buried differently, placed inside pottery jars near the homes. It is a quiet, moving detail. It suggests that parents wanted their youngest close by, even after death. Please walk gently here. The reconstructed markers are modern, but the ground beneath them held real people.

Before you leave, visit the exhibition hall, called the Jomon Jiyukan. Inside you can see objects found right here. Look for the tall, cylinder-shaped pots with deep cord patterns on their surface. Look for the flat clay figurines, called dogu, with simple faces and outstretched arms. There are also traces of lacquer work and woven baskets. Some of these pieces are thousands of years old, yet the fingerprints of their makers still feel close.

In 2021, Sannai-Maruyama became part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing called the Jomon Prehistoric Sites in Northern Japan. The listing groups together many sites across Hokkaido and northern Honshu. Together they show how people lived settled lives here for thousands of years without farming rice. For Aomori, this field is a source of real pride. Local schoolchildren come here on trips, and volunteers often help explain the site to visitors.

Now stand still once more and look around you. The pillars, the thatched roofs, the long grass. Beyond the field, you may see modern roads, trees and the outline of the city. The Jomon people watched the same seasons change over these same hills. As you walk back toward the entrance, notice the soft ground under your shoes. Thousands of years of footsteps have crossed it before yours... and the village is still here, quietly waiting to be understood.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the exhibition hall to see real excavated objects.
Wear comfortable shoes for the soft, uneven grass paths.
Bring a rain layer; the open field has little shelter.
Combine your visit with the nearby Aomori Museum of Art.
Ask a volunteer guide for on-site stories if available.
