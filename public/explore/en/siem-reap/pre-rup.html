The guide

The story

Put your hand on the nearest wall. The stone feels rough and full of small holes, almost like a dry sponge. It is warm from the sun and has a deep rust colour. That colour is the first thing to notice here. Pre Rup is built mostly from laterite and brick. In the late afternoon, the whole temple turns from orange to a deep red, like embers in a fire that is slowly going out.

You are standing at a royal temple from the tenth century. That makes it about two hundred years older than Angkor Wat. The king who built it was Rajendravarman the Second. He is remembered for bringing the royal capital back to the Angkor region after a period when it had moved north to a place called Koh Ker. Pre Rup was one of the great projects of his reign, and it was dedicated to the Hindu god Shiva.

Before you climb, look at the shape of the whole building. It is a temple mountain. This was a central idea in Khmer religious architecture. The temple is a model of Mount Meru, the sacred mountain at the centre of the universe in Hindu and Buddhist belief. Gods were believed to live on its summit. So the builders raised the temple in stacked terraces, each smaller than the one below, rising toward five towers at the top.

Those five towers stand in a pattern called a quincunx. That is one tower at the centre and one at each corner, like the five dots on a dice. The central tower is the tallest and holds the most sacred space. From a distance, the towers look like a cluster of peaks. This same pattern appears again, much later and much larger, at Angkor Wat. Here you can see an earlier version of that idea.

Now, the name. Pre Rup is usually translated as "turn the body." The name comes from a local belief that this temple was used for royal funerals. People said the body was turned, or its outline traced, during cremation rites. In the eastern courtyard there is a large stone basin, and locals have long linked it to these ceremonies. But treat this as tradition, not proven history. Many scholars think the basin had a different purpose, perhaps as a base for a statue.

That is the first surprise many visitors miss. The name of a place does not always tell you what the place was for. Khmer temples were often given new names centuries after they were built, by people who lived nearby and told their own stories about the ruins. The original name of this temple was probably quite different. The local name stayed because the story was memorable. So when you look at that stone basin, you are looking at a real object with a story attached to it.

As you walk up through the levels, notice the change in materials. The terraces and outer walls are laterite, that porous red stone. The towers are brick. The doorframes, the false doors, and the carved lintels above the doorways are sandstone, which is finer and easier to carve. The builders used each material for a different job. Laterite gave mass and strength. Brick allowed tall towers. Sandstone allowed fine detail.

Here is the second surprise. The brick towers were not always bare. Look closely at the surface of the towers and you may find small patches of pale, crusty material. This is the remains of stucco, a kind of plaster made with lime. Once, it covered the brick and was shaped into decoration — figures, flowers, and patterns. It may also have been painted. The red temple you see today was probably much lighter and more decorated when it was new.

The stairs are steep. They rise straight up each side of the pyramid, and the steps are narrow. Take them slowly, and use your hands if you need to. Stone lions sit beside the stairways as guardians. Many have lost their faces or paws to weather and time. On the upper level, the sandstone false doors on the towers carry carved figures of guardian deities. Stop at each corner and look closely. The carving is shallow but clear in good light.

Look out toward the north from the upper terrace. Not far away is another temple from the same king, called East Mebon. It once stood on an island in the middle of a huge rectangular reservoir called the East Baray. Today the reservoir is dry land, fields and trees. But when these temples were active, there was a great body of water nearby. The two temples share many features, including the five towers, the brick, and the laterite.

Pre Rup is a popular place to watch the sunset. The upper terrace looks west over a flat green landscape of rice fields, palms, and forest. As the sun drops, the light hits the brick towers directly. The stone glows red, and the shadows of the towers stretch across the terraces. It gets crowded at this hour, so come early to find a spot on the steps. Or come in the morning, when the temple is quieter.

Listen while you are up here. You will probably hear cicadas in the trees, motorbikes on the road below, and voices from other visitors. In the dry season, the ground around the temple is dusty and pale. In the wet season, the fields turn bright green, and the stone darkens after rain. Moss and lichen grow in the cracks. Small trees sometimes take root in the brickwork. The temple changes with the season and the time of day.

This temple is part of the Angkor Archaeological Park, which was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1992. It sits on the route often called the Grand Circuit, a longer loop around the main temples. Many visitors pass it quickly on the way to bigger sites. But Pre Rup is a good place to slow down. It shows the early form of ideas that later grew into Angkor Wat, built on a smaller and more human scale.

Before you leave, stand at the bottom of the stairs and look up once more. See the three terraces, the five towers, and the red stone against the sky. More than a thousand years ago, workers carried these blocks into place by hand. Priests climbed these same steps for ceremonies to Shiva. Now, as you walk back toward the road, notice how the colour of the stone continues to change behind you, minute by minute, as the light moves.

What to look for

Pale patches of old lime stucco on the brick towers.
Weathered stone lions guarding the steep stairways.
The large stone basin in the eastern courtyard.
Carved guardian figures on the sandstone false doors.
Porous, rust-red laterite blocks in the terrace walls.
Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive well before sunset to find space on the steps.
Visit in early morning for a quieter climb.
Wear shoes with good grip for the steep stairs.
Combine your visit with nearby East Mebon temple.
Bring water and a hat for the exposed upper terrace.
