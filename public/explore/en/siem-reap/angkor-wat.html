The guide

The story

The first light comes slow. You stand at the edge of a wide moat, and across the still water five stone towers rise against a paling sky. The air is warm and close. Birds call from the trees behind you. On the surface of the pool, the towers appear a second time, upside down, almost as sharp as the stone above. People gather quietly along the bank, waiting for the sun.

This is Angkor Wat. It is the largest religious monument on Earth by land area. A Khmer king named Suryavarman the Second ordered it built in the early twelfth century. The work went on for decades. Thousands of laborers cut and shaped the sandstone. They floated the heavy blocks down rivers and canals from a quarry many miles to the north. When it was done, it became the center of a great city.

The temple was first made for the Hindu god Vishnu. Later, as the kingdom turned toward Buddhism, the people changed its use. Monks came to live and pray here. They still do today. Through war, through floods, through the long pull of the forest, this place was never fully left behind. For more than eight hundred years, someone has always returned to care for it.

Cross the long stone causeway toward the gate. The moat is wide, almost two hundred meters across on each side. In the old belief, this water stands for the great ocean that rings the world. The five towers stand for Mount Meru, the home of the gods. The whole temple is built as a small model of the universe, laid out in careful stone.

Notice that Angkor Wat faces west. Most Khmer temples face east, toward the rising sun. West is the way of the setting sun, and of death. Some scholars believe this temple also served as a tomb for the king who built it. Others point to its bond with Vishnu, who is linked to the west. The true reason is still discussed by those who study it.

Walk into the long outer gallery. Here the walls carry some of the longest carved stories in the world. They run for hundreds of meters, panel after panel. Look closely as you pass. Armies march in rows. Kings ride high on elephants. Gods and demons pull on the body of a great serpent, churning the sea to draw out the drink of long life. The whole tale is told in stone.

Run your eyes along the figures. In some places the stone is worn smooth, polished by countless hands over many years. Look for the dancers carved across the walls. These are the apsaras, the women of the heavens. There are thousands of them here. Study their faces and their dress. No two of them wear quite the same hair, the same jewels, or the same quiet smile.

Climb higher, toward the central towers. The steps are steep and very narrow. They were made this way on purpose. To reach the home of the gods, a person had to climb slowly, with care, leaning forward, almost in the shape of prayer. Take your time on the way up. Hold the rail. The view from the top is worth each careful step.

From the upper level, the land opens out around you. Green forest reaches to the far edge of sight. Below, the courtyards form clean and ordered squares. The builders worked with deep knowledge of number and of the stars. The lengths of the temple may mark the ages of the world. At the spring point of the year, the sun climbs straight up over the central tower.

Think of the scale of the labor. There were no machines. There was no iron holding the walls together. The blocks were cut and rubbed until they fit so closely that a knife blade cannot slip between them. They are held in place by their own great weight, and by the skill of the hands that set them, one upon the next, across the years.

Angkor Wat is the pride of this country. Its shape sits at the center of the national flag. It is the only building anywhere in the world to hold such a place on the flag of a nation. For the people here, it is not a dead ruin. It is a living tie to those who came long before, and a sign of who they are.

Stand still a moment before you go. The stone holds the heat of the day under your hand. Somewhere within the walls, a bell sounds from a small shrine. Eight hundred years of sunrises have washed over these towers, and another will come tomorrow, with or without you. You are one of the countless many who have stood on this spot, small and quiet beneath the patient stone.

Good to know

Key facts

Built in the early twelfth century by Suryavarman the Second.
Largest religious monument on Earth by land area.
First dedicated to the Hindu god Vishnu.
The moat measures almost two hundred meters wide.
Its image appears at the center of Cambodia's flag.
Look closer

What to look for

Apsara dancers carved with differing hair and jewels.
The Churning of the Sea of Milk relief panel.
Steep narrow steps leading to the central sanctuary.
The towers reflected in the northern pool.
Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive before dawn for the sunrise over the towers.
Wear long trousers and covered shoulders to enter upper levels.
Bring water and a hat for the open courtyards.
Visit the central towers early before midday crowds arrive.
