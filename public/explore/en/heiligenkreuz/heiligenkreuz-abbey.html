The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beyond the courtyard gate, the traffic of the Vienna Woods fades away. Gravel shifts under your shoes. A bell may sound from the tower, slow and unhurried. The air here often smells of damp beech leaves and cool stone. This is Heiligenkreuz Abbey, a Cistercian monastery in a green valley southwest of Vienna. Monks have prayed in this valley for nearly nine centuries. That is the first surprise. This is not a museum. It is a home that never closed.

The story begins in 1133. Margrave Leopold the Third of the Babenberg family ruled this region. The Babenbergs were the dynasty that governed Austria before the Habsburgs. Leopold invited monks of the Cistercian order to settle here. The Cistercians were a strict reform movement within Benedictine monasticism. They came from France and valued silence, manual work, and simplicity. Leopold was later declared a saint, and today he is the patron saint of Lower Austria and Vienna.

The first monks came from the French abbey of Morimond. According to tradition, Leopold's son Otto had joined the Cistercians there, and this family link helped bring the community to Austria. Otto later became Bishop of Freising and one of the important historians of the Middle Ages. The monks cleared land, built in stone, and began the daily pattern of prayer that continues now. Since that founding, the community has lived here without interruption. Few monasteries anywhere in the world can say that.

Now look at the name. Heiligenkreuz means Holy Cross. In 1188, Duke Leopold the Fifth gave the monks a relic believed to be a fragment of the cross of Jesus. The relic became the spiritual heart of the abbey, and the village took its name from it. Pilgrims still come to see it. Whatever you believe, the relic shows how a small monastery in the woods became tied to the wider Christian world.

Stand in front of the abbey church and look at the facade. It is plain and severe. There are no towers rising from the front, no crowd of statues. This plainness is deliberate. The Cistercians rejected decoration that might distract a monk from prayer. Inside, the nave is Romanesque, the heavy, round-arched style of the twelfth century. The walls are thick. The pillars are solid. The windows are small. The light falls in narrow, quiet bands across the floor.

Walk further toward the altar, and the building changes. The eastern end is a Gothic hall choir, completed at the end of the thirteenth century. Here the ceiling rises higher, the pillars are slimmer, and the space fills with light. This is the second surprise. You can stand in one church and feel two centuries of architecture meet. Dark Romanesque weight on one side. Bright Gothic height on the other. The change happens almost in a single step.

Look up at the windows in the choir. Many are filled with grisaille glass. Grisaille means glass painted in grey and silver tones, with patterns of leaves and interlaced lines instead of colourful saints. The Cistercians preferred this restrained glass. Some of these panels are among the oldest stained glass in Austria. On a cloudy day, they glow a soft grey. In sunlight, they turn almost white, and the whole choir seems to brighten.

If you visit the cloister, usually on a guided tour, you enter the oldest daily world of the monks. The cloister is a square covered walkway around a garden. Its arches were built in the thirteenth century, as the Romanesque style gave way to Gothic. Look at the small columns. They are grouped in clusters, often in red-toned marble. Monks walked here to read, to think, and to move between the church and the rooms where they ate and worked.

Off the cloister stands the fountain house. It is a small chamber where the monks once washed before meals. Water ran from a basin in the centre. Its windows hold some of the abbey's medieval glass, including images connected to the Babenberg family. Nearby is the chapter house, where the community met each day. Several members of the Babenberg dynasty are buried there. Among them is Duke Frederick the Second, the last Babenberg ruler, who died in battle in 1246.

When you return to the outer courtyard, the mood changes again. This large open space is Baroque, the dramatic style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. In the middle rises a column dedicated to the Holy Trinity. It was created by Giovanni Giuliani, a Venetian-born sculptor who worked for the abbey for many years. Giuliani spent his final years here and was buried at Heiligenkreuz. His carved figures appear in the church too, in wood and stone.

Here is a modern twist that many visitors do not expect. In 2008, a recording of the monks singing Gregorian chant became a surprise international success. Gregorian chant is the ancient, single-line Latin song of the Catholic liturgy. The album reached the pop charts in several countries. Suddenly, a quiet monastery in the Vienna Woods was in the news. If you come at prayer time, you may hear the same chant sung live, as part of the monks' daily worship.

The abbey is also a place of study today. It runs a university of philosophy and theology, named after Pope Benedict the Sixteenth, who visited in 2007. Young monks and students from many countries come here. In the cemetery, a short walk away, you will find a quieter story. Mary Vetsera, the young woman who died with Crown Prince Rudolf at nearby Mayerling in 1889, is buried there. Her grave still receives flowers.

Before you leave, stand still in the courtyard once more. Look at the plain church front, the Baroque column, the woods rising beyond the roofs. Each layer belongs to a different century, yet the place feels whole. Listen for the bell. Somewhere inside, the monks may be gathering for prayer, as they have for almost nine hundred years. As you walk back toward the gate, the gravel will sound under your feet again... and the valley will grow quiet behind you.
