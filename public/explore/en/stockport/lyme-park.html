The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across open moorland, and somewhere a deer coughs in the bracken. The gravel crunches under your shoes, and the air smells of wet grass and peat. Then the ground falls away, and a great pale house appears in its hollow, with its stone glowing softly against the green hills. This is Lyme Park, on the western edge of the Peak District, a short drive from Stockport.

For around six hundred years, one family lived here, the Leghs. Their story at Lyme begins in the late fourteenth century, when the land came into the family's hands. Generation after generation, they hunted these hills, rebuilt the house, and filled it with treasures. In 1946, the last family owner gave the estate to the National Trust, a charity that cares for historic places across Britain. That gift is why you can walk through it today.

There is a legend about the family's beginnings. The legend involves a dog. Locals say that Sir Piers Legh was wounded at the Battle of Agincourt in 1415, and that his mastiff stood guard over him on the battlefield. The story is probably more romance than record. But the Leghs did breed mastiffs at Lyme for centuries, and the hall became closely linked with the breed. Keep an eye out for dogs in the paintings and carvings as you explore.

Now look at the house itself. From the outside, it seems like one calm, classical building. In fact, it is many buildings folded into one. The core is Elizabethan, from the late sixteenth century. In the early eighteenth century, the Venetian architect Giacomo Leoni gave it a grand Italian character. His style followed Andrea Palladio, a Renaissance architect who took ideas from ancient Roman temples. In the nineteenth century, Lewis Wyatt made further changes.

Walk around to the south front, and you will see Leoni's work most clearly. Tall columns hold up a triangular pediment, like the front of a temple. The stone is a local gritstone, a coarse, sandy rock from these hills. Over the years, it has weathered to soft greys and honey browns. Up close, you can feel its rough grain under your fingers. That is the first surprise here. Behind this Italian face, the Tudor house is still standing.

Some visitors know this place from another life entirely. In 1995, the BBC filmed its adaptation of Pride and Prejudice here, and Lyme played Pemberley, the home of Mr Darcy. One scene became famous. Darcy, played by Colin Firth, dives into the water and walks out in a wet shirt to meet Elizabeth Bennet. That scene was filmed beside the house, and many people still come to stand by the lake and picture it.

Inside the house, the rooms move through the centuries. Wood panelling darkens the older spaces, and the light falls in long bars through the windows. In the grand rooms, you will find carved limewood, with flowers, fruit, and game birds cut so finely that they look almost soft. These carvings have long been linked to Grinling Gibbons, the most celebrated woodcarver of his age in England. Look closely at the petals. Some are thinner than a fingernail.

Here is the second surprise, and it is easy to miss. Lyme holds a rare book, a missal, which is a service book for the Catholic Mass. This copy was printed by William Caxton, the man who brought the printing press to England in the fifteenth century. Very few copies of this missal survive anywhere. The Lyme copy also carries handwritten notes added by the family over generations. Printed history and family history sit together on the same pages.

The house also keeps a collection of clocks. In the quieter rooms, you may hear them ticking at slightly different speeds. Many of them are English, and some are several centuries old. Their faces show painted moons, brass engraving, and slow-moving hands. It is a gentle sound, and it suits this place. Time here has always been measured in seasons and generations, more than in hours.

Step outside again, and look up at the hill behind the house. A square stone tower stands alone on the ridge. This is the Cage. It was first built as a hunting lodge, a place where guests could watch the deer hunt across the park below. Later, it is said to have held prisoners for a time, which may explain the name. Today, it is mostly a viewpoint. From the top of the hill, on a clear day, you can see the Cheshire Plain and the towers of Manchester.

The deer are still here. Red deer have roamed this parkland for centuries, and the herd at Lyme is one of the oldest in England. In autumn, during the rut, which is the breeding season, the stags roar across the valleys. The sound is deep and rough, and it carries a long way. Please keep your distance from the deer. They are wild animals, and the stags can be dangerous in this season.

Below the house, the gardens offer a softer world. There is a sunken Dutch garden, with neat beds laid out in patterns that are best seen from above. There is also an orangery, a glass and stone garden room, where tender plants shelter from the Pennine weather. Beyond the formal gardens, paths lead into woodland and out onto open moor. In the space of a few minutes, you can walk from clipped hedges to wild heather.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look back at the house in its green bowl, and at the Cage on its hill. Listen for the wind, the birds, and perhaps a distant deer. Families, hunters, film crews, and walkers have all passed through this valley. Now it is your turn. As you walk away, notice how the house slips out of sight behind the slope. It stays hidden until the next visitor comes over the rise.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk up to the Cage for wide views on clear days.
Bring waterproof layers because moorland weather changes quickly.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep, muddy park paths.
Keep well back from red deer during the autumn rut.
Check the National Trust website before visiting the house interior.
