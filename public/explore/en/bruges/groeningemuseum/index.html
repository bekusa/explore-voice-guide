The guide

The story

Leave the canal behind for a moment. The water along the Dijver moves slowly here, and the sound of tour boats fades as you walk through a quiet garden toward a low, pale building. Inside, the air is cool and still. The light is soft and even, kept low to protect old paint. Your footsteps sound louder than you expect. This is the Groeningemuseum, the city museum of fine art in Bruges. It holds some of the most remarkable paintings ever made in northern Europe.

The building itself is quieter than the art it holds. It opened in the early twentieth century, on ground that once belonged to a medieval abbey. The architecture is plain and modest. That suits the collection well, because nothing here competes with the pictures. Many of the rooms are small. The paintings hang close to eye level, and you can stand nearer to them than you might expect. That closeness is the real gift of this museum.

To understand why these paintings exist, picture Bruges in the fifteenth century. The city was one of the great trading centres of Europe. Italian bankers, Hanseatic merchants from the Baltic, and traders from Spain and England all did business here. Wool, cloth, spices and silk passed through its harbour. The Dukes of Burgundy, who ruled these lands, spent long periods in the city. Money and ambition gathered in one place. And money, then as now, wanted to be remembered in paint.

The painters who answered that demand are often called the Flemish Primitives. The name is misleading. There is nothing primitive about their work. The word simply marks them as early, the first generation of a northern tradition. These artists perfected painting with oil. They built up thin, glowing layers of colour, one over another. The result was a depth and a precision that had never been seen before. Velvet looks soft. Metal looks cold. Skin looks warm.

The central figure is Jan van Eyck. He worked in Bruges and served the Burgundian court. One of his greatest works hangs here — the Madonna with Canon Joris van der Paele, painted in 1436. The Virgin sits on a throne with the Christ Child on her lap. Beside her kneels an elderly canon in a white surplice. Look at his face. Every wrinkle, every fold of loose skin, every tired vein near the temple is there. Van Eyck did not flatter him.

Now look at Saint George, standing behind the canon in polished armour. Many visitors walk past without noticing a small detail. On the curved metal, van Eyck painted reflections of the room. Some viewers believe they can see a tiny figure reflected there — perhaps the painter himself, at work. Take your time. Lean in, as far as the guards allow. The detail is small, and it rewards patience.

Nearby, look for a much smaller panel. It is a portrait of Margaret, van Eyck's wife, painted in 1439. She looks straight out at you, calm and direct. Her red dress is trimmed with fur, and her white headdress is folded with great care. The original frame carries an inscription. It says, in effect, that her husband completed the portrait, and it adds his personal motto: "As I can." It is a modest phrase from a painter with very little reason to be modest.

Other masters of the age are here as well. Hans Memling, who settled in Bruges, painted calm faces and clear, balanced compositions. Hugo van der Goes painted the Death of the Virgin, with apostles gathered close around her bed, their faces full of private grief. Each artist had his own temperament. Walking from one panel to the next, you can start to tell them apart by their colours and by the mood of their faces.

Then comes a painting that stops many visitors in their tracks. It is The Judgement of Cambyses by Gerard David, completed in 1498. It tells an ancient story. A Persian judge named Sisamnes takes a bribe. The king has him arrested — and then flayed alive. David shows the skinning in calm, exact detail, while well-dressed onlookers stand by. The painting was made for the city's own magistrates. It hung where they worked, as a warning to every judge who looked up from his desk.

Not far away, the mood turns even stranger. A Last Judgement triptych connected to Hieronymus Bosch shows a world of small monsters, burning landscapes and odd hybrid creatures. A triptych is a painting in three hinged panels, often made for an altar. Paradise sits on one side. Hell spreads across the other. Look closely at the tiny figures. Some are funny. Some are cruel. Bosch, or the artists working in his manner, packed every corner with invention.

The museum does not stop in the Middle Ages. As you move through the later rooms, you follow Belgian art across the centuries. There are works from the Baroque period and from the nineteenth century. Then the twentieth century arrives with Symbolists and Expressionists, and with Surrealists such as René Magritte and Paul Delvaux. Belgian painters have always had a strong taste for the strange and the dreamlike. In these rooms, you can see that thread running from Bosch all the way to modern times.

A practical word as you walk. The collection is not enormous, so you do not need to rush. Some works travel on loan or rotate for conservation, so the hang may change between visits. If a particular painting matters to you, look for it first, then let yourself wander. Sit on a bench. Return to a favourite picture after an hour. The paintings look different once your eyes have adjusted to the quiet light.

When you leave, step back out into the garden and walk toward the canal. Look at the brick houses, the stepped gables and the slow, green water. Many of the people in those panels walked these same streets. The light that van Eyck studied so carefully still falls across these rooftops. For a moment, the city outside and the paintings inside belong to the same world. Take that thought with you as you cross the bridge.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Flemish Primitives rooms first, while your eyes are fresh.
Arrive early to have the van Eyck panels mostly to yourself.
Check ahead whether favourite works are on loan or rotated.
Combine the visit with a walk along the Dijver canal.
Use the benches to rest and revisit a favourite painting.
