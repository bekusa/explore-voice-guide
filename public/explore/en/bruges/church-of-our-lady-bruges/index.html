The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look up. A wall of dark brick climbs above you, then keeps climbing, until your neck aches. The air in this corner of Bruges is cool and slightly damp. It carries the smell of canal water and old stone. Somewhere nearby, horse hooves knock on cobbles. This is the Church of Our Lady, and its tower has watched over the city for most of a thousand years.

That tower is the first surprise. It rises roughly 115 metres, and it is built almost entirely of brick. Very few brick structures anywhere in the world reach this height. In a region with little good building stone, Flemish builders learned to do remarkable things with baked clay. Brick also explains the colour, a deep reddish brown. It turns almost purple in the evening light, and pale and dusty at noon.

The church grew slowly. Most of what you see was built between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries, in stages, as money and ambition allowed. The oldest parts follow a style historians call Scheldt Gothic. This is a local version of Gothic architecture, named after the Scheldt river, which carried stone and ideas through Flanders. Later builders added chapels, aisles and that soaring spire. So the building is less a single design and more a long conversation between generations.

To understand why a church of this scale stands here, picture Bruges in the Middle Ages. This was one of the richest trading cities in northern Europe. Wool, cloth, spices and silk moved through its canals and markets. Merchants from Italy, Spain and the German cities kept offices here. That wealth flowed into churches, guild halls and art. Pious donors paid for chapels, altarpieces and tombs, hoping to secure their reputations and their souls.

Step inside, and the temperature drops. The light changes too. It falls in long, soft shafts across whitewashed walls and dark wooden furnishings. Your footsteps echo on the stone floor, and many of those floor slabs are old grave markers, worn smooth by centuries of shoes. Take a slow look around before you go looking for the famous treasures. The ordinary texture of this space is part of the story.

Now for the reason many visitors come. In a side chapel sits a white marble sculpture of the Virgin Mary with the infant Jesus. It is the work of Michelangelo, carved in the early 1500s. As far as historians know, it is the only sculpture by Michelangelo that left Italy during his lifetime. It came here because of Bruges money. A wealthy Flemish cloth-merchant family, the Mouscrons, bought it and gave it to this church.

Look closely at the figures. Mary does not hold her son tightly. She sits calm and serious, almost distant, with her eyes lowered. The child stands between her knees and seems ready to step away from her. Many people read this as a quiet sign of what is to come, a mother who already knows she will lose her son. The marble surface is smooth and cool, and the folds of her robe look heavy, almost like real cloth.

This small statue has had a dramatic life. In the 1790s, French revolutionary forces took it to Paris. It came back to Bruges only after the fall of Napoleon. Then, near the end of the Second World War, retreating German troops took it again. Allied specialists, the art-recovery team later known as the Monuments Men, found it hidden in a salt mine in Austria. Their story became a Hollywood film, and the Madonna appears in it.

Further inside, in the choir, lie two of the most important tombs in Flemish history. One belongs to Mary of Burgundy, heir to a powerful and wealthy realm. She died young in 1482, after a fall from her horse while hunting. Her marriage to Maximilian of Austria tied these lands to the Habsburg family, and that link shaped European politics for centuries. Her gilded effigy lies with hands joined in prayer, small dogs resting at her feet.

Beside her lies her father, Charles the Bold, the last great Duke of Burgundy. He died in battle at Nancy in 1477. His remains were brought here much later, in the sixteenth century. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Historians still debate whether the bones in this tomb are really his. The identification on the battlefield was uncertain from the start. So the grand monument may honour a man who lies somewhere else entirely.

Now look down, near the tombs. In the late 1970s, excavations beneath the choir uncovered medieval burial vaults. The surprise was inside them. Their walls were painted with simple, bright images, such as crosses, angels and saints, made to keep the dead company in the dark. These paintings were never meant to be seen by the living. Some of these vaults are now visible, and they show an intimate side of medieval faith.

Before you leave, look up toward the choir for a wooden balcony with a window. This is the Gruuthuse oratory, a private gallery that connects the church to the palace of the Gruuthuse family next door. The lords of Gruuthuse could attend mass here without walking through the street, and without mixing with ordinary worshippers. It is a small architectural detail, but it shows very clearly how wealth and power shaped daily life in Bruges.

When you step back outside, walk a few minutes around the building. The Gruuthuse palace stands close by, and the old Saint John's Hospital lies just across the street. The little Bonifacius bridge is nearby, with its view of brick facades leaning over green water. Pause there and turn back toward the church. The tower rises above the rooftops, the same brick that has stood through wars, revolutions and centuries of rain... and it is still here, watching the city go about its day.
