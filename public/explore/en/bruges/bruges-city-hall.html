The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the paving stones of the Burg. Feel how they rise and dip slightly under your shoes, worn smooth by centuries of carts, boots, and bicycle tyres. Listen. There is the clatter of a horse-drawn carriage somewhere behind you, the murmur of voices in a dozen languages, and the soft call of pigeons on the ledges above. Now look up at the pale stone front in front of you, crowded with statues and pointed windows. This is Bruges City Hall.

The building you are facing is one of the oldest town halls in the Low Countries. Work on it began in 1376, and the construction continued for decades, into the early fifteenth century. That makes it older than most of the guildhalls and town halls in this part of Europe. And here is the first surprise: it is still a working building. The city council of Bruges still meets inside. Couples still come here to be married. Over six hundred years after the first stones went in, it has never stopped doing the job it was built for.

To understand why Bruges could afford such a building, imagine the city in the fourteenth century. Bruges was then one of the great trading centres of northern Europe. Ships brought wool from England, spices and silk from the south, furs and timber from the Baltic. Italian bankers and German merchants kept offices here. The money that flowed through this city was enormous. A town hall in carved stone was a message to every visitor: this city governs itself, and it is rich enough to show it.

The style of the building is Gothic — the style of pointed arches, tall windows, and fine stone tracery that you also see in the great cathedrals of the Middle Ages. But this is not a church. It is a civic building, and that matters. Here, Gothic decoration was used to honour the city and its rulers rather than the saints alone. Many historians see this facade as a model that later town halls in Flanders followed, though each city added its own ambitions and its own scale.

Look now at the statues that fill the niches across the front. They show the counts and countesses of Flanders, the medieval rulers of this region, standing in a long row of crowns, robes, and folded hands. Here is the second surprise. These are not the original figures. In the 1790s, when French revolutionary forces controlled the region, the medieval statues were pulled down and destroyed as symbols of the old order. The figures you see today are later replacements, carved from the nineteenth century onward.

That same revolutionary period changed the whole square around you. Turn and imagine a great church filling part of the open space behind you. Saint Donatian's Cathedral once stood on this square, the most important church in the city. Under French rule it was sold and demolished around the end of the eighteenth century. Its stones were carried away. Today, the open paving and the trees stand where its walls and towers once rose. The Burg feels spacious now, but it once felt very different.

Let your eyes travel up to the roofline. Slender turrets rise at the top of the facade, pointed and decorative, like small towers set against the sky. Below them, the windows are tall and narrow, divided by stone mullions — the vertical stone bars that split a window into sections. Notice how the whole front is almost covered in carving. There are very few blank surfaces. The builders wanted the eye to keep moving, never resting, always finding another detail.

If you go inside, climb to the upper floor and enter the Gothic Hall. This is the room most visitors remember. Above you hangs a vaulted wooden ceiling, carved in oak and painted in deep colours, completed around the year 1402. Look closely at the points where the ribs of the vault meet. Carved pendants hang down from these points, like stone drops frozen in wood. They show figures and scenes, and they reward a slow, patient look with your head tilted back.

The walls of the Gothic Hall are covered with large painted murals. They are much younger than the ceiling. They were painted in the late nineteenth and early twentieth century, at a time when Belgium was a young country and many cities wanted to celebrate their medieval past. The paintings show key moments from the history of Bruges — scenes of rulers, citizens, and civic pride. They are history as the nineteenth century wanted to remember it: grand, heroic, and full of colour.

That is worth keeping in mind as you look around. Much of what we call medieval Bruges was carefully restored, rebuilt, or reimagined in the nineteenth century. For a long period, the city had lost its trade and much of its wealth. Then writers and artists rediscovered it as a romantic, sleeping medieval town. Restoration followed. So in this building, you are looking at two pasts at once — the real fourteenth century and the nineteenth century's dream of it.

Next to the Gothic Hall, a smaller room usually displays documents, maps, and objects from the city's past. Old charters and plans help explain how Bruges was governed and how it grew. Take a moment with any old map of the city you find. You can often trace the egg-shaped ring of canals that still surrounds the historic centre today. Then step outside and you will understand that the streets you are walking still follow those medieval lines.

Back on the square, look at the neighbours of the City Hall. In one corner stands the Basilica of the Holy Blood, a small church with a lower chapel in heavy, round-arched Romanesque style — an older style of thick walls and simple curves — and a later chapel above. Nearby you will see the former Liberty of Bruges, another seat of government with its own history. Together these buildings make the Burg the old centre of power in the city, while the Market Square nearby was the centre of trade.

Before you walk on, stand back near the middle of the square and take in the whole front once more. Watch how the light moves across the pale stone as clouds pass. Notice the row of rulers in their niches, the turrets against the sky, the people going in and out of the doors below. This building has seen merchants, revolutionaries, restorers, and newlyweds. As you leave the Burg, listen again to the stones under your feet. They have carried all of those people before you.
