The guide

The story

Lean on a railing in downtown Napa and watch the water. It moves slowly here, wide and green-brown, breathing with the ocean tides. This is the Napa River: the reason the town exists, the force that nearly destroyed it, and one of the quietest comeback stories in California. Give it ten minutes and it will slow you down.

The river runs about 55 miles from the slopes above Calistoga down to San Pablo Bay. It drains the whole Napa Valley, gathering creeks from the Mayacamas mountains on one side and the Vaca range on the other. By the time it reaches the city of Napa, it is tidal. Salt water and fresh water trade places twice a day.

For the valley's first peoples, the river was a pantry. Wappo and Patwin communities fished its runs of salmon and steelhead and gathered food in its marshes for thousands of years. The name Napa itself is usually traced to a native word, though its exact meaning is debated.

American settlement turned the river into a road. From the 1850s, steamboats churned up from San Francisco Bay to the wharves of Napa, hauling passengers one way and grain, hides, and wine the other. Mills and tanneries crowded the banks. The river made this inland town a working port.

Railroads and highways slowly stole the traffic. The last working boats faded away in the twentieth century, and the town turned its back on the water. Warehouses decayed. The banks filled with brush and rubbish. The river became something you crossed on a bridge without looking down.

But the river refused to be ignored. Napa sits low, and winter storms stack rain against the incoming tide. The city flooded more than twenty times in its recorded history. The flood of February 1986 was the worst: thousands of people left their homes, and the damage ran to many millions of dollars. Another major flood struck on New Year's Eve of 2005.

In 1998, Napa County voters did something unusual. They approved Measure A, taxing themselves half a cent on the dollar to fund a flood project built around a simple idea: stop fighting the river. Engineers call it the living river principle. Give the water room, and it will do less harm.

The results reshaped the city. Crews lowered banks into planted terraces and reconnected the river to hundreds of acres of tidal marsh downstream. At the oxbow bend, a dry bypass channel now waits for high water; in 2015 it opened as parkland that doubles as a floodway. Bridges were raised. A promenade replaced crumbling walls.

The project took patience. Work began in 2000 and advanced in stages as money allowed. Federal engineers partnered with the county, and environmental groups helped shape the design, an alliance that had rarely happened in flood control before. Other cities now study Napa's example when they plan their own rivers.

Nature answered quickly. Steelhead and Chinook salmon still ride the winter flows upstream. River otters hunt along the downtown banks at dawn. Osprey circle over the oxbow, and white egrets stand in the restored shallows like small statues. In a valley famous for what people grow, the river is where things grow back on their own. Seasons change the river's mood. Summer draws it down thin and lazy, all glare and dragonflies. First autumn rains stain it brown with hillside soil. In winter it moves fast and serious, carrying whole branches toward the bay. Spring settles it again, green at the edges, loud with birds.

You can meet the river at its own pace. Walk the promenade through downtown and follow the trail toward the oxbow. Rent a kayak or a paddleboard and ride the flat water between the bridges. At low tide the mudflats click and pop with unseen life; at high tide the river fills its banks like a held breath.

The river also explains the wine. Over long ages, its floods spread gravel and silt across the valley floor, building the deep soils the vineyards stand on today. Every glass of Napa wine holds a little of the river's work. The valley's most famous product begins with its least famous feature.

So give the water a few unhurried minutes. Watch the tide turn against the pilings. Somewhere upstream, winter rain is already waiting, and the river will rise and fall long after every visitor has gone home. Towns, like rivers, are healthiest when they remember what shaped them. Napa finally does.
