The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The stones of Praça da Sé are worn smooth, and they carry the steady traffic of a city of millions. Pigeons lift and settle. A street preacher raises his voice, a vendor calls out, and buses rumble somewhere behind the palm trees. Then you look up. Two grey spires rise above the noise, sharp and pale against the sky. This is the Catedral da Sé, the metropolitan cathedral of São Paulo, and it stands at the true centre of the city.

That phrase, the true centre, is meant literally. Somewhere in this square sits a marker called Marco Zero, the zero point of São Paulo. Road distances from the city are traditionally measured from here. Look for a small stone monument set into the ground near the cathedral steps. Most visitors walk straight past it. Locals sometimes joke that if you are lost in this enormous city, you can always start again from zero.

The building in front of you is younger than it looks. Its pointed arches and soaring towers follow the Neo-Gothic style, a nineteenth and twentieth century revival of the great medieval cathedrals of Europe. But this is not a medieval church. An older colonial cathedral stood on this site for a long time. In the early twentieth century, the city decided it needed something grander, and the old church was demolished to make room.

Work on the new cathedral began in 1913. The design came from Maximilian Emil Hehl, a German-born architect who taught in São Paulo. Progress was slow. Two world wars, economic crises and the sheer scale of the project stretched construction over decades. The cathedral was inaugurated in 1954, timed to the four hundredth anniversary of the founding of São Paulo. Even then it was not finished. The two great towers were only completed in the late 1960s.

Now look at the shape of the building as a whole. Here is the first surprise. Above the Gothic spires and pointed windows sits a large dome. Domes belong to the Renaissance tradition, the style of Florence and Rome, not to Gothic architecture. Hehl combined the two. The result is a cathedral that looks northern European from the front and Italian from certain angles. Walk a little to one side of the square and you can see the dome rise behind the towers.

The stone itself is worth a closer look. The exterior is clad in granite, pale and grey, and the city air has marked it over the years. At the start of the 2000s, the cathedral went through a major restoration. Workers cleaned the facade, repaired damage and finished decorative details that had never been completed in the original build. So some of the carving you see today is much newer than the walls around it.

Step inside, and the sound changes at once. The roar of the square drops away. Footsteps echo on the floor, and voices fall to a murmur. The nave is long and tall, with columns rising toward ribbed vaults. Light falls through stained glass windows and lays patches of colour on the stone. The cathedral is one of the largest churches in Brazil, and it can hold several thousand people at once.

Here is the second surprise, and it rewards a patient eye. Look closely at the tops of the columns, the carved sections called capitals. In a European Gothic cathedral, you would expect acanthus leaves or saints. Here, the carvers worked in Brazilian plants and animals. Coffee branches appear among the decoration, a nod to the crop that built the fortune of São Paulo. Pineapples and other tropical motifs appear too. It is a quiet way of saying this Gothic church belongs to Brazil.

If you are lucky, you may hear the organ. The cathedral houses a large pipe organ built by the Italian firm Balbiani, and it is counted among the biggest instruments of its kind in Latin America. When it plays, the sound fills the vaults and seems to come from every direction. Even in silence, look up toward the pipes. They show how seriously the builders took music as part of worship here.

Beneath your feet lies another layer of the story. Under the main altar is a crypt, a burial chamber reached by stairs. It holds the tombs of bishops and archbishops of São Paulo. It also holds the remains of two figures from very different worlds. One is Tibiriçá, an Indigenous chief who allied with the Portuguese in the early years of the settlement. The other is Diogo Feijó, a priest who served as regent of the Brazilian empire in the nineteenth century.

The cathedral has never been only a religious space. The square outside has long served as a stage for the city. In 1984, Praça da Sé filled with huge crowds for the Diretas Já movement. People gathered to demand direct elections for president at the end of military rule. Church leaders in São Paulo played a visible role in defending human rights during that era. The steps in front of you have seen prayer, protest and celebration.

Today the square still carries that mix. Office workers cut across on their way to the metro. People rest on benches in the shade. Some of the city's homeless residents live in and around the square, and charity groups often work here. It is a busy, sometimes rough, very human place. Keep your belongings close, as you would in any crowded city centre. Also take a moment simply to watch how São Paulo moves around its own heart.

Before you leave, stand back near the palm trees and let your eyes travel up the facade. Follow the granite from the doors to the tips of the spires. Remember that this building took most of a century to finish, and that its walls stand on ground the city has called its centre for far longer. The noise around you will carry on. The towers will stay where they are. And somewhere under your feet, the city starts again from zero.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the crypt beneath the altar, which may need separate access.
Keep bags zipped and in front of you in the square.
Arrive by metro; Sé station sits right beside the cathedral.
Check the schedule for organ recitals or sung Masses.
Dress modestly and stay quiet during services.
