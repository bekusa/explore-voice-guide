The guide

The story

Listen first. A low, steady roar of traffic rolls along the riverbank. Engines, tyres on concrete, the hiss of a passing train. Above all that noise, something stands perfectly still. A tall concrete mast shaped like a giant letter X rises over the water. Steel cables fan down from it like the strings of a harp. This is the Octávio Frias de Oliveira Bridge, and in a city of endless motion it is one of the few things that holds still.

Most people in São Paulo do not use the official name. They call it simply the Ponte Estaiada, which means the cable-stayed bridge. A cable-stayed bridge carries its road deck on straight cables that run directly from a tower. It does not hang from long curved cables the way a suspension bridge does. Here the idea goes one step further. The bridge carries two decks, and those decks curve and cross each other in mid-air, right beneath the tower.

Look closely at the X-shaped mast. The two curving roadways do not simply pass beside it. Each one passes through the open space inside the X. Drivers heading in each direction glide between the legs of the tower. Then they sweep away in a long arc. The arrangement looks almost like a knot tied in the sky. It is the detail that most visitors miss from a distance... and it is the reason the bridge looks so unusual from every angle.

The tower rises to roughly 138 metres. That makes it taller than many of the office towers around it. Its concrete is pale grey, smooth, and plain. The cables are bundles of steel, sheathed and anchored along the edges of the decks. Together they form two great fans of thin lines. From some angles, the fans overlap and create a shimmering pattern, like a sheet of fine fabric. Walk a few steps, and the pattern shifts.

The bridge opened in 2008. It spans the Pinheiros River and links the expressway along the riverbank, the Marginal Pinheiros, with Avenida Jornalista Roberto Marinho. That avenue leads toward the southern districts and the airport at Congonhas. Before the bridge existed, this junction was a difficult place for traffic. The new structure gave cars a direct, elevated route across the water. It was a practical piece of road engineering — but the city clearly wanted it to be a landmark too.

The name honours Octávio Frias de Oliveira. He was a publisher who led Folha de S.Paulo, one of Brazil's major newspapers, for decades. He died in 2007, shortly before the bridge opened. Naming the bridge after him linked this new symbol to the history of the city's press. It is an interesting choice. A bridge that crosses a river in a fixed place carries the name of a man whose work was about moving information.

Here is a surprise for many visitors. You cannot walk across the bridge. It was designed for motor vehicles, and it has no walkway for pedestrians. The best way to enjoy it is from the outside — from the riverbanks, from nearby streets, or from the windows of a passing train. In a way, that suits the bridge. It is a structure people look at more than they stand on.

One of the best moving viewpoints is the commuter railway. The trains of Line 9, the Emerald line, run along the Pinheiros River. As a train approaches, the tower appears between buildings, grows larger, and then slides past the window. For a few seconds, the cables line up and separate again. Many residents see the bridge this way every day, on the way to work and back home. For them, it marks a familiar point on the daily journey.

The neighbourhood around the bridge tells its own story. To one side rise the glass towers of the Berrini business district, full of offices, banks, and technology companies. Not long ago, much of this area was lower and quieter. The bridge arrived as the district grew upward. Today the mast stands among these modern towers like a frame around them. Television news programmes have often used this skyline as a backdrop, so many Brazilians recognise the view from their living rooms.

The river below deserves a moment too. The Pinheiros once wound across a wide floodplain. During the twentieth century, engineers straightened its course and lined it with expressways. For many years the water has suffered from pollution, and the city has worked on cleaning it. As you look down, you may see the slow brown-green current and the concrete banks. The bridge gives an elegant shape to a landscape that is mostly practical and heavily built.

Wait until evening if you can. After dark, lights wash the tower and cables in colour. The colours change, and on some occasions they mark public campaigns or special dates. The cables glow against the night sky, and their reflections ripple on the river. Traffic becomes a stream of red and white lights flowing through the X. Photographers often come at dusk, when the sky still holds some blue and the lights have just come on.

For the clearest photographs, look for a spot along the riverbank with an open view of the tower. A longer lens can compress the cables into a dense web of lines. A wide lens works better if you want the full curve of both decks. On clear days, the morning and late afternoon light falls across the concrete at a low angle. That light brings out the edges of the mast and the depth of the cable fans.

It helps to remember what the bridge means to the city. São Paulo is a vast place of roads, rail lines, and towers. It has few single structures that everyone can point to and name. This bridge became one of them very quickly. It appears on postcards, in advertisements, and in countless photographs from car windows. Some people love its bold shape. Others see it as a monument to car traffic. Both views are part of its story.

Now take one more look. Follow a single cable from the deck up to the top of the mast. Then follow the curve of a roadway as it passes through the X and bends away across the river. Listen again to the traffic, the trains, and the wind. As you leave, watch the tower over your shoulder. Even from far away, between buildings and above the road, that pale X will still be standing there, holding the city's movement in place.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride CPTM Line 9 for a moving view along the river.
Come at dusk to see the coloured lights switch on.
Plan to view it from outside; it has no pedestrian walkway.
Bring a long lens to compress the cable fans.
Combine your visit with a walk around the Berrini district.
