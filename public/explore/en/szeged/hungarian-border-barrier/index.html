The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. On the open plain south of Szeged, the wind moves without stopping. It runs through fields of maize and sunflowers and meets almost nothing in its way. The land is so flat that the sky takes up most of what you see. Then a straight line of grey steel posts crosses the fields, one after another, toward the horizon. Coils of wire catch the light along the top. This is where Hungary meets Serbia.

What you are looking at is the Hungarian border barrier, a fence that runs along the country's southern frontier. It was built in 2015, first along the border with Serbia and later along much of the border with Croatia. Szeged is the largest Hungarian city close to the line. That makes this flat corner of the Great Hungarian Plain one of the places most closely linked to a story that divided Europe.

To understand the fence, go back to the summer of 2015. Hundreds of thousands of people were travelling through the Balkans toward western Europe. Many were escaping the war in Syria. Others came from Afghanistan, Iraq, and beyond. Hungary sat at an important point on that route. It is inside the Schengen area, the group of European countries with no passport checks at their shared borders. For many travellers, crossing into Hungary meant reaching the open door to the rest of the continent.

The government decided to close that door. Construction began in the summer of 2015, and it moved quickly. The first version was simple: rolls of razor wire laid along the frontier, followed by a fence of steel mesh. By the middle of September, the border with Serbia was sealed with barrier. The speed surprised many observers. A border that had been almost invisible in these fields only weeks before was now a hard physical line.

The village of Röszke, just south of Szeged, became the focus of the world's attention. The main road crossing to Serbia is here, on the motorway that runs from Budapest to the south. In September 2015, crowds gathered on the Serbian side when the crossing closed. Police used tear gas and water cannon. Television crews from many countries filmed the scenes. One moment became widely known: a camerawoman was filmed tripping a man who was running with a child in his arms.

The first fence was only the beginning. In the following years, Hungary added a second, taller fence behind the first one. Between the two lines there is a patrol road for police vehicles. The newer fence carries cameras, sensors, and loudspeakers. Here is a detail most visitors miss — the loudspeakers were designed to give warnings in several languages. So the barrier is not one wall. It is a system of layers, built to watch as well as to block.

For several years, there was also a place to wait. At Röszke and at Tompa, a crossing further west, the authorities set up transit zones. These were camps made of metal shipping containers, surrounded by fences, right on the border line. People who asked for asylum had to stay there while officials examined their cases. Human rights groups criticised the conditions strongly. In 2020, after a ruling by the Court of Justice of the European Union, Hungary closed the transit zones.

Now for a surprise that many people find hard to believe. Hungary was the first country to cut through the Iron Curtain, the fortified line that divided communist eastern Europe from the West. In 1989, Hungarian border guards began taking down the fence on the border with Austria. That summer, thousands of East Germans used Hungary as their route to the West. So a country once celebrated for opening a fence became, a generation later, famous for building one.

The frontier here has a human side too. Szeged has long looked south. Across the line lies the Serbian province of Vojvodina, home to a large Hungarian-speaking community. The Serbian town of Subotica is only a short drive away. For many families in this region, the border has never been just a line on a map. It runs between cousins, markets, churches, and old friendships. People still cross every day for work, shopping, and visits.

The barrier remains a subject of strong debate. Supporters say it gave Hungary control of its border and reduced irregular crossings sharply. Critics say it pushed desperate people onto longer and more dangerous routes, and that it broke European rules on the right to seek asylum. Both sides use this same steel line to make their arguments. Standing here, you do not have to decide. But it helps to know that people see very different things in it.

A practical word before you look more closely. The barrier is an active, guarded border, not a museum. You cannot walk up to the fence, and the strip beside it is a restricted area. Police and soldiers patrol it. The safest way to see it is from a public road, or as you pass the official crossing at Röszke. Keep a respectful distance, and do not point a camera at officers or their vehicles without care.

If you can see the fence clearly, notice how it is made. The posts are set at regular intervals, straight as a ruler across the farmland. The steel mesh is fine enough that a hand cannot get a good grip. Coils of razor wire sit along the base or the top. Behind it, look for the pale gravel or sand of the patrol track. Watch for the tall poles that carry cameras and lights. At night, those lights draw a bright line across the dark plain.

The contrast with the landscape is the strongest impression for many visitors. On one side there are farms, irrigation ditches, and lines of poplar trees. Birds fly across without stopping. Tractors work the fields right up to the edge of the restricted strip. The soil is the same dark earth on both sides. Only the steel shows where one country ends and the next begins.

Before you leave, look along the line once more. Follow it with your eyes until it becomes too thin to see. Notice the wind again, the same wind that met you when you arrived. It crosses the fence without any effort at all. As you drive back toward Szeged and its river, you may find yourself watching for other borders — the ones we notice, and the many we pass every day without a thought.

Before you go

Practical tips

View the barrier from public roads, never inside the restricted strip.
Pass the official Röszke crossing to see the frontier up close.
Avoid photographing police officers or patrol vehicles directly.
Carry your passport if you plan to cross into Serbia.
Combine the trip with a day in central Szeged.
