The guide

The story

Leave the street noise behind and walk down the steps. The air changes almost at once. It is cooler here, and slightly damp, and it smells of old stone and brick. Your footsteps sound different too. They land, then come back to you a moment later from somewhere in the dark. Ahead of you, rows of pale marble columns run off into the shadows, one after another, like trees in a planted forest. You are standing inside the Cistern of Philoxenos, under the streets of old Istanbul.

In Turkish, this place is called Binbirdirek. The name means "one thousand and one columns." That is not a real count. In Turkish, "one thousand and one" simply means a great many, the way an English speaker might say "countless." The real number is much smaller. Most sources give it as 224. Still, stand here for a minute and let your eyes follow the rows. The Ottoman name starts to make sense. The columns seem to multiply as the light fades between them.

So why is there a forest of marble under a city street? The answer is water. Constantinople, the Byzantine capital that later became Istanbul, sat on a hilly peninsula with few natural springs. The emperors brought water from far away through aqueducts. Then they stored it underground in huge reservoirs like this one. A sealed room below ground kept the water cool and clean. It also protected the city if an enemy cut the supply during a siege.

The name Philoxenos comes from a story about the cistern's origins. Tradition links it to a wealthy official of that name. He is said to have come to the new capital in the time of Constantine the Great, in the fourth century. That early date is part of the legend. Many historians think the structure you see today was built or rebuilt later, in the fifth or sixth century. Either way, this was Byzantine engineering, built to serve a growing imperial city.

In the Byzantine world, this space was not meant for visitors. It was meant to be full. Imagine the water rising up the columns, dark and still, almost to the ceiling. No one walked here. Workers might row in by small boat to check the walls. The brick vaults above you held up the buildings and gardens on the surface. Today you can stand on the floor and look up, but for centuries this whole room was a hidden lake.

Now look closely at one of the columns. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Each column is not one piece of stone. It is two shafts, set one on top of the other, and joined in the middle. You can often see the seam where they meet. Stacking shorter pieces let the builders reach the height they needed. It also made the stone easier to cut, carry, and lift into place under the ground.

There is a second surprise on the marble itself. Many columns carry small carved marks. Some of them look like Greek letters. These are thought to be masons' marks, the signatures of workshops or quarries that cut the stone. They were not decoration. No one expected these marks to be seen once the water rose. They were practical labels, a way to track whose work was whose. Move slowly along the rows and see how many you can find.

Above the columns, the ceiling is made of brick. It forms a series of shallow domes, row after row, each one resting on four columns. This repeating pattern spread the weight of the city above evenly across the whole room. It is simple, strong, and very old as a building idea. Notice how the warm colour of the brick sits against the cooler grey and white of the marble. The two materials work together here, holding each other up.

After the Ottoman conquest in 1453, the city's water needs changed, and many cisterns fell out of use. This one found a second life. In later Ottoman centuries, the dry cistern became a workplace. According to accounts of the period, thread makers and silk spinners used the space. The cool, damp air was helpful for their work, because it kept fibres from drying out and snapping. So for a long time, this room was filled not with water, but with the hum of people working.

Over the years, earth and rubble collected on the floor. Part of each column disappeared under the fill. This is why the columns may look shorter than you would expect. Some of their original height is still hidden below your feet. In the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, the cistern was cleaned and restored. Lighting, walkways, and new floors were added. Today the space is used for events, exhibitions, and receptions, so what you see may change from one visit to the next.

It also helps to know where you are on the map. This cistern lies in the historic heart of the old city, inside the modern district of Fatih. Only a short walk away is the old Hippodrome, where Byzantine crowds once watched chariot races. Nearby are the great domes of Hagia Sophia and the Blue Mosque. Above you, life goes on. Shops, cafes, and hotels line the streets. Most people walking past have no idea this room is under them.

Many visitors to Istanbul know the Basilica Cistern, the city's largest and most famous underground reservoir. The Cistern of Philoxenos is usually described as the second largest. Because it is less well known, it is often quieter. That quiet is worth noticing. Stop for a moment and listen. You may hear water dripping somewhere, or the low echo of a voice across the room. Without crowds, it is easier to feel how big this space really is.

Before you climb back up into the daylight, take one more look down a single row of columns. Follow it with your eyes until the stone fades into the dark. Think about the many hands that cut this marble, carried it here, and stacked it piece on piece. Think about the water that once filled this room in silence. Then walk up the steps, and feel the warm air of the street return to your face.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early in the day for a quieter underground experience.
Bring a light layer because the air feels cool below.
Check ahead in case a private event closes the space.
Combine your visit with a walk to the nearby Hippodrome.
Watch your footing on steps that can feel damp.
