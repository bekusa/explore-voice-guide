The guide

The story

Listen first. Trams rattle along the avenue behind you. Ferries call out from the Bosphorus nearby. Gulls cry above the waterfront at Tophane. Then you step closer, and the noise of the city seems to fall away. Pale stone rises in front of you, smooth and carved, almost soft in the light. Two thin minarets climb high above the dome, so slender they look like pencils against the sky. This is Nusretiye Mosque, and it does not look like the older mosques you may have seen across the water.

The name gives the first clue. Nusret is a word of Arabic origin. It means victory, or more exactly, divine help that brings victory. So this is, in a sense, a mosque of triumph. But the victory it marks was not won against a foreign army. It was won inside the empire itself, in the streets of this city. To understand the building, you need to know about a sultan and the soldiers he decided to destroy.

The sultan was Mahmud the Second. He ruled the Ottoman Empire in the early nineteenth century, at a difficult time. The empire was losing ground to European powers. Mahmud believed it had to modernise its army, or it would not survive. The great obstacle was the Janissaries. They had once been the elite infantry of the empire, feared across Europe. By Mahmud's time, they had become a powerful and conservative force. They had removed sultans before, and they resisted every reform.

In 1826, the conflict came to a head. The Janissaries revolted against a new military order. Mahmud was ready. Loyal troops and artillery turned on the Janissary barracks, and the corps was destroyed in a matter of hours. Ottoman historians later called this the Auspicious Incident. Thousands died, and an institution several centuries old disappeared almost overnight. The mosque in front of you was being built at that moment. When it was finished, Mahmud gave it its name — a monument to that bloody victory.

Now look at the place where it stands. This district is Tophane, which means the cannon house. For centuries, the Ottoman state cast its cannons in a large foundry here, close to the shore. The artillerymen were among the troops who stood with the sultan against the Janissaries. So the location is not an accident. The victory mosque rises beside the very arm of the military that helped win the fight. Here, the building and the neighbourhood tell one story together.

The architect came from a remarkable family. Krikor Balyan was an Ottoman Armenian, and the Balyans served the palace as builders for several generations. Later members of the family designed some of the great waterfront palaces of the nineteenth century along the Bosphorus. Nusretiye is one of their earlier major works. It is worth pausing on this. A mosque celebrating a Muslim sultan's victory was designed by a Christian Armenian architect. In the Ottoman capital, that was not unusual at all.

Let your eyes move over the stone. You will not find the heavy, calm geometry of the classical mosques of the sixteenth century. Instead, you see curves, garlands, scrolls and carved details that feel close to European design. Historians often call this style Ottoman Baroque, or speak of the influence of the French Empire style. Baroque, put simply, was a European fashion for movement, curves and rich decoration. Ottoman builders adopted these forms and mixed them with their own traditions. The result is something in between.

The minarets deserve a second look. They are unusually thin and tall for the size of the mosque. Each one has a balcony, where in earlier days a muezzin climbed to give the call to prayer. Today loudspeakers do that work. Look at the fluting on the shafts, the vertical grooves that catch the light. From certain angles along the street, the minarets seem to stretch the whole building upward. That sense of height and lightness was part of the new taste of the age.

The complex was not only a place of prayer. Like many Ottoman mosques, it gathered smaller buildings around it that served the public. One of these, according to descriptions of the site, was a timekeeper's house. A trained timekeeper used instruments and tables to set the exact hours of prayer. In a city without clocks on every wall, this was important work. Small details like this show how a mosque organised daily life for the whole neighbourhood, not just for worshippers.

If you step inside, take a moment as your eyes adjust. The prayer hall is tall and bright, lit by many windows. The decoration continues the playful curves of the exterior. Look at the marble, the painted details, and the lines of the gallery above. Remember that this is an active mosque. People still come here to pray five times a day. Remove your shoes at the entrance, dress modestly, and keep your voice low. Women should cover their hair before going in.

The mosque has also lived through hard times. Over nearly two centuries, salt air from the sea, traffic and earthquakes all left their mark on the stone. In the twenty-first century, the building went through a long restoration. Workers cleaned and repaired the carved surfaces, which had grown dark with dirt. That is one reason the facade looks so pale today. When you look closely, you may notice that some stones are brighter than others. That is the work of repair.

Walk a few steps in any direction, and the layers of Tophane open up. A short distance away stands the Kılıç Ali Paşa Mosque, built centuries earlier by the great architect Sinan. Near the tram line, you can find an ornate eighteenth-century fountain. The waterfront has changed too, with new museums and cafés where warehouses once stood. Within a few hundred metres, you can see Ottoman power, Ottoman craft, and the modern city sitting side by side. The old and the new meet here.

Before you leave, stand back again and look up at those minarets. Think of the year this mosque was finished. An old order had just been swept away, and a sultan was trying to build a new state. This building was his statement in stone — confident, modern for its day, and looking toward Europe. Now listen once more to the trams and the ferries. The city kept changing after Mahmud... and this pale mosque has watched all of it from the shore.

Good to know

Key facts

Commissioned by Sultan Mahmud the Second in the 1820s.
Named for victory after the Janissaries were abolished in 1826.
Designed by Krikor Balyan, an Ottoman Armenian architect.
Built in the Ottoman Baroque style with European influence.
Stands in Tophane, once home to the imperial cannon foundry.
