The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Students' voices drift across the paving stones, pigeons beat their wings near the mosque, and the traffic of Beyazıt Square hums somewhere behind you. Then look up. Above the trees of Istanbul University rises a slim, pale stone shaft, square at the base and stacked with lighter, windowed storeys at the top. It looks almost too delicate for its height. This is Beyazıt Tower, and for generations it was one of the most important lookouts in the city.

Its job was simple, and it was urgent. Old Istanbul was a city of wooden houses, packed close together along narrow lanes. A single spark from a kitchen or a lamp could spread fast, street by street. Fire was the great fear of this city for centuries. So the authorities built towers on high ground, and they staffed them day and night. Watchmen scanned the rooftops for smoke. From up there, a few minutes of warning could save whole neighbourhoods.

The tower you see today is not the first one on this spot. Earlier watchtowers here were made of wood, and wood, of course, burns. The stone tower was built in 1828, during the reign of Sultan Mahmud the Second. The design is usually credited to Senekerim Balyan, a member of the Armenian Balyan family. Over the nineteenth century, that family designed many of the palaces and mosques that shape the Istanbul skyline today. Here, the brief was practical: build something tall, and build something that cannot catch fire.

The timing matters too. Only two years earlier, in 1826, Mahmud the Second had abolished the Janissaries — the old elite infantry corps of the Ottoman Empire. Their power had grown large, and the sultan ended it by force. The Janissaries had played a role in firefighting, and an older wooden tower in this area was lost in the upheaval of that time. So this stone tower belongs to a moment of deep change. A new order wanted new institutions, and it wanted them made to last.

Now follow the tower upward with your eyes. Notice how the lower shaft is plain and solid. Then, near the top, the style changes. The upper sections are lighter, with more windows and decorative details. These upper storeys were added in 1849, under Sultan Abdülmecid. They gave the watchmen more room and a wider view. The result is a tower with two personalities: a sober stone body, and a crown that feels almost like a small pavilion floating above the city.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. Spotting a fire was only half the task. The watchmen also had to tell the city where it was. Accounts describe signal baskets hung from the tower by day, and coloured lamps at night. The number and position of the signals showed which district was burning. Below, people could read the message from far away. Runners also carried the news through the streets, calling out the location so that firefighters knew where to go.

The tower was not alone. Across the Golden Horn — the long inlet of water that divides the old city from the districts to the north — stands Galata Tower. The two towers watched the city together, each covering its own side of the water. If you have already climbed Galata, you have stood at the other end of this conversation. On a clear day, from the right spots on the hills, you can sometimes pick out both towers in the same view.

In the twentieth century, the role of the tower slowly shifted. Modern fire services, telephones and radio made lookouts less necessary. According to many accounts, the lights at the top were later used for weather signals, showing simple forecasts to the people below. Today the tower is a monument more than a working station. But it still carries the shape of its old purpose. Every window at the top faces outward, toward rooftops that once needed watching.

Now take in the ground under your feet. The tower stands inside the main campus of Istanbul University. This land has a long story of its own. After the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453, Sultan Mehmed the Second built his first palace on this hill. It became known as the Old Palace, once the newer Topkapı Palace rose near the tip of the peninsula. Later, in the nineteenth century, the site housed the Ottoman Ministry of War. The grand gateway on the square dates from that period.

So when you stand here, you are standing on several layers at once. A conquering sultan's first residence. A military headquarters. A university where generations of Turkish scholars and students have walked. And above it all, a fire tower that watched over every change. The tower is usually closed to the public, so most people experience it from the ground. That is not a loss. Its true scale only makes sense when you look up from below, the way the city always did.

If you want a good view, walk a little way around the campus grounds and watch how the tower lines up with the trees and the old buildings. Late afternoon light warms the pale stone and makes the carved details at the top easier to see. Outside the campus, the square leads to the Beyazıt Mosque, one of the oldest surviving imperial mosques in the city. Close by, a gate opens into the old book market, and a few steps further lies the Grand Bazaar.

Think for a moment about the people who climbed this tower. Night after night, a watchman went up the long spiral stair inside. He stood at the top in wind, rain and summer heat. Below him, thousands of families slept in houses made of timber. He could not stop a fire himself. He could only see it first, and raise the alarm. It was quiet, lonely, important work, and the city depended on it for generations.

Before you move on, look up one more time. Notice the windows at the top, now empty of lamps and baskets. Notice the pigeons circling, and the students passing below without a glance. The tower still faces every direction at once, as if it has not quite stopped watching. And as you walk toward the bazaar or the mosque, you might find yourself checking the rooftops too — just for a second — the way people here once did every day.

Before you go

Practical tips

Expect to view the tower from the ground only.
Come in late afternoon for warm light on the stone.
Walk around the campus grounds for cleaner sightlines.
Pair your visit with the nearby Grand Bazaar.
Carry ID in case the university gate checks visitors.
