The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of Taksim Square and listen for a moment. Tram bells, the hiss of a simit seller's cart, footsteps crossing pale stone in every direction. Then, five times a day, a new sound rises over all of it — the call to prayer from the minarets of Taksim Mosque. For most of the city's modern history, this square had no voice like that at all. The building in front of you is very young, but the argument behind it is old.

Taksim Mosque opened for worship in 2021, after several years of construction. That makes it one of the newest major landmarks in a city where some buildings count their age in centuries, even millennia. Yet the idea of a mosque on this spot was debated for decades before the first stone went in. Plans came and went across the twentieth century. Each time, the proposal raised strong feelings about what this square should mean, and who it should belong to.

To understand why, it helps to know what Taksim Square is. This is not an old Ottoman marketplace or a palace forecourt. It grew into a public square in the early years of the Turkish Republic, and it became the symbolic heart of modern, secular Istanbul. Look across the square and you will see the Republic Monument, unveiled in the late 1920s. It shows Atatürk and the founders of the Republic. Rallies, celebrations, and protests have gathered around it for generations.

So a mosque here was never only a question of architecture. For some residents, it was a long-overdue place of prayer in one of the busiest corners of the city. For others, it changed the character of a square built to represent the new republic. You do not need to take a side to feel the weight of that history. You simply need to notice how the mosque, the monument, and the cultural centre all share the same open ground.

Now look at the building itself. The design follows the classical Ottoman pattern that Istanbul knows so well — a central dome, smaller half-domes and cupolas stepping down around it, and tall, slender minarets. This is the language of the great imperial mosques on the historic peninsula. But the details here are cleaner and sharper. The stone is pale and even. The lines feel crisp, almost graphic, in the way that only a newly finished building can.

Here is something many visitors miss. The mosque does not sit flat on the square. Taksim rises and falls, and the building was set into this slope with care. Walk around its edges and you will notice terraces, steps, and changes in level. These help a large structure fit into a crowded, uneven corner of the city. From some angles it seems to rise straight out of the pavement. From others, it seems to rest quietly behind the street.

If you step inside, remove your shoes at the entrance and move gently. Visitors are welcome outside prayer times, and modest dress is expected — shoulders and knees covered, and a headscarf for women. Under the dome, the noise of the square drops away almost at once. Soft carpet takes the place of hard stone. Light falls from rows of windows high in the walls. The space feels open and calm, and the contrast with the square outside is part of the experience.

Look up at the inside of the dome. In the Ottoman tradition, this surface often carries calligraphy — verses written in elegant Arabic script, arranged in circles and bands. Calligraphy holds a special place in Islamic art, because it gives visual form to sacred words without using images of people. Even if you cannot read the script, you can follow the rhythm of the letters, the long sweeping strokes and the tight, careful curves.

Now for a second surprise, and it hides in the name of the square itself. The word taksim means division or distribution in Turkish. It comes from an old stone water building that still stands near the top of the square. In the eighteenth century, water arrived here from reservoirs north of the city. At this point it was divided and sent down through pipes to different neighbourhoods. The whole square is named after a piece of water infrastructure.

Once you know that, the square looks a little different. Look for the small, solid stone structure near the head of Istiklal Avenue. It is easy to walk past, especially with a large new mosque so close. But that modest building is the reason this area has its name. Old and new stand within a short walk of each other here. That closeness is one of the most interesting things about this corner of Istanbul.

Turn slowly and take in the wider scene. On one side stands the Atatürk Cultural Centre, a large, modern performing arts building that reopened in recent years. Behind you, the trees of Gezi Park offer shade and benches. Down the hill, Istiklal Avenue begins its long pedestrian walk toward Galata, past shops, cinemas, and nineteenth-century apartment buildings. Not far away, the domes of the Greek Orthodox Church of Hagia Triada also rise among the rooftops of Beyoğlu.

That mix tells you a lot about this district. Beyoğlu has long been a place where communities lived side by side — Muslim, Greek, Armenian, Jewish, and European residents, each with their own churches, schools, and shops. The new mosque adds another layer to that story. Whatever you think about the decision to build it, it now belongs to the everyday life of the square. People pray here, meet friends on the steps, and wait for the tram nearby.

Before you move on, stand still for a minute near the mosque's edge. Watch how people use the space around you. Some walk quickly toward the metro. Some pause to photograph the dome against the sky. Others sit on low walls with tea or a phone in hand. If the call to prayer begins, notice how the square responds — some people turn toward the doors, and others simply keep walking.

As you leave, look back once more. The pale dome, the thin minarets, the monument, the trees of the park — they all sit together in one frame. This is a square that has never stopped changing, and the mosque is its newest chapter. Then let the sound of the tram bell pull you down Istiklal, into the older streets of the city, carrying this view with you.
