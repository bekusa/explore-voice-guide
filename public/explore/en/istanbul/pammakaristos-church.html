The guide

The story

Listen first. A car horn somewhere down the hill, pigeons on a ledge, a shopkeeper pulling up a metal shutter. The streets of this quiet corner of Fatih climb and fold, and then a wall of warm brick and pale stone appears between the houses. Stripes of red and cream run around it like layers in a cake. This is the Pammakaristos Church, and for more than eight hundred years it has stood on this slope above the Golden Horn.

The name is Greek. Pammakaristos means something close to all-blessed, or most joyful, and it was a title for the Virgin Mary. The building began as a Byzantine monastery church. Its founding is commonly linked to a nobleman named John Komnenos and his wife Anna Doukaina, probably in the twelfth century. Some details of that early story are debated by historians. What is clear is that this was a church of the city's aristocracy, built with care and money.

Now look at the walls themselves. Byzantine builders liked to alternate courses of brick with bands of dressed stone. The pattern is decorative, but it was also practical. The thin brick layers, set in thick mortar, helped the walls flex during earthquakes. You can also see niches, blind arches, and small patterns made from brick laid at angles. Up close, the mortar shows centuries of patching. Each repair belongs to a different hand and a different century.

The most precious part of the complex sits on the south side. It is a small side chapel, which scholars call the parekklesion. It was added in the early fourteenth century by a woman named Maria, the widow of a Byzantine general, Michael Glabas Tarchaneiotes. She built it as a funerary chapel in his memory. After his death, she became a nun and took the name Martha. So this chapel began as an act of grief.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Run your eyes along the outside of that chapel, high up near the cornice. A long inscription runs across the stone. It is a poem by Manuel Philes, a court poet of the time, written in honour of the general and his widow. Few churches anywhere wear a poem on the outside like a ribbon. Even if you cannot read the old Greek letters, you can follow the line with your eyes.

Then step inside, if the chapel is open. The space is small, almost intimate, and the light falls from windows around a narrow dome. Above you, Christ looks down from the centre of that dome. This image is called the Pantokrator, which means ruler of all. Around him stand twelve prophets of the Old Testament, each one holding a scroll. The gold background catches whatever light the day allows, so the figures seem to change colour hour by hour.

In the apse, the curved wall at the eastern end, there is another mosaic group. Christ sits at the centre, with the Virgin Mary on one side and John the Baptist on the other, both turned towards him in prayer. Art historians call this arrangement a Deesis, from the Greek word for supplication. Elsewhere in the chapel you can find the Baptism of Christ in the river Jordan. Look for the water, shown in soft blue and green tesserae, the tiny cubes of glass and stone.

These mosaics belong to a late flowering of Byzantine art. By the early fourteenth century the empire was smaller and poorer than before. Yet its artists were making some of their most graceful work. Faces became softer and more human, and bodies moved with more ease. If you have visited the Chora, another Byzantine church a short distance away in Fatih, you will recognise the same spirit. These two buildings come from the same generation of patrons and craftsmen.

Now comes the first great turn in the story. In 1453, Ottoman forces under Sultan Mehmed the Second took Constantinople. A few years later, this church became the seat of the Greek Orthodox Patriarchate, the leading office of the Orthodox Church. For well over a century, the patriarchs worked from here. Popular tradition says the Sultan came here to discuss Christian belief with the Patriarch Gennadios. The story is widely repeated, though the details are hard to confirm.

The second turn came at the end of the sixteenth century. The Patriarchate moved away, and under Sultan Murad the Third the main church became a mosque. It was given a new name, Fethiye, which means conquest. The name celebrated Ottoman military victories in the Caucasus, including campaigns against Georgia. So this one building holds layers of very different memories. Builders removed interior walls and added a mihrab, the niche that shows the direction of Mecca.

That is why the complex today has two lives side by side. The main body is still a working mosque, with prayer rugs, quiet worshippers, and the call to prayer from the minaret. The funerary chapel, with its mosaics, operates as a museum space. In the twentieth century, American and Turkish specialists worked on cleaning and conserving the mosaics. Much of what you can see today survived under layers of plaster and centuries of dust.

Take a moment in the surrounding streets as well. This part of Fatih is residential and conservative. You will see women in long coats carrying shopping bags, children running to school, and old men drinking tea on low stools. Laundry hangs from windows. Few tour buses come this far. That makes the visit feel less like a monument stop and more like finding something among ordinary daily life.

Before you leave, walk once around the outside and look at the building from a little distance. Notice how the domes sit at different heights, and how the minaret rises from one corner. Brick, stone, gold, and the slim white minaret all share the same small plot of ground. Then listen again to the street, the pigeons, the shutters, the voices... and carry that quiet mix with you down the hill.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly with shoulders and knees covered for the mosque.
Avoid visiting the mosque during the five daily prayers.
Check whether the mosaic chapel needs a separate museum entry.
Combine your visit with the nearby Chora church on foot.
Come on a bright day for the best mosaic light.
