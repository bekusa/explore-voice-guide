The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. Up here on Çamlıca Hill, the air moves differently than it does down by the water. It carries the smell of pine from the surrounding park, the faint hum of traffic far below, and sometimes the cry of gulls drifting up from the Bosphorus. Ahead of you, pale stone rises in tiers, dome upon dome, toward a cluster of slender towers. This is the Çamlıca Mosque, and it sits on one of the highest points in all of Istanbul.

Let's start with where you are. Üsküdar is on the Asian side of the city, across the Bosphorus strait from the old imperial heart. For centuries, Çamlıca Hill has been a place where Istanbul people came to breathe. They came for picnics, for tea, for the view. The name itself comes from the Turkish word for pine. So before there was a mosque here, there was a hilltop of trees and open sky. That older character still survives in the gardens around you.

Now look up at the minarets. Count them... there are six. That number matters. In the Ottoman tradition, six minarets were rare and carried great prestige. Only a few imperial mosques in Istanbul have them, including the Blue Mosque on the European side. By choosing six, the builders placed this mosque in conversation with the grandest buildings of the city's past. It is a statement written in stone against the sky.

The mosque is young. It opened for worship in 2019, after several years of construction. That surprises many visitors, because the design looks so classical. It follows the Ottoman style of the sixteenth century. In that style, a large central dome sits above a cascade of smaller half-domes and domes, so the whole building seems to flow upward like a hill of its own. You are looking at a modern building wearing very old clothes.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. The mosque was designed by two women, the architects Bahar Mızrak and Hayriye Gül Totu. In a country where mosque design has long been a male profession, that was notable. The project also drew debate. Some critics felt a new building should look new, not copy the past. Others felt the classical form was exactly right for a hill that faces the old city. You can decide for yourself as you walk around.

The building is full of numbers with meaning. The main dome is often described as seventy-two metres high, a nod to a traditional saying about the many peoples who live in Istanbul. Its diameter is often given as thirty-four metres, and thirty-four is the city's licence plate code. The tallest minarets are said to rise just over one hundred and seven metres, recalling 1071, the year of the Battle of Manzikert. That battle opened Anatolia to the Turks. The architecture here carries a lot of history in its measurements.

Before you go inside, spend a moment in the courtyard. Ottoman mosques traditionally have a forecourt surrounded by a covered arcade. In the centre stands a fountain where worshippers wash before prayer. The ritual washing is called ablution, and it prepares the body and mind for worship. Listen for the sound of running water. Watch how people move here: slowly, quietly, shoes in hand. The courtyard is a pause between the city and the prayer hall.

Now step inside, and let your eyes adjust. The prayer hall is vast. This is one of the largest mosques in Turkey, built to hold tens of thousands of worshippers. Thick carpet runs under your feet, soft and warm after the stone outside. Light falls from rows of windows set high in the walls and around the base of the dome. On a bright day, the space feels almost weightless. The dome seems to float above you.

Look closely at the surfaces. Calligraphy runs along the walls and the dome. These are verses from the Quran and religious phrases, written in flowing Arabic script. In Islamic art, calligraphy takes the place that painted figures hold in many churches. The writing itself is the decoration. You will also see tilework, carved stone, and painted patterns in blues, reds, and gold. Each pattern repeats and interlocks, a geometry meant to suggest the infinite.

Find the mihrab. This is the niche in the wall that shows the direction of Mecca, the direction worshippers face in prayer. Beside it rises the minbar, a tall staircase pulpit used for the Friday sermon. Then look up at the great chandelier hanging beneath the dome. In older Ottoman mosques, low chandeliers held oil lamps. Here the idea continues with electric light, but the form still draws your eye to the centre of the room.

The mosque is more than a prayer hall. It is a complex, in the old Ottoman sense. Historic mosques often stood among schools, kitchens, and libraries that served the community. This one follows that model with a museum, an art gallery, a library, and a conference hall. The museum focuses on Turkish and Islamic arts. If you have time, it is worth exploring. It gives the building a life beyond the hours of prayer.

When you step back outside, walk to the edge of the grounds and look out. On a clear day, the city opens below you. You can see the Bosphorus bridges, the curve of the strait, and the domes and minarets of the old peninsula across the water. Nearby on the hill stands a tall modern broadcasting tower, a very different kind of landmark. Between the two, you can see two faces of Istanbul at once: the devout and the technical, the old form and the new.

Five times a day, the call to prayer rises from these minarets. If you are here when it begins, stop and listen. The voice rolls out over the hill and down toward the water. Other mosques across Üsküdar answer, one after another, until the sound seems to come from everywhere. It is one of the oldest rhythms of this city, and it still sets the pace of the day for many people here.

Before you leave, turn around once more. Look at how the domes step down, one after another, like water spilling over stone. Feel the wind again, and smell the pines. People came to this hill long before the mosque, to sit and look at the city. They still do. Find a bench, rest a moment, and let your eyes follow the Bosphorus as it runs toward the sea.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly; women should bring a scarf to cover hair.
Remove shoes before entering the prayer hall carpet.
Avoid visiting during Friday midday prayer crowds.
Come on a clear day for long Bosphorus views.
Save time for the museum of Turkish and Islamic arts.
