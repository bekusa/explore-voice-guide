The guide

The story

Listen first. Behind you, cars hum along the coastal road. Somewhere close, a commuter train rattles past. And in front of you, a wall of old stone and brick stands against the light from the Sea of Marmara. Look up and you will see tall openings framed in white marble, like empty window frames in a stage set. Salt air moves through them. Gulls call overhead. This broken façade is one of the last visible pieces of the imperial palace of Byzantium, the Greek-speaking eastern Roman Empire that ruled from this city for more than a thousand years.

You are standing at the Boukoleon Palace. To understand it, picture a much larger world behind this wall. For centuries the Byzantine emperors lived in a vast complex called the Great Palace. It ran downhill from the area near the Hagia Sophia and the old Hippodrome, all the way to this shoreline. It held halls, chapels, gardens, courtyards and terraces. The Boukoleon was its seaward end. It was the part of the palace that faced the water and touched it directly.

The name itself carries a small story. Boukoleon comes from two Greek words: bous, meaning ox or bull, and leon, meaning lion. Byzantine writers describe a sculpture near the palace harbour showing a lion attacking a bull. Over time, the name of the statue passed to the harbour, and then to the palace above it. The statue is long gone. But every time someone says the name, they are still describing that frozen fight in stone.

Now think about the water in front of you. In the Byzantine period, the shoreline sat close to this wall, and the palace had its own small private harbour. This mattered. An emperor could step from his rooms onto a boat without crossing the crowded city streets. He could greet guests arriving by sea, or leave quietly if danger came. Today the coastline has moved outward, filled in by later construction, and the road runs where boats once tied up.

The palace grew and changed over many generations. Different emperors added rooms, repaired walls and strengthened defences. In the ninth century, the emperor Theophilos is associated with building work along this stretch of the palace. In the tenth century, the soldier-emperor Nikephoros the Second Phokas fortified the Boukoleon with stronger walls. He wanted a safe residence, protected from rivals inside the city as well as enemies outside it.

That protection did not save him. In the winter of 969, Nikephoros was murdered inside the palace he had fortified. The plot involved his own wife, the empress Theophano, and the general John Tzimiskes, who then took the throne. Byzantine chroniclers tell that the conspirators reached the emperor's rooms from the sea side, at night. So these walls, built for safety, became the setting for one of the most famous palace murders in Byzantine history.

Look again at those marble-framed openings. They tell you something about how the palace was used. These were not narrow slits for archers. They are wide, generous openings, made for light, air and views across the water. From inside, an emperor would have seen ships on the Marmara, the Asian shore in the distance, and the islands on a clear day. Above the openings, notice the brick bands set between rows of stone. This alternating pattern is typical of Byzantine building.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Several large lion sculptures are believed to have come from this palace area. Today some of them are in the collection of the Istanbul Archaeology Museums, a short walk uphill. So if you visit that museum later, look for the lions. They may once have watched over this harbour, guarding the sea entrance of an emperor's home.

In 1204, the palace saw another turning point. Soldiers of the Fourth Crusade, Western European knights who were meant to sail to the Holy Land, attacked Constantinople instead. They captured the city and looted it. One of the crusader leaders, Boniface of Montferrat, took control of the Boukoleon. For the following decades, the Latin emperors, the Western rulers installed by the crusade, used parts of the Great Palace as their residence.

When the Byzantines retook the city in the thirteenth century, the old palace was already in poor condition. The emperors moved their main residence to the Blachernae palace, in the northwest of the city near the land walls. The Great Palace, and the Boukoleon with it, slowly fell out of use. Stone was taken for other buildings. Gardens disappeared. Houses grew up over the ruins. By the time the Ottomans conquered the city in 1453, much of the old imperial complex was already broken.

The second surprise sits right beside you. That railway line, so close to the wall, was built in the late nineteenth century. Its construction cut through parts of the old palace and the sea walls. Later, the coastal road added another layer of damage and noise. So the ruin you see today is not only the work of time. It is also the work of modern transport, laid straight through the emperor's former rooms. In recent years, the city has carried out restoration and conservation work on the site.

Take a moment to walk a little along the wall, if the path allows. You will notice how the palace blends into the sea walls of the city. These walls once ran along the whole Marmara shore, protecting Constantinople from attack by sea. In places, you can still see fragments of carved marble, re-used blocks, and patches of later repair. Each patch shows a different hand and a different century. It is a wall built, broken and mended many times.

Now come back to the openings and stand still for a moment. Behind you, modern Istanbul moves fast: cars, trains, fishermen with long rods on the shore. In front of you, the same water that carried emperors, crusaders and merchants still catches the afternoon light. As you walk away, look back once more. Imagine boats tying up below these windows... a lion and a bull locked together in stone... and an emperor looking out to sea, believing these walls would keep him safe.
