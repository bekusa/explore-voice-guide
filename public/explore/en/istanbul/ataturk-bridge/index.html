The guide

The story

Feel the deck under your feet for a moment. Each time a bus or a heavy truck rolls past, a faint tremor runs up through the soles of your shoes. The wind comes off the water carrying salt, diesel and a little of the sea. Gulls turn above the railings, calling over the steady hum of traffic. You are standing over the Golden Horn, and on either side of you, Istanbul climbs its hills.

This is the Atatürk Bridge. Many locals simply call it the Unkapanı Bridge, after the district at its southern end. It links the old historic peninsula, the heart of the former Ottoman capital, with the Beyoğlu side to the north. It is not the most photographed bridge in the city. That honour usually goes to the Galata Bridge, a little further toward the sea. But this crossing has its own long story.

First, the water beneath you. The Golden Horn is a long, curving inlet. It branches off the Bosphorus, the strait that divides Europe from Asia. Its shape on a map really does look like a horn. For centuries this sheltered water was one of the finest natural harbours in the Mediterranean world. Byzantine fleets anchored here, and later Ottoman ones. Shipyards, warehouses and docks once lined both shores where you now see roads and parks.

Now look south, toward the old city. The neighbourhood at the foot of the bridge is Unkapanı. The name comes from Turkish words meaning roughly the flour weighing place. In Ottoman times, grain and flour arrived here by boat. Officials weighed it and controlled its sale, so that the capital would not go hungry. Today the old trade is gone, but the name still carries that memory of sacks, scales and busy quays.

Above Unkapanı, raise your eyes to the ridge. The large mosque with its cluster of domes and slender minarets is the Süleymaniye. It was built in the sixteenth century for Sultan Süleyman, the ruler Europeans called the Magnificent. Its architect was Mimar Sinan, the most celebrated builder of the Ottoman age. From this bridge you see the mosque the way sailors once saw it, sitting high above the harbour.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The road leaving the bridge to the south is Atatürk Boulevard. Follow it with your eyes up the valley. A short distance ahead, it passes beneath the tall stone arches of the Valens Aqueduct. That aqueduct was built in the Roman period to bring water into the city. Modern traffic still drives under it every day.

People have crossed the water at this spot for a long time. In the early nineteenth century, under Sultan Mahmud the Second, a wooden bridge was built here between Unkapanı and Azapkapı. It was known as the Hayratiye Bridge. Over the following decades, newer bridges replaced older ones, as wood weathered and traffic grew. The city kept returning to this same narrow point between the two shores.

The bridge you are standing on belongs to a later era. It was built in the late 1930s and opened in 1940, in the early years of the Turkish Republic. It was named after Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, the founder of the Republic. In those years, Istanbul was no longer the capital. Ankara had taken that role. Even so, the young state invested in roads and crossings to connect the old city with its growing districts.

Turn now toward the northern shore. That end of the bridge lands at Azapkapı. The name means the gate of the azaps. Azaps were a class of Ottoman soldiers, and some served as marines on the fleet. A gate in the old Galata walls once stood near here. Close to the bridge foot, you may spot a small, graceful mosque. That is the Sokollu Mehmed Pasha Mosque of Azapkapı, another work by Sinan.

That little mosque is a second surprise. Most people rush past it in a car without noticing it at all. Yet it was designed by the same architect who built the great Süleymaniye on the opposite hill. So from the middle of this bridge, you can see two works by one master, one on each shore. One is grand and imperial. The other is modest and close to the water.

Look downstream, toward the Bosphorus. The nearest crossing is the Golden Horn Metro Bridge, with its tall white masts and cables. It opened in the 2010s and carries trains between the two sides. Beyond it lies the Galata Bridge, low and busy. On the hill to the left rises the round stone Galata Tower, with its pointed cap. On clear days the view stretches toward the mouth of the inlet.

Now look upstream, away from the sea. The Golden Horn narrows and bends inland. Both banks hold a mix of old neighbourhoods, newer parks and the remains of industry. For much of the twentieth century these shores were known for factories and polluted water. Large clean-up efforts later changed that. Today you may see rowers, small ferries and birds working the surface of the water.

If you walk across, take your time. The pavements give you space, but the traffic beside you is fast and loud. Stop in the middle, where the breeze is strongest. Listen for the call to prayer if your timing is right. It rises from one minaret, then another, then many at once across both hills. The sound travels well over open water.

Before you move on, look once more at the city around you. Domes and minarets on one shore. Towers, steep streets and apartment blocks on the other. Water, gulls and the steady crossing of people in between. This bridge is not a monument you visit for its own sake. It is a working line between two halves of Istanbul. As you step off, notice how the noise changes, and how quickly the old streets close around you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk to the middle of the deck for the widest views.
Keep to the pavement; traffic beside you moves very fast.
Bring a windproof layer; the breeze over the water is strong.
Combine your walk with a visit to Süleymaniye Mosque.
Time your crossing for a call to prayer to hear it echo.
