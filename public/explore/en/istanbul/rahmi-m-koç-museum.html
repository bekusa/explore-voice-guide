The guide

The story

The air smells faintly of machine oil and old wood. You are standing inside one of Istanbul's most unusual museums — a sprawling industrial archive housed in a restored Ottoman foundry and shipyard on the northern shore of the Golden Horn. The building itself is part of the collection: brick arches, iron beams, and tall windows that once lit the workbenches of craftsmen casting anchors and repairing steamships in the late eighteen-hundreds.

Rahmi M. Koç, one of Turkey's leading industrialists, opened this museum in 1994 to preserve the country's engineering and transport heritage. He spent decades collecting machines, vehicles, and scientific instruments — not as static relics, but as working examples of human ingenuity. Many of the exhibits here still turn, hum, or roll. This is not a museum where you tiptoe past roped-off artifacts. It is a place where you can climb into a tram, peer into a submarine, and watch a steam engine fire up on weekends.

The collection spans three centuries. You will find Ottoman-era navigational tools, early Turkish automobiles, American locomotives, British double-decker buses, and a full-size replica of a nineteenth-century pharmacy. One gallery holds delicate scientific instruments — brass telescopes, wooden theodolites, early calculating machines — arranged like jewellery. Another hall is dominated by a massive Krupp steam engine, its flywheel taller than a person, restored to gleaming black and red.

Outside, along the waterfront, sits the Fenerbahçe, a retired passenger ferry that once shuttled commuters across the Bosphorus. You can board her, walk the decks, and step into the engine room where twin diesels still rest in their mounts. Nearby, a German-built submarine from the nineteen-forties lies open for exploration — narrow bunks, periscope, torpedo tubes. The contrast between the cramped steel interior and the wide blue water beyond is quietly startling.

The museum also holds one of the world's finest collections of model ships — hundreds of miniature vessels, each rigged and painted with obsessive care. Some were built by prisoners in the nineteenth century, others by naval engineers as presentation pieces. Look closely at the rigging on a three-masted frigate and you will see individual knots tied in thread finer than human hair.

Koç's philosophy was simple: technology is culture. He believed that a society's tools — its trains, its looms, its printing presses — tell the story of how people lived, worked, and imagined the future. Walking through these halls, you are not just looking at old machines. You are tracing the arc of modernization in Turkey, from the Tanzimat reforms of the eighteen-fifties through the Republic's industrial ambitions in the mid-twentieth century.

The café overlooks the water, housed in a glass pavilion that feels more like a greenhouse than a museum annex. From here you can watch ferries slide past, their wakes catching the light. The museum gardens are planted with plane trees and benches, a quiet place to sit after an hour of iron and brass.

One gallery is devoted entirely to toys — tin cars, wind-up trains, mechanical dolls. It is easy to walk past this section quickly, but pause for a moment. These toys were the first machines most children ever touched. They taught a generation what motion looked like, what gears did, how energy transferred from spring to wheel. In a way, they are the ancestors of everything else in the museum.

Before you leave, step into the olive oil factory at the far end of the complex. It is a working reconstruction of a traditional press, complete with stone wheels and wooden beams. On certain days, staff demonstrate the crushing process, and the smell of fresh oil fills the room — earthy, green, almost sweet. It is a reminder that industry is not only about steel and steam. It is also about the quiet, ancient technologies that fed empires.

As you walk back toward the entrance, the Golden Horn spreads out before you — the same water that carried Byzantine grain ships, Ottoman war galleys, and Republican ferries. The museum sits at the edge of that continuity, a place where the past is not preserved behind glass but kept turning, humming, ready to move.
