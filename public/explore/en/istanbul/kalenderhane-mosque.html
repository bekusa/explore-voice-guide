The guide

The story

Listen first. Traffic hums along the boulevard nearby, and pigeons settle on the old stone arches of the aqueduct. Then you turn a corner, and the noise drops away. In front of you stands a low building of red brick and pale stone, laid in long horizontal bands. A single dome rises above it on a ring of small windows. This is Kalenderhane Mosque, and for most of its life it was a Byzantine church.

You are in the Fatih district, the historic heart of Istanbul, the city once called Constantinople. The arches you just passed belong to the Aqueduct of Valens, a Roman water channel that still runs across this valley. The mosque sits almost in its shadow. Istanbul University is a short walk away, so students often cut through these lanes. The setting feels ordinary. That ordinariness hides one of the most surprising buildings in the old city.

Scholars generally identify this building as the Church of the Theotokos Kyriotissa. Theotokos is a Greek title for the Virgin Mary. It means the one who gave birth to God. Kyriotissa is harder to translate, and its exact meaning is still debated. The name was confirmed by a mosaic found here during excavations, which showed the Virgin with that very label. Before that discovery, the identity of the church was uncertain.

The church you see today mostly dates from the late twelfth century. That was a confident period for the Byzantine Empire, under a ruling family called the Komnenoi. But this ground was used long before that. Excavations found the remains of a Roman bath beneath the site. Later, earlier churches stood here too. So the walls in front of you rest on several layers of the city, one stacked on another, each layer built with materials from the one before.

Look closely at the outside walls. Bands of brick alternate with bands of stone. In some places the Byzantine builders used a method called the recessed brick technique. Every other row of bricks was set back and hidden behind thick mortar. From a distance, the mortar lines look unusually wide. Up close, you can sometimes see the pattern in the weathered surface. It is a quiet signature of Middle Byzantine builders in Constantinople.

Now for the first surprise. In 1204, armies of the Fourth Crusade captured Constantinople. They were Western Europeans, Latin Christians, and they ruled the city for more than half a century. During those years, this church was used by Western clergy. And on one of its walls, someone painted scenes from the life of Saint Francis of Assisi, the Italian friar who loved the poor and the natural world.

Those frescoes are remarkable for their timing. Francis was declared a saint in 1228, and the paintings here were made not long afterward. They are considered among the earliest known painted cycles of his life anywhere. Imagine it. A story from a small town in Italy was painted on the walls of a Greek church in Constantinople, within a generation of the saint's death. The fragments were carefully removed after excavation and are now kept in the collections of the Istanbul Archaeology Museums.

The second surprise is even older. Excavators also found a mosaic showing the Presentation of Christ in the Temple. It is believed to date from before the Iconoclast period. That was a time in the eighth and ninth centuries when religious images were banned and many were destroyed across the empire. Very few figurative mosaics from before that crisis survive in Istanbul. Finding one here, under later layers, was a genuine discovery.

These finds came from a long research project in the 1960s and 1970s. A team of American and Turkish scholars studied and restored the building, working stone by stone. Much of what we know about this place comes from their careful work. Without it, the Saint Francis paintings and the early mosaic might still be hidden behind plaster. It is a reminder that great discoveries do not always happen in famous monuments. Sometimes they wait in a neighbourhood mosque.

After the Ottoman conquest of the city in 1453, the church became a mosque. Its new name comes from the Kalenderi, a wandering order of dervishes. Dervishes are members of Sufi brotherhoods, the mystical tradition within Islam. According to tradition, the building was given to this group for their use, and the name stayed. The Ottomans added a minaret and a mihrab, the niche that shows the direction of Mecca. The Byzantine shell stayed largely in place.

If you are able to step inside, take a moment before you look up. The space is arranged around the central dome, with arms reaching out in the shape of a cross. Parts of the original marble wall panels survive. You may notice veined slabs in grey and soft colours, set in frames of carved moulding. The interior is calm and plain. There are no gold mosaics on the walls now. Instead, the light from the dome windows falls on old stone and modern carpet together.

This is an active mosque, so the rhythm of the day still shapes the building. At prayer times, worshippers fill the space, and visitors should wait outside. Shoes come off at the door. Women usually cover their hair. Quiet voices are welcome. Respecting these small customs is part of the experience. The building has served faith for many centuries, in two religions, and it still serves the people who live around it today.

Before you leave, walk back a few steps and look again at the dome. Notice how the brick drum holds the windows, and how the minaret stands beside a building that was never designed for one. Then turn toward the aqueduct arches behind you. Roman, Byzantine, Latin, Ottoman, and modern Istanbul all meet on this one small square. As the traffic noise returns, you carry that quiet with you, back into the living city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Avoid visiting during prayer times; wait outside if prayer starts.
Bring a scarf; women usually cover their hair inside.
Combine your visit with a walk along the Valens Aqueduct.
See the removed frescoes at the Istanbul Archaeology Museums.
Remove shoes at the entrance and keep voices low.
