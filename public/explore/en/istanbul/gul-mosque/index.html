The guide

The story

Listen first to the Golden Horn. Just beyond the old sea walls, ferries hum across the water, and gulls call over the rooftops of Ayakapı. Then look up. Above the narrow lanes rises a tall, heavy building of brick and stone. Its walls stand on a high base, and its dome sits like a cap on an older body. This is Gül Mosque, the Rose Mosque. It was not built as a mosque at all.

Before it was a mosque, this was a Byzantine church. The Byzantines were the Greek-speaking Christian empire that ruled Constantinople for more than a thousand years. Scholars generally date the building to the middle Byzantine period, roughly the eleventh or twelfth century. That makes it one of the older surviving structures along this stretch of the Golden Horn. Its exact original name, however, is still debated... and that uncertainty is part of its story.

The most popular identification links the building to the Church of Saint Theodosia. Theodosia was a nun of Constantinople. Christian tradition remembers her as a martyr from the eighth century, when the empire was torn by a fierce argument over religious images. Her shrine became a place of pilgrimage, and people came to it hoping for healing. Some historians, though, suggest this building belonged to a different monastery. Both views have serious supporters, so treat the name with a little caution.

Now for the roses. Legend has it that the feast day of Saint Theodosia fell on the twenty-ninth of May. That is the same day in 1453 when the Ottoman army entered the city. According to the popular tale, the church was full of roses for the festival when the soldiers arrived. Locals say the flowers gave the later mosque its name. It is a beautiful story — but remember, it is tradition, not a documented record.

Walk slowly around the outside. Look at the walls. You will see bands of red brick laid between rows of pale stone. This striped pattern is typical of Byzantine builders in this city. The thin bricks sit in thick beds of mortar, so the mortar lines look almost as wide as the bricks themselves. Here and there the pattern breaks. These breaks show centuries of repair, earthquake damage, and change of use.

The building's shape follows a plan called the cross-in-square. Imagine a square room with a cross drawn inside it, and a dome raised over the centre where the arms meet. This was the favourite church design of the middle Byzantine period. Inside, the arms of the cross are still easy to read, even after later changes. If you stand under the dome, the space opens around you in four directions.

After the Ottoman conquest, the building did not become a mosque straight away. Accounts suggest it was first used for practical purposes, possibly as a storehouse, given how close it stands to the waterfront. In the decades that followed, it was converted for Muslim prayer. Ottoman builders added a minaret, the slender tower used for the call to prayer. They also placed a mihrab, a niche that shows the direction of Mecca.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The dome you see today is not the original Byzantine dome. It was rebuilt in the Ottoman period, and its shape is different from what a Byzantine builder would have made. Look also at the arches. Some of them are slightly pointed, an Ottoman touch, rather than the fully rounded arches of the earlier church. You are looking at two architectural traditions sharing one body.

Inside, in one corner, there is a tomb. Muslim tradition connects it with a holy man, sometimes called Gül Baba. Over the centuries, other stories grew around the same spot. One Christian legend even claimed that the last Byzantine emperor lay buried here. No evidence supports that claim. Still, the tomb shows how one small space can carry the memories of two faiths at once.

Pay attention to the height of the building. The church stands on a raised lower level, so the main floor sits well above the street. From the eastern side, toward the Golden Horn, the walls look especially tall. The rounded apses — the curved ends that once held the altar — push outward on this side. They are one of the clearest Byzantine features still visible from outside.

This is a working mosque, and the neighbourhood around it is a living one. Children play in the lanes. Older men sit outside small shops and tea houses. Laundry hangs between windows. Ayakapı takes its name from one of the gates in the sea walls, and pieces of those old walls still stand nearby. Few large tour groups come this far, so the area feels calm and ordinary — which is exactly its charm.

If you go inside, remember the rhythm of prayer. Visitors are usually welcome outside the five daily prayer times. Remove your shoes at the entrance. Women should cover their hair, and everyone should cover shoulders and knees. Speak quietly. Look up at the dome and the pendentives, the curved triangles that carry the round dome on a square base. They are the hidden engineering that makes the whole space work.

Think about what this building has survived. It has seen the empire of Byzantium, the arrival of the Ottomans, and the modern city of Istanbul. It has known Greek prayers and Arabic prayers. It has faced earthquakes, fires, and repairs. And through all of it, the same brick walls have stood above the same stretch of water.

Before you leave, step back toward the water and turn around once more. Look at the stripes of brick and stone in the late light. Listen to the call to prayer, if it comes, rolling over the rooftops. As you walk along the old walls, notice how many layers this city keeps in plain sight — and how often a single building holds more than one story.
