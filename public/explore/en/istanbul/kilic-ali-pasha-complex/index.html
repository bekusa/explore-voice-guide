The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, trams hum along the waterfront road and ferry horns carry across from the Bosphorus. In front of you, the sound drops away. Pale grey stone walls rise above a quiet courtyard, and a single slender minaret points into the sky. The air here often smells of salt from the strait. The stones are cool to the touch, even on a warm afternoon. This is the Kılıç Ali Pasha Complex, in the old Tophane district of Istanbul.

The complex is named after one of the most surprising figures of the Ottoman sixteenth century. Kılıç Ali Pasha was not born a Turk. Historians generally agree that he came from Calabria, in the far south of Italy. As a young man, he was captured by North African corsairs. He converted to Islam, served at sea, and climbed through the ranks. In time, he became Grand Admiral of the entire Ottoman fleet.

His name tells part of his story. Kılıç means sword in Turkish, and Ali was the Muslim name he took. Before that title, he was known by other names in the Mediterranean world. Europeans called him Uluç Ali, or Occhiali, and he appears under those names in Spanish and Italian accounts. So a single man carried several identities across the sea. That mix of worlds suits this neighbourhood, where ships from many nations once anchored just a short walk away.

The defining moment of his career came in 1571, at the Battle of Lepanto, off the coast of Greece. It was a huge naval clash between the Ottomans and a Christian alliance. The Ottoman fleet suffered a heavy defeat. But the commander of one wing managed to break away and bring his ships home. That commander was the man honoured here. Back in Istanbul, he led the effort to rebuild the navy, and the Ottoman fleet returned to sea remarkably fast.

That success brought him wealth and favour. Like many powerful Ottomans, he used his fortune to build a pious foundation for the public. Such a foundation, called a külliye, was more than a mosque. It was a small community of buildings, each with a purpose. Here, the plan included a mosque for prayer, a madrasa for religious study, a public bath, a fountain, and a tomb. Together, they served both the soul and the daily needs of the neighbourhood.

To design it, the admiral turned to Mimar Sinan, the chief architect of the empire. Mimar simply means architect, and Sinan shaped much of Istanbul's skyline. By the time work here was finishing, around 1580, Sinan was already very old and near the end of a long career. Yet this building shows a different side of his work. Instead of trying something completely new, he looked back to one of the oldest giants of the city.

Here is the first surprise. Step inside the mosque, and look up. Many visitors notice that the space feels familiar. The great dome rests on a central square, supported by half-domes on two sides. Scholars often describe this mosque as a smaller echo of Hagia Sophia, the vast sixth-century church that became a mosque after 1453. Sinan seems to have studied that building closely here, at a gentler, more human scale.

Inside, the light comes through rows of windows set high in the walls and in the dome. On a clear day, it falls in soft bands across the carpet. Look for the tile panels near the prayer niche, the mihrab, which shows worshippers the direction of Mecca. The blue, white, and red tiles come from Iznik, the town that produced the finest Ottoman ceramics. Their colours stay bright against the plain stone around them. Please remember this is still an active place of prayer.

Now a second surprise, and this one belongs to legend. Locals tell a popular story about the location. According to the tale, the admiral asked the sultan for land to build on. The sultan supposedly replied that a man of the sea should build in the sea. So, the story goes, the shoreline was filled in to make space. Whether the exchange really happened is uncertain. But the coast here has certainly moved over the centuries, and the water once came much closer.

Walk around the complex and you will find the hamam, the public bath. For centuries, people came here to wash, rest, and talk. Over time, the bath fell into poor condition. In recent years, it was carefully restored and reopened as a working bath once again. Today, visitors can lie on the warm marble beneath its domes. Small glass openings in the domes let thin beams of daylight fall through the steam.

Near the mosque stands the founder's tomb, a modest domed structure. The admiral was buried here when he died, close to the building he paid for. Ottoman founders often chose this arrangement. It kept their name tied to the good work they had funded, and passers-by would remember them in their prayers. Notice how the tomb sits in a quiet corner of the garden. It is a small, still place among the noise of the modern road.

The district around you has its own story. The name Tophane means cannon house. For centuries, the Ottoman state cast its cannons in a foundry nearby. So this corner of the city connected the army and the navy, the forge and the fleet. Today, the area mixes old mosques, fountains, art galleries, and small cafes. Older men play backgammon at tea tables, and the click of the pieces carries along the side streets.

Before you leave, stand in the courtyard once more. Look at the minaret rising against the sky, and at the lead-grey domes stepping down toward the street. Feel the worn stone under your feet, polished by more than four hundred years of footsteps. Then turn toward the water. Somewhere beyond the traffic, ships still move through the strait, much as they did when an Italian-born admiral rebuilt an empire's fleet... and chose to leave this quiet place behind.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly and cover shoulders before entering the mosque.
Avoid visiting during the five daily prayer times.
Book the restored hamam ahead if you want to bathe.
Take the tram to the Tophane stop on the waterfront.
Combine your visit with nearby Nusretiye Mosque and Tophane fountain.
