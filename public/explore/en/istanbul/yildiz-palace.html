The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of Beşiktaş is still down there, somewhere below the trees. Up here it softens into a low hum. You hear gravel under your shoes, birds in the plane trees, and the wind moving through a slope of old woodland. The air is cooler than on the waterfront. It smells of damp leaves and cut grass. This is not the kind of palace that greets you with one grand façade. It opens slowly, one path at a time.

Yıldız means "star" in Turkish. The name fits a place that sits high above the Bosphorus, looking down over the water toward the Asian shore. Yıldız Palace is not a single building. It is a scattered complex of pavilions, offices, workshops, gardens and courtyards, spread across a wooded hillside. Think of it less as one house and more as a private town for a sultan, hidden among trees.

For a long time, this hill served as a royal retreat. Ottoman rulers came here to hunt, to walk, and to escape the formality of the great palaces on the shore. In the late eighteenth century, Sultan Selim the Third built a pavilion here for his mother. Over the following decades, other sultans added kiosks and garden buildings. Still, for most of that time, Yıldız stayed a place for short visits, not a seat of government.

That changed with one man — Sultan Abdülhamid the Second. He came to the throne in 1876, in a period of war, debt and political crisis. Soon afterwards, he left the vast Dolmabahçe Palace on the waterfront and moved up this hill. Historians often link the move to his concern for security. Dolmabahçe faces the open water. Yıldız is enclosed by walls and trees. From here, for more than three decades, he ruled one of the largest empires in the world.

So the quiet you hear today is a little misleading. In the late nineteenth century, this hillside was the political centre of the Ottoman state. Ministers, secretaries, foreign envoys and guards moved along these paths. Reports from every province arrived here. The sultan read many of them himself. He was known for long working days and close personal control of the state. The bureaucracy grew around him, and the palace grew with it.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Abdülhamid was a skilled carpenter. He kept a woodworking workshop inside the palace grounds and made furniture with his own hands. Some pieces of his work survive today. Picture it — a ruler of an empire, with sawdust on his sleeves, shaping a cabinet in the evening. For a man who trusted very few people, the workshop may have been one of the few places where he could truly rest.

His interests did not stop at wood. He also founded a porcelain factory on these grounds in the late nineteenth century. Its workers produced vases, plates and decorative pieces for the palace and as gifts. The factory buildings still stand within the complex. The sultan also collected photographs. He commissioned large albums showing schools, barracks, bridges, factories and people across the empire. He rarely travelled, so the camera brought his empire to him.

Now let us turn to the Şale Pavilion, the best-known building of the complex. Its name comes from the French word chalet, and you can see why. It has timber details and a style that looks more like a lodge in the Alps than a building on the Bosphorus. The pavilion was used for important guests. Its most famous visitor was the German emperor, Kaiser Wilhelm the Second, who came to Istanbul more than once during Abdülhamid's reign.

Inside the Şale, the rooms change in style from one to the next. Some carry European influences, and others use Ottoman patterns and woodwork. The largest hall holds a very large carpet woven at Hereke, the imperial carpet workshop on the Sea of Marmara. Standing in that hall, you feel the scale of state ceremony. Then you step into a smaller room nearby, and the mood becomes almost domestic. The contrast is part of the experience.

A second surprise waits just outside the walls. Close to the palace stands the Hamidiye Mosque, also known as the Yıldız Mosque. Every Friday, the sultan rode out to pray there in a public procession. Crowds and diplomats gathered to watch. In 1905, a bomb exploded near the mosque after Friday prayers. The sultan survived. According to the accounts, he had stopped to talk with a religious official on the steps, and that short delay may have saved his life.

Abdülhamid lost the throne in 1909, after the Young Turk revolution, a reform movement led largely by army officers. With his fall, the palace lost its role. Parts of the complex were later used for other purposes over the twentieth century. Some buildings fell into disrepair. In recent decades, many of them have been restored. Today, the grounds are divided. Some areas are open to visitors, and others still serve official or institutional functions.

Below the palace walls lies Yıldız Park, once part of the imperial gardens and now a public park. On weekends, Istanbul families come here to walk, picnic and drink tea. Within the park you can find smaller pavilions, such as the Malta Pavilion and the Tent Pavilion, now used as cafés. Students sit on benches with books. Newly married couples pose for photographs under the trees. The sultan's private forest has become the city's shared garden.

As you walk, notice how the paths curve and climb. Few of them run in straight lines. Views of the water appear and then disappear behind branches. This was deliberate in Ottoman garden design. The landscape rewards slow movement. If you stop at an opening in the trees, you may see ferries crossing below and the Bosphorus Bridge in the distance. The modern city is right there — yet it feels far away.

So before you leave, pause for a minute where you stand. Feel the slope under your feet. Listen to the leaves, and the faint traffic far below. A sultan once chose this hill because it hid him from the world. Now it is open to anyone with a little time. As you walk back down toward the water, the noise of Beşiktaş will rise to meet you. Let the quiet of the trees stay with you a little longer.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear comfortable shoes; the hillside paths climb steeply.
Check which pavilions are open before you go.
Separate areas of the complex may need separate tickets.
Visit the park on a weekday morning for quiet.
Walk downhill afterwards toward Ortaköy and the waterfront.
