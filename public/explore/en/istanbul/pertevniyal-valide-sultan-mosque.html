The guide

The story

Listen first. Trams hum along the rails. Car horns echo off concrete. Buses sigh as they stop at the Aksaray junction. Then, just beside all this noise, a pale stone mosque rises from its own small island of calm. Its walls are a warm cream colour, and in the afternoon light they look almost golden. You are standing at one of the busiest crossroads in old Istanbul, and you are looking at one of its most unusual places of prayer.

This is the Pertevniyal Valide Sultan Mosque. Locals often call it simply the Valide Mosque of Aksaray. It was completed around 1871, in the final decades of the Ottoman Empire. By then the great age of classical mosque building was long over. The famous architect Sinan had been dead for nearly three centuries. A new generation of builders looked in a very different direction... towards Europe.

First, a word about the woman behind the name. Valide Sultan was the title for the mother of the reigning sultan. In Ottoman court life, this was one of the most powerful positions a woman could hold. Pertevniyal was the mother of Sultan Abdülaziz, who ruled from 1861 to 1876. As Valide Sultan, she controlled large funds, and like many royal mothers before her, she used them to build. This mosque was her great public gift to the city.

Now look at the building itself. At first glance it seems familiar — a central dome, two slender minarets, grey lead on the roof. But look again at the windows. Many of them are pointed and narrow, closer to a Gothic church than to a classical Ottoman mosque. Look at the carved stone bands, the small towers, the decorative edges. This mix of styles is the surprise of the place. It is an Ottoman mosque dressed in European and Moorish clothing.

Architectural historians call this style eclectic. That word simply means borrowing freely from many traditions at once. Here you can find touches of Gothic, Renaissance, and North African Islamic design, all on one facade. Some visitors also see Indian influence in the shapes of the small domes and towers. In the nineteenth century, this kind of mixture was fashionable across the Ottoman capital. It was a way for the empire to show that it belonged to the modern, international world.

Who actually designed it? Here the record is honestly uncertain. Some sources credit an Italian architect known as Montani, who worked in Istanbul during this period. Others connect the building to the Balyan family, Armenian architects who designed many palaces and mosques for the sultans in the nineteenth century. Possibly more than one hand shaped the final building. What matters for us is the result — a monument that sits right at the point where East and West met in late Ottoman taste.

Walk along the side of the mosque and notice that it stands on a raised base. Many late Ottoman royal mosques include a special section for the sultan, a private lodge where he could rest and pray apart from the crowd. This mosque follows that pattern. The royal lodge gave the ruler his own entrance and his own rooms. It shows how, by this period, a mosque was also a stage for royal ceremony.

Next to the mosque you will find the tomb of Pertevniyal herself. Ottoman founders were often buried beside their foundations, so their names and prayers would stay connected to the good works they paid for. Her tomb is a quiet space, set apart from the main prayer hall. Stand near it for a moment. Behind you is the noise of the twenty-first century. In front of you is the resting place of a woman who shaped this corner of the city.

Now here is the second surprise, and many visitors miss it. The mosque did not always sit beside a giant road. In the middle of the twentieth century, Istanbul widened many of its old streets for cars. Large roads were cut through the historic peninsula, and Aksaray became a major traffic hub. Parts of the old surroundings disappeared. Today the mosque sits lower and closer to the traffic than its builders ever planned. Its calm courtyard became a small refuge inside a modern junction.

If you step inside, take a moment to let your eyes adjust. The light falls softly through the high windows onto the carpet. The decoration here also mixes traditions. There is painted ornament, carved marble, and the flowing lines of Arabic calligraphy. Look toward the mihrab, the niche in the wall that shows the direction of Mecca. Notice how the ornament around it is richer and more detailed than the plain stone outside. The interior feels more intimate than the large classical mosques nearby.

Remember that this is a working mosque. Local people come here for the five daily prayers, and on Fridays the hall fills with worshippers. Visitors are welcome outside prayer times. Remove your shoes at the entrance. Women usually cover their hair, and everyone should cover shoulders and knees. Speak quietly, and avoid walking directly in front of people who are praying. These small gestures of respect make your visit easier, and they are always noticed.

Pertevniyal's generosity did not stop with this mosque. In this district there is also a school that still carries her name, and it continues as an educational institution today. Royal mothers in the Ottoman world often funded schools, fountains, and kitchens alongside their mosques. These buildings served ordinary people in daily life. So as you walk through Aksaray, you are moving through a neighbourhood that one nineteenth-century woman helped to shape, long after her lifetime ended.

Before you leave, step back to a safe spot where you can see the whole building at once. Look at the dome, then the two minarets, then those pointed Gothic windows. Listen again to the trams, the traffic, the voices of the street. This mosque was built for an empire that was trying to change. Today it stands in a city that is still changing around it. As you walk away, glance back once more... the cream stone will still be catching the light.

What to look for

Pointed Gothic-style windows unusual for an Ottoman mosque.
Two slender minarets framing the central lead-covered dome.
The raised royal lodge built for the sultan's private use.
Cream-coloured stone glowing warm in afternoon sunlight.
Ornate carved marble surrounding the mihrab prayer niche.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit outside the five daily prayer times for easier access.
Avoid Friday midday, when the prayer hall fills completely.
Bring a scarf to cover hair, shoulders, or knees.
Use the Aksaray tram stop to arrive without crossing traffic.
View the full facade from a safe spot across the junction.
