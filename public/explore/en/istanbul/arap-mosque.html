The guide

The story

Listen first. The clang of metal on metal, a shop shutter rolling up, a handcart rattling over uneven stone. These narrow lanes of Karaköy belong to hardware sellers, rope merchants and pipe fitters. Then, between the workshops, a tall square tower rises above the rooftops. It does not look like an Ottoman minaret. It looks like a bell tower from an Italian hill town. That is because, for its first two centuries, that is exactly what it was.

You are standing in the old heart of Galata, the walled district across the Golden Horn from the historic city. In the late Middle Ages, Galata was a Genoese colony. Genoa was a powerful trading republic in northern Italy, and its merchants ran this harbour town with their own laws, their own walls and their own churches. The building in front of you began life as one of those churches, raised by Dominican friars in the early fourteenth century.

The church was dedicated to Saint Paul, and later also to Saint Dominic, the founder of the friars' order. It served the Latin Catholic community of Galata, people whose lives were tied to ships, warehouses and long sea routes to the Black Sea and the Mediterranean. Historians consider it one of the most important surviving medieval Latin church buildings in Istanbul. Few structures from Genoese Galata still stand. This one has kept its basic shape for around seven hundred years.

Now look carefully at the walls and the tower. The style is Gothic, the architecture of medieval Western Europe, with its pointed arches and tall, narrow openings. Many traces of that style have been covered, altered or filled in over the centuries. But the long rectangular hall and the square tower still read clearly as a European church. Most visitors walk past without realising they are looking at Italian architecture in the middle of Istanbul.

The story changed after the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453. Galata surrendered without a long fight, and its Latin residents kept some of their churches for a time. Later, the building was converted into a mosque. The tower kept its square body but took on a new role. Instead of bells, it now carried the call to prayer. The minaret you see today is that old bell tower, with a conical cap added on top.

Here is the second twist in the story, and it explains the name. Arap Camii means the Arab Mosque. In 1492, the Catholic monarchs completed their conquest of Granada, the last Muslim kingdom in Spain. In the years that followed, many Muslims from Spain, often called Moors, fled across the Mediterranean. Some of them found refuge in the Ottoman Empire, and a community settled here in Galata. This mosque became their place of worship.

To the Istanbul residents of that time, these newcomers from Andalusia were simply Arabs. So the name stuck. Think about that for a moment. A church built by Italian friars became the spiritual home of refugees from Spain, under the rule of an Ottoman sultan. In one building, you can trace three Mediterranean worlds meeting on a single street corner. That layering is the real treasure of this place.

There is also an older tale, and it belongs to legend rather than to the historical record. Locals say a mosque stood on this spot much earlier, founded by an Arab commander during one of the early sieges of Constantinople, in the eighth century. According to this popular story, the Arab army was allowed a place of prayer in Galata. Historians have found no firm evidence for it. But the legend adds another layer to the name.

The building has had a hard life. Fires swept through Galata many times, and the mosque was damaged and repaired more than once. Restorations in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries changed its interior and parts of its exterior. During one restoration, workers lifting the old floor found medieval gravestones from the Genoese period. Several of these carved stones were later moved to the Istanbul Archaeology Museums, where they help tell the story of Latin Galata.

If you step inside the courtyard, you will find a quieter world. The noise of the hardware market fades behind the walls. There is a fountain for ablutions, the ritual washing that comes before prayer. Trees give shade in summer. Men from the nearby shops come here to pray between jobs, still wearing work clothes. This is a working neighbourhood mosque, not a museum, and that everyday rhythm is part of its character.

Inside, the prayer hall is long and rectangular, which is unusual for an Ottoman mosque. Most mosques in Istanbul are built around a central dome. Here, a wooden roof covers the hall instead, following the shape of the old church. The direction of prayer, toward Mecca, does not match the original axis of the building, so the carpets and the prayer niche sit at an angle to the walls. Watch how the room adjusts to that difference.

A few practical notes as you visit. This is an active place of worship. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered, and women should bring a scarf for their hair. Remove your shoes before entering the prayer hall. Avoid visiting during the five daily prayers, and especially at midday on Fridays, when the hall fills with worshippers. Keep your voice low, and ask before photographing anyone at prayer.

Before you leave, walk back out into the lane and look up at the tower once more. Notice how the stone of the shaft meets the later cap on top. That join marks the moment one faith handed the building to another. Around you, the shops keep selling bolts, cables and taps, much as merchants traded here when Genoese ships filled the harbour. Galata has always been a place of exchange... and this tower has stood above all of it.
