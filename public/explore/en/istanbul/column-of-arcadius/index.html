The guide

The story

Listen first. A scooter hums somewhere up the slope. A door clicks shut. Someone is hanging laundry on a line above your head. Now look down at the block of grey stone pressed between the houses. It is weathered, chipped, and stained by rain. It looks almost like part of a garden wall. But this block is the last surviving piece of a monument that once rose high above the city of Constantinople.

You are standing in the Cerrahpaşa quarter of Fatih, on the seventh hill of old Istanbul. The Byzantines called this hill the Xerolophos, which means the dry hill. In the early fifth century, this was not a quiet residential lane. It was a grand public square, the Forum of Arcadius. Processions passed through here on their way along the main ceremonial road toward the land walls and the countryside beyond.

The emperor behind it all was Arcadius. He was the elder son of Theodosius the Great, the last emperor to rule both halves of the Roman world. When Theodosius died in 395, the empire was divided between his two young sons. Arcadius took the East, with Constantinople as his capital. His brother Honorius took the West. That division would shape the next thousand years of history.

Arcadius was not remembered as a strong ruler. Much of real power sat with his ministers and generals. Around the year 400, a Gothic general named Gainas briefly seized control of the capital. The people of Constantinople rose against his soldiers, and the Gothic forces were driven out and later defeated. The new column was built to celebrate that victory. In stone, it told the city that order had returned.

The model was clear to anyone who knew Rome. In the old capital, Trajan's Column carried a long spiral band of carved scenes, winding upward like a ribbon. The column of Arcadius followed the same idea. A continuous frieze, a long band of sculpture, wrapped around the shaft. It showed soldiers, ships, triumphal scenes, and the defeat of the enemy. Inside, a staircase climbed toward the top.

Here is the first surprise. Arcadius never saw his column finished. Work began early in the fifth century, but the emperor died in 408. The project passed to his son, Theodosius the Second. A statue of Arcadius was placed on the summit in 421, many years after his death. So the monument became partly a son's tribute to his father, as much as a father's boast about his own victory.

For centuries, the column stood over this hill. Sailors on the Sea of Marmara could likely see it from the water. Constantinople, though, sits in an earthquake zone. In the eighth century, a powerful earthquake shook the city and threw the imperial statue from the summit. The column itself survived, scarred and weakened. It went on standing without its emperor, a bare shaft of carved marble above the rooftops.

After the Ottoman conquest in 1453, the column stayed in place. The forum around it slowly filled with houses, workshops, and lanes. Over time the old public square disappeared under the growing neighbourhood. Byzantine writers had also attached prophecies and strange tales to the reliefs. Legend has it that some people read the carvings as predictions of the city's future. These stories belong to folklore, not to documented history, but they show how mysterious the monument had become.

Then came the end. By the early eighteenth century, more earthquakes had left the column unstable. Residents feared it would fall onto the homes around it. In 1715, the Ottoman authorities ordered it taken down. The great shaft was dismantled, and only the base was left behind. That is the stone in front of you now — about the height of a single storey, with houses built right up against it.

Here is the second surprise. We still know roughly what the lost frieze looked like. Before the column came down, European travellers and artists drew it. One set of detailed drawings from the sixteenth century, known as the Freshfield Album, is kept today in Cambridge, in England. Its pages show the spiral of soldiers and ships rising around the shaft. Much of what historians know about these carvings comes from paper, not from stone.

Now look closely at the base itself. The surface is worn, but parts of its carved decoration are still visible in places. Look for the edges of panels and fragments of relief where figures once stood. Notice how rough patches sit next to smoother areas. Fire, weather, and centuries of daily life in this lane have all left marks. Look, too, at how the modern walls meet the ancient blocks.

This is a different kind of visit from the great mosques and palaces of the old city. There is no grand entrance and no crowd. The base is often behind a fence, and you will mostly view it from the lane. Be quiet and respectful, because people live right here. The neighbourhood carries on around you — children, delivery vans, the smell of bread or cooking from an open window.

Before you walk on, turn slowly and take in the whole lane. The street level here is higher than the original forum, so imagine a wide open square below your feet. Imagine marble paving, crowds, and a column rising far above. Then come back to what is really here: a grey stone block, a strip of sky, and the laundry moving gently overhead. As you walk away down the hill, glance back once more.
