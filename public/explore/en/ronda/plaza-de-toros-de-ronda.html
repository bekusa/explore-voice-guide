The guide

The story

Step through the gate, and the first thing you feel is the sand. It is soft, pale, and slightly loose underfoot, warm on a sunny afternoon. Around you rises a perfect ring of stone and shadow. Two tiers of arches circle the arena, and the noise of the town drops away almost at once. For a moment it is just you, the open sky, and an enormous circle of golden earth. This is the Plaza de Toros de Ronda, one of the oldest bullrings in Spain.

Before we look closer, it helps to understand where you are standing. Ronda sits high on a rocky plateau in the mountains of Andalusia, in southern Spain. A deep gorge splits the town in two. The bullring stands in the newer part, a short walk from the famous bridge that crosses that gorge. Many visitors come for the bridge and the cliffs. Then they turn a corner and find this quiet, round building waiting for them.

The ring was completed in 1785, in the late eighteenth century. It was built for an unusual owner, the Real Maestranza de Caballería de Ronda. That name means the Royal Cavalry Order of Ronda. This is an aristocratic riding society, founded in the sixteenth century to train noblemen in horsemanship and the skills of mounted combat. The order still owns the bullring today. In a sense, this is not only an arena. It is also the home of a very old club of riders.

Here is the first surprise. Look across the arena and notice how wide it is. The sand circle here is unusually large, among the widest of any bullring in the world. Yet the seating feels close and intimate, because there are only two tiers. Many later bullrings were built bigger and taller to hold huge crowds. Ronda kept a more human scale. Standing in the centre, you can almost imagine the arena as a theatre, with every seat watching you.

The architecture rewards a slow look. The stands are covered by a roof and supported by elegant stone columns. On the upper level, the columns stand in a long, repeating row, and their rhythm guides your eye around the whole ring. The building is made mostly of local stone, which turns honey-coloured in the afternoon light. The design is often credited to José Martín de Aldehuela, an architect also associated with Ronda’s great bridge over the gorge.

Now for the human story. Ronda holds a special place in the history of bullfighting on foot. In earlier centuries, bullfighting in Spain was largely a mounted affair, performed by noblemen on horseback. Over the eighteenth century, a style on foot developed, with the matador facing the bull with a cape. A local family, the Romeros, became central to this change. Francisco Romero is often remembered as a pioneer of this approach, and his grandson Pedro Romero became one of the most celebrated matadors of his age.

Pedro Romero’s name still runs through the life of the town. Every year, in early September, Ronda holds a festival in his honour. Its high point is a special event called the Corrida Goyesca. The word Goyesca refers to Francisco de Goya, the great Spanish painter who made many images of bullfighting. During this event, the participants wear costumes inspired by the clothing in Goya’s paintings, from the late eighteenth century. It is one of the few times each year that this old ring fills with a crowd.

That brings us to an honest point. Bullfighting divides opinion deeply, both in Spain and abroad. For some people it is an art form and a living tradition. For many others it is cruelty, and support for it has fallen in recent decades. You do not need to admire it to find this place interesting. The building also tells a story about horsemanship, social class, architecture, and the identity of a mountain town. Each visitor can hold their own view while standing here.

Ronda also drew writers and filmmakers from far away. The American novelist Ernest Hemingway spent time here and wrote about bullfighting with real passion. The film director Orson Welles loved the town too. According to accounts of his life, his ashes were laid to rest on a country estate near Ronda, belonging to his friend, the matador Antonio Ordóñez. So this ring is connected not only to local families, but to a wider world of artists who came looking for something they felt was disappearing.

Here is a second detail many visitors miss. The visit does not end at the sand. Beneath and behind the stands, you can usually explore the working spaces of the ring. Look for the pens where the bulls were held before a fight, and the passages that lead out into the arena. There are stables too, a reminder of the Maestranza’s deep link with horses. These rooms feel very different from the open arena. They are narrow, shaded, and quiet, with thick walls and heavy wooden gates.

Inside, there is also a museum. One part focuses on the history of bullfighting, with old posters, costumes, and objects linked to famous matadors, including the Romero family. Another part reflects the history of the cavalry order itself. Here you may find saddles, riding equipment, and a collection of historic firearms. Take your time with the costumes. Up close, the embroidery is dense and heavy, and you can see how much skilled handwork went into each piece.

Before you leave, walk back out onto the sand. Climb into the stands if you can, and sit for a moment in the shade of the arches. From up here, the shape of the ring becomes clear. The circle feels complete and calm. Listen for the small sounds that fill it now: footsteps in the sand, voices of other visitors, swallows calling overhead. Outside the main entrance, look for the bronze bull statue and the monuments to famous local matadors nearby.

As you step back into the street, notice how quickly the town returns. Cafés, traffic, and the smell of coffee wait just beyond the walls. A few minutes away, the land drops into the gorge, and the mountains spread out toward the horizon. Take one last look at the curved stone wall behind you. For more than two centuries it has held crowds, riders, and arguments about tradition. Today, it holds the quiet footsteps of travellers like you.

Practical tips

Bring sun protection; the open arena gets very hot.
