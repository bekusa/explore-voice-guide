The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you look for anything, just listen. Teak leaves shift high above you, dry and papery. A bird calls somewhere you cannot see, then calls again. Under your feet, the ground is red earth, packed hard, scattered with fallen leaves that crack when you step on them. This is the forest of Indravati, in the far south of Chhattisgarh, and its first lesson is patience.

Indravati National Park lies in Bijapur District, in the heart of the region known as Bastar. It takes its name from the Indravati River, which runs along its northern edge. The river does more than give a name. It forms a natural boundary, and across the water lies the state of Maharashtra. For much of the year the river is the main source of water for the animals that live here, so the life of this forest leans towards its banks.

The park was declared a national park in the early 1980s. In 1983 it became a tiger reserve under Project Tiger, India's national programme to protect its big cats and the habitats they need. Today it is the largest protected tiger habitat in Chhattisgarh. That status matters. It means this landscape was judged important enough to set aside, not only for tigers, but for the whole web of life that supports them.

Now, here is something many visitors do not expect. The animal most closely tied to Indravati is not the tiger. It is the wild water buffalo. This is not the domestic buffalo you may see on farms across India. It is a wild species, heavier and more powerful, with long, wide, curving horns. Only a few small populations survive anywhere in the world, and this corner of central India holds one of them.

The wild buffalo is also the state animal of Chhattisgarh. So in a sense, this forest guards a living emblem of the whole state. Their numbers here are very small, and conservation teams have worked hard to protect the remaining animals. If you see one, you are seeing something genuinely rare... a survivor from a time when great herds of wild cattle moved freely across the plains and forests of South Asia.

Look around at the trees. The forest here is mostly deciduous, which means many trees drop their leaves in the dry season. You will find teak, with its broad, rough leaves, and sal, a tall, straight hardwood common across central India. Thick stands of bamboo grow in between. In the hot months the canopy thins and light falls through in patches. After the monsoon, everything turns a deep, wet green, and the forest closes in around you.

The mix of grassland, forest and river creates room for many kinds of animals. Alongside tigers and leopards, the park is home to gaur, the massive Indian bison. There are sambar and chital, two kinds of deer, and the nilgai, a large antelope. Sloth bears forage for termites here. Packs of dhole, the Indian wild dog, hunt together. Smaller, shyer animals also live here, including the four-horned antelope and the tiny mouse deer.

Listen for birds too. Chhattisgarh's state bird is the hill myna, a glossy black bird with bright yellow patches on its head. It is famous for its voice. In captivity it can copy human speech, but in the wild it fills the forest with whistles, gurgles and sharp calls. Hornbills, peafowl and many raptors also move through the canopy. Even when the large mammals stay hidden, the birds tell you the forest is full of life.

The people of this land are part of its story. Bastar is home to Adivasi communities, the Indigenous peoples of India, including Gond, Maria and Muria groups. For generations their lives have been woven into these forests. They gather forest produce, hold weekly village markets, and keep traditions of song, dance and craft. Bastar is known across India for its metalwork and its carved wooden figures. The forest is not empty wilderness. It is a homeland.

There is a harder side to the story, and an honest guide should mention it. For many years, this part of Bastar has been affected by armed conflict between Maoist insurgents and the state. That conflict made the forest difficult to reach. Researchers, forest staff and visitors have often been unable to move freely here. As a result, Indravati remains one of the least studied tiger reserves in India, and much about its wildlife is still not fully known.

This is why visiting Indravati is not like visiting a busy safari park. There are no long rows of jeeps, and few tourist facilities. Access rules can change, and some areas may be closed. The quiet you hear around you is partly the result of that isolation. It is a strange contrast. The same forces that kept people away may also have left parts of this forest less disturbed than many others.

So take your time. Watch the edges where forest meets grassland, because that is where deer often graze in the early morning. Look at the soft mud near water for hoofprints and pugmarks, the paw prints of big cats. Notice scratch marks on tree trunks, and the small mounds of termite nests. Each one is a clue. In a forest like this, most of what you learn comes from small signs, not big sightings.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Feel the warmth rising from the red earth. Watch the light move through the teak leaves, and listen for the river somewhere to the north. You may not have seen a tiger or a wild buffalo today... and that is all right. They are out there, somewhere in this forest. As you walk away, carry the quiet with you. It is the real voice of Indravati.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current access rules with the forest department first.
Hire a local guide approved by forest authorities.
Go early morning when animals visit grassland edges.
Carry water, snacks and sun protection for remote roads.
Wear muted colours and keep voices low.
