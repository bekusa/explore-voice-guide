The guide

The story

Feel the stone under your hand first. It is rough and grey-brown, and it is warm where the sun has reached it. Large blocks sit side by side, and the gaps between them are packed with small, flat stones, laid one on another like the pages of a closed book. Above you, the hill continues to rise. Below you, the green plain of Mardan stretches out in every direction. The wind up here is steady, and the noise of the town has faded to a faint hum.

This is Takht-i-Bahi, a Buddhist monastery built on a rocky ridge in what is now Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, in northern Pakistan. The name is usually explained as "the throne of the spring" or "the throne of the water." Takht means throne. Bahi is often linked to a source of water. Either way, the throne part makes sense once you stand here. The monastery sits high above the land, like a seat set apart from the world below.

The complex took shape around the first century of the common era. At that time, this region was part of Gandhara. Gandhara was a cultural crossroads where Indian, Persian, Central Asian and Greek influences met and mixed. Traders, monks and armies passed along the routes that ran through these valleys. Buddhism spread here with force, and monasteries like this one became centres of study, prayer and daily discipline. Monks lived, worked and meditated on this hill for several centuries.

Here is the first surprise. Takht-i-Bahi is linked to a king whose name also appears in a very different story. An inscription found at the site mentions Gondophares, a ruler of the Indo-Parthian dynasty. The Indo-Parthians were a branch of Persian-speaking rulers who controlled parts of this region. In Christian tradition, a king with a similar name appears in the legend of Saint Thomas the Apostle, who is said to have travelled to India. Historians still discuss how the two connect. The legend is uncertain, but the inscription itself is real, and it helps date this site.

Now look at the walls again. That pattern of big stones with small stones packed between them is called diaper masonry. It is typical of Gandharan building. The small stones fill the gaps and spread the weight. No heavy mortar holds this together in the way modern walls are held. And yet, much of it is still standing after nearly two thousand years. Few monasteries of this age in the region survive in such a complete state.

Why did this one last? Part of the answer is the hill itself. Its height and its awkward approach kept it away from the main paths of later armies and builders. Many lowland sites were stripped for their stone, or ploughed over, or built upon. Up here, the monastery was simply hard to reach. Isolation protected it. Because of this, the site was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1980, together with the nearby ruins of Sahr-i-Bahlol.

The monastery is not one single building. It is a group of courts and rooms set on different levels of the ridge. One of the most striking areas is the Court of Stupas. A stupa is a dome-shaped Buddhist monument that holds relics or marks a sacred place. Here, many small stupas once stood close together, crowded into an open court. Pilgrims and monks offered them as acts of devotion. Today you see mostly their square bases, set in neat rows.

Above the stupa court is the main stupa area, where a larger stupa once rose. Monks and visitors walked around it in a clockwise direction. This walking ritual is still practised by Buddhists today. Around the court, small niches once held statues of the Buddha and of bodhisattvas, who are enlightened beings in Buddhist belief. Many of those sculptures were removed during excavations, which took place in stages from the late nineteenth century onward. Some are now kept in museums, including the Peshawar Museum.

Next comes the monastic court. This was the living quarter. It is an open square surrounded by small cells. Each cell was the private room of a monk. Step inside one if you can. The space is tight, perhaps enough for a mat, a lamp and a few possessions. Many cells have small niches in the walls, likely for lamps or objects of worship. Imagine dozens of monks waking here before dawn, the courtyard slowly filling with quiet movement.

Here is the second surprise, and many visitors walk right past it. Below the main levels are underground chambers. They are low, narrow rooms with very little light. Scholars often describe them as spaces for meditation, or possibly for storage. Whichever is true, the effect is powerful. Duck inside for a moment. The air feels cooler. Sound becomes dull and close. It is easy to understand why a monk might choose a room like this to sit in silence.

Near the monastic court are the remains of an assembly hall, where the community gathered. There are also traces of a kitchen and other working spaces. Together, these rooms show something important. This was not only a place of worship. It was a full community, with meals, meetings, chores and teaching. Buddhist monasteries in Gandhara were often supported by local donors, who gave food, money and land in return for spiritual merit.

As you move between the levels, notice how the builders used the slope. Courts are set on terraces cut into the hill. Stairs and passages link one level to the next. Water would have been precious up here, which may explain the old meaning of the name. Look outward too. On a clear day, the plain below shows fields of crops, villages and roads. The monks saw a quieter version of this same view every day.

Buddhism in Gandhara slowly declined in later centuries, and the monastery was eventually abandoned. The exact reasons are still debated. Changes in trade, politics and religion all played a part. But the stones stayed. The courts stayed. Even the small cells stayed, open to the sky now, their wooden roofs long gone.

Before you start down, stand still for a moment in one of the open courts. Feel the wind on the ridge. Listen for birds, or for distant voices from the town. Look again at those small stones packed between the large ones. Someone placed each of them by hand. As you walk down the path, glance back once. The monastery will sink into the hill, grey stone against grey rock, just as it has for almost two thousand years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb early in the morning to avoid midday heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for steep, uneven stone steps.
Carry water because shade on the ridge is limited.
Bring a small torch for the dark underground chambers.
Visit Peshawar Museum to see sculptures from Gandharan sites.
