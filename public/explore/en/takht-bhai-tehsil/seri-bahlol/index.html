The guide

The story

Listen first. A motorbike passes somewhere below. A rooster calls from a courtyard. Children's voices carry between mud-brick walls. Under your feet the ground rises gently, and it is not natural ground at all. This low mound is built from the remains of a town that stood here many centuries ago. Welcome to Seri Bahlol, where people still live on top of the ancient city.

This is the quiet partner of a much more famous neighbour. A few kilometres away, the Buddhist monastery of Takht-i-Bahi sits high on its rocky ridge. In 1980, UNESCO placed both sites together on the World Heritage List. The monastery was the spiritual centre. Seri Bahlol was a fortified settlement on the plain below. Together they show two sides of one world: the life of the monks and the life of ordinary people.

That world is called Gandhara. The name refers to an ancient region in what is now northwest Pakistan and eastern Afghanistan. For several centuries it was a meeting point of cultures. Traders, pilgrims and armies moved through these valleys. Buddhist teaching grew strong here. Local artists also learned from Greek, Persian and Indian traditions, and they mixed these influences into a style that is still recognised around the world today.

The town grew most during the rule of the Kushans. The Kushans were a dynasty from Central Asia who controlled a large empire across this region in the first centuries of the Common Era. Under their rule, Buddhist monasteries were built across the Peshawar Valley. Seri Bahlol was one of the settlements that supported this landscape of faith, farming and trade.

Look at the shape of the land around you. Seri Bahlol stands on a raised mound above the surrounding fields. This height did not appear by accident. When people build, rebuild and build again in the same place, the ground slowly rises with each layer. Archaeologists call this kind of mound a tell. Every step up this slope is a step over older floors, walls and lost rooms.

The town was protected by defensive walls. Fragments of these fortifications still survive in places around the edge of the mound. In Gandhara, builders often used a technique now called diaper masonry. They set large stone blocks in rows and packed the gaps with neat stacks of small, flat stones. The result looks like a patterned grid. If you find an old wall here, check whether it follows this pattern.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Seri Bahlol is not a closed ruin behind a fence. It is a living village. Houses, lanes and courtyards sit directly on the archaeological layers. Families have lived here for generations. This makes the site unusual and also fragile. Every new foundation or dug pit can disturb remains that no one has studied yet.

In the early twentieth century, archaeologists working under British India excavated parts of the mound and the surrounding area. They found many sculptures carved in a grey stone called schist. Schist is a fine-grained rock that splits into smooth layers, which allowed sculptors to carve very fine detail. The figures show the Buddha, Buddhist saints called bodhisattvas, and scenes from the Buddha's life.

Many of these sculptures did not stay here. Today, important pieces from Seri Bahlol are kept in museums, especially the Peshawar Museum. So the village you see is only half of the story. The other half is in glass cases, often a long drive away. If you visit Peshawar, look for labels that name this place. It is a good way to connect the two halves.

The site has also suffered. Over the years, illegal digging for antiquities has damaged parts of the mound. Gandharan sculpture has a high value on the international art market, and this creates a strong temptation. Conservation work and protection are ongoing challenges. This is one reason visitors should never pick up fragments, buy objects offered for sale, or dig in the soil.

Now think about daily life here in the town's best years. Picture farmers bringing grain in from the plain. Picture potters, stonecutters and traders working in narrow streets. Monks from the hills perhaps came down for supplies and alms. The monastery on the ridge needed food, labour and support, and towns like this one provided much of it. Faith and farming depended on each other.

The view helps you understand this relationship. On a clear day, look toward the hills. The ridge of Takht-i-Bahi is close enough to feel connected. Monastery and town were part of one landscape. Many visitors see only the monastery and leave. Standing here, you see the ordinary half of the picture... the half where most people actually lived.

Visit with respect. This is someone's home as well as a heritage site. Ask before photographing people or houses. Stay on paths and lanes. A local guide or a member of the community can often show you which parts of the old walls are still visible. Conversations here are often the best way to learn what has been found over the years.

Before you go, pause on the highest ground you can reach. Feel the slope under your feet once more. The wind moves across the fields, and the sounds of the village continue around you. Somewhere below these houses lie older walls, older floors and perhaps more stone figures still waiting. As you walk away, notice how the town and the land join together. Here, the past is still under the present.
