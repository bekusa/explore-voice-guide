The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you look up. There is the low rumble of steel wheels on track, a rising chorus of screams, then a sudden quiet as a train drops out of sight. Underneath it all, if you stand near the edge of the water, you can hear something gentler. Small waves lap against the banks, and waterbirds call across the lakes. This is Thorpe Park, on the edge of Chertsey in Surrey, and it is a theme park built on water.

That water is the first surprise most visitors miss. The land here was once worked for sand and gravel. This stretch of the Thames valley has long supplied building material for London and the towns around it. When the digging stopped, the deep pits filled with groundwater and became lakes. The park you are walking through sits on the islands and strips of land between those flooded workings. Look at a map of the site and it seems less like a field of rides and more like an archipelago, a scattered chain of small islands.

The idea of turning an old quarry into a leisure park came from the business that dug it. A building materials company that owned the land developed the park, and it opened to the public in 1979. In those early years it was a very different place. It was gentler, aimed at families, and partly educational. Exhibits and attractions looked at British history and Britain's long relationship with the sea. Thrill rides came later, and slowly they took over the skyline.

The park has changed hands over the decades. Later it became part of the group that runs Madame Tussauds and several other British attractions. Today it is operated by Merlin Entertainments, the same company behind other well-known parks across the country. Each owner pushed it in a new direction. Step by step, the quiet lakeside day out turned into one of Britain's best-known destinations for roller coasters. The lakes stayed. Almost everything else was rebuilt around them.

If you want to understand that shift, stand somewhere you can see Colossus. This red coaster opened in 2002, and it was built to make a statement. It turns riders upside down ten times. When it opened, no other roller coaster in the world had more inversions. Watch a train complete the long run of twisting rolls near the end. From the ground, the riders look like a string of beads being tied into knots.

Not far away is a ride with a darker mood. Saw, The Ride is based on the horror film series, and it opened in 2009. Its best-known feature is the first drop. The track bends past vertical, so riders fall at an angle steeper than straight down. The queue and the building around it are designed to unsettle you. There are grimy walls, harsh lighting, and sound effects that play on your nerves. This is not a coaster for everyone. It does show how far the park has moved from its family beginnings.

Then there is Stealth, and you will probably hear it before you see it. This is a launched coaster. Instead of climbing a slow lift hill, the train fires forward from a standstill, reaching very high speed within a couple of seconds. It shoots straight up a tall tower, over the top, and straight back down again. The whole ride is over in moments. Many people say the launch is the part they remember most, that sudden push back into the seat.

The newest giant on the skyline is Hyperia. It opened in 2024, and at that time it was the tallest and fastest roller coaster in Britain. Look for its long, sweeping hills rising high above the trees and the lakes. Its scale is easier to understand from a distance than from directly underneath. From some paths across the water, the whole layout fits into a single view. Then you can follow a train from its first climb to its final turn.

Here is a second detail many visitors overlook. Because the park sits on islands, water shapes how every ride is placed. Several coasters swoop low over the lakes or skim close to the surface. The Swarm, a winged coaster where riders sit out on either side of the track, carries its trains over and around a scene of disaster and wreckage. Designers here use the water as a stage. It adds a sense of speed and danger, even when the track is well clear of the surface.

Not everything here is about extreme height. Water rides are a big part of the park, and on warm days they are among the most popular. Tidal Wave sends a boat down a steep chute into a large splash, and the spray reaches people on the footbridge nearby. Some onlookers stand there on purpose to get soaked. Others are caught by surprise. It is one of the easiest places in the park to watch people laugh. Even if you stay dry, it is a good spot to pause.

The park also has a strong seasonal life. In autumn it is known for its Halloween event, Fright Nights. Live scare actors, themed mazes and late evening sessions change the mood of the whole site. The same paths you walk in daylight feel very different after dark, with fog, coloured lights and sudden shouts from the shadows. If you are visiting at the right time of year, notice how the park dresses itself. The rides stay the same, but the atmosphere around them shifts completely.

It is worth thinking about where you are on a wider map. Chertsey is an old Thames-side town, with a history that reaches back to a medieval abbey long gone from the landscape. All around the park, the countryside is laced with reservoirs and former gravel pits, many now used for sailing, fishing and birdwatching. Thorpe Park is one of the loudest reuses of that industrial past. Just beyond its fences, the same kind of water is calm and almost empty.

As you move through the day, try to notice the small rhythms. Queues grow and shrink as people move from one area to another. Trains go round and round on the coasters, and the screams rise and fade with every circuit. Gulls and ducks carry on as if none of it is happening. The water barely moves, even beside the fastest rides. That contrast is the real character of this place, an old quarry where machines once dug for gravel now holds machines built for pleasure.

So before you head to the next ride, take one more look across the lake. Pick out the tallest track on the horizon and follow it with your eyes. Then look down at the water's edge, where the reeds grow and the surface holds the reflection of steel and sky. Both belong to the same story. One is very new and very loud. The other is quiet and much older, and it was here long before the first coaster.

Before you go

Practical tips

Head to the biggest coasters first before queues build.
Pack a change of clothes if you plan water rides.
Check height restrictions before queuing with younger children.
Find a lakeside path to view Hyperia's whole layout.
Book ahead if visiting during the autumn Fright Nights season.
