The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across the dry grass on the slope, and somewhere below a tractor works the fields. The ground under your feet is uneven, soft in places, hard with broken stone in others. Look closely and you may spot a fragment of pottery, reddish or grey, lying in the dust. This is not a natural hill. You are standing on a city, built and rebuilt for thousands of years, until its layers rose into the mound that holds you now.

This is Tel Rehov, in the Beit She'an Valley of northern Israel. The word tel, or tell, is the name archaeologists use for a mound like this one. Each generation built on the ruins of the last. Mud-brick walls collapsed, people levelled the debris, and new houses went up on top. Over centuries the city lifted itself higher and higher. Today it sits a short distance south of Beit She'an, a quiet rise in a wide, flat valley.

Turn slowly and take in the landscape. To the west, the long ridge of Mount Gilboa runs along the horizon. To the east, beyond the Jordan River, the hills of Gilead rise on the far side of the valley. Between them lie fields, fish ponds, and orchards. Springs and streams feed this ground, and that water is the reason people settled here at all. In a hot, dry region, a reliable spring meant bread, animals, and a future.

The name Rehov is very old. It appears in ancient Egyptian records, among the lists of places that the pharaohs claimed to control in this region. One mention appears on a stone monument of Pharaoh Seti the First, which was found at nearby Beit She'an. For Egypt, this valley mattered. It sat on routes that linked the coast with the Jordan Valley and the lands beyond. A city on this spot could watch the traffic pass.

For a long time, the mound kept its secrets. Then, in the late 1990s, a team from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem began systematic excavations here, led by the archaeologist Amihai Mazar. Season after season, they dug through the layers. What they found changed how scholars think about this region in the Iron Age, the period roughly three thousand years ago when the kingdoms described in the Hebrew Bible were taking shape.

Here is the first surprise, and it is a famous one. In the heart of the city, the excavators uncovered rows of clay cylinders, stacked on their sides like pipes. They were beehives. This was an organised apiary, an ancient honey farm, right among the houses. These are the oldest known man-made beehives ever found by archaeologists anywhere. Several dozen survived, and the team estimated that the original apiary held many more.

The hives were simple and clever. Each one was a tube of unbaked clay mixed with straw. One end had a small hole for the bees to come and go. The other end could be opened by the beekeeper to take out the honeycomb. Imagine the low hum of thousands of bees in this space, the smell of wax and smoke, and the sticky work of harvesting honey under the valley sun.

Now the second surprise. Inside the hives, researchers found the remains of the bees themselves. When scientists studied them, the bees did not match the local honeybees of this land. They appeared to be closer to a type of bee from Anatolia, the region that is now Turkey. That suggests the beekeepers of Rehov may have brought in bees from far away, perhaps because those bees were calmer or made more honey. Long-distance trade, it seems, included living insects.

Writing also survived here. On pieces of pottery, the excavators found short inscriptions in an early Hebrew alphabet. One carries the name Elisha, and the building where it was found was nicknamed the House of Elisha. Another inscription seems to name a family called Nimshi. In the Bible, Nimshi is linked to the line of Jehu, a king of Israel. Scholars are careful here. A matching name is a fascinating clue, but it is not proof of a direct link.

The Iron Age city was large and well planned for its time, with streets, houses, and workshops packed tightly together. Most buildings were made of mud brick on stone foundations. Mud brick is cheap and practical, but it does not last once a roof falls in. That is why so much of what you see now is soft, rounded earth rather than standing walls. Life here ended in violence. The excavators found signs of destruction and burning, probably during wars between kingdoms in the ninth century before the common era.

The story of Rehov did not end with that fire. Many centuries later, in the Byzantine period, a Jewish village with the same name grew up close by. Its synagogue had a mosaic floor with a long inscription in Aramaic and Hebrew. The text lists rules about farming and produce in the land, including which towns counted as part of it. It is one of the longest inscriptions of its kind ever found in Israel, and it shows how the name Rehov lived on.

As you walk the mound, notice how little is fenced off or rebuilt. This is a working archaeological landscape, not a polished park. The trenches and their shapes may be softened by weather and grass. Most of the important finds, including the hives and inscriptions, were taken away for study and protection. What remains is the shape of the city, the lie of the land, and the view that its people saw every morning.

Pause for a moment before you leave. Feel the warmth coming off the ground, and look again at the fields below, still green with irrigated crops. The springs that drew people here three thousand years ago still feed this valley. As you walk back down, watch the soil at your feet. Every sherd of broken pottery once belonged to someone's kitchen. Somewhere in the grass, the bees of today are still at work.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning before the valley heat becomes intense.
Bring plenty of water, a hat, and sun protection.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose soil and uneven slopes.
Check access locally since the mound sits among farmland.
Combine with the excavated Roman city at Beit She'an.
