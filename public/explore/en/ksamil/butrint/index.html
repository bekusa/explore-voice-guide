The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Frogs call from the reeds, and somewhere close a turtle slips into still green water. Under your feet the path is soft with fallen leaves from laurel and oak. Light comes through the trees in broken pieces. Ahead, grey stone walls sit low in the forest, half in shade. This is Butrint, a city that people lived in, rebuilt and finally left over more than two thousand years. Today it sits quietly on a small wooded peninsula in the far south of Albania.

Look at the setting first, because the setting explains everything. On one side is Lake Butrint, wide and calm. On the other side runs the Vivari Channel, a narrow waterway that links the lake to the Ionian Sea. Across the strait, on clear days, you can see the mountains of Corfu. A city here could watch the water, trade by boat and defend itself on high ground. That simple geography drew Greeks, Romans, Byzantines, Venetians and Ottomans, one after another.

The earliest story of Butrint is a legend, not a record. The Roman poet Virgil wrote in the Aeneid that Aeneas, a refugee from fallen Troy, stopped here on his journey west. In the poem he meets other Trojan exiles who had built a small copy of their lost city. Romans liked this tale, because it linked Butrint to their own founding myth. Archaeology tells a different story — a Greek settlement, connected to the local Chaonian people of the region, growing here over many centuries.

One of the first things you reach is the theatre, and it often surprises people. Its stone seats curve up against the slope of the hill. But the lowest part, where the stage and orchestra once stood, is frequently flooded. The ground here sits close to the water table, so the lake seeps in. In spring you may find the orchestra full of shallow water, with frogs and small turtles living where actors once stood. It is a strange and quiet sight.

Look carefully at the stone walls near the theatre entrances. Many blocks carry ancient Greek inscriptions, cut in neat rows. These are records of enslaved people being given their freedom. Writing these acts in public stone made them permanent and visible to the whole community. Most visitors walk past without noticing. Yet these lines hold the names of real people, and the moment their lives changed. It is one of the most human details in the whole site.

The theatre belonged to a larger sacred area dedicated to Asclepius, the Greek god of healing. People came here hoping for cures, and they left offerings. Healing sanctuaries were busy places, part hospital and part pilgrimage site. Around this area you will see the remains of small temples, a treasury and other buildings. The ground plan is broken now. But try to imagine visitors arriving by boat, tired and sick, climbing these same slopes with hope.

Under Roman rule, Butrint grew into a proper Roman town. It became a colony in the time of Julius Caesar and Augustus, and settlers arrived from Italy. The Romans built baths, houses, a forum and an aqueduct that carried fresh water across the channel. Butrint was never one of the great cities of the empire. It was a comfortable provincial town on a trade route. That ordinary character is part of its value — this is how many people actually lived.

Follow the path to the baptistery, one of Butrint's best-known buildings. It dates from the late Roman or early Christian period, around the sixth century. The floor holds a large mosaic of animals, birds and plants arranged in circles. For protection, the mosaic is usually kept covered with sand and cloth. It is only uncovered on rare occasions. So what you see on most days is a ring of columns around a quiet, sandy floor, with the real treasure just underneath.

Not far away stands the Great Basilica, a large early Christian church. Its tall walls still rise high above the path, and you can read the shape of the long nave and the curved apse at the far end. Light falls through the empty window openings. The building was repaired and changed in later centuries, so the stonework shows different phases. Notice how bricks and stone mix in the walls. Each change of material is a sign of a different period of repair.

Now walk toward the Lion Gate in the old city wall. Above the low doorway is a carved stone slab showing a lion attacking the head of a bull. The carving is older than the gate itself. Later builders found the slab and reused it here, as a lintel above the entrance. The doorway is so low that you need to lower your head to pass through. It is a small moment, but it connects your body to the people who walked here before.

After the classical period, Butrint shrank. The marshes spread, and malaria made life hard. In the medieval period the city became a smaller fortified town, and later the Venetians controlled this area. On the hilltop acropolis stands a castle rebuilt in modern times on older Venetian foundations. Today it holds a small museum with finds from the site. From up here the view opens over the lake, the channel and the fields toward the sea.

Look west from the hill toward the mouth of the channel. On the far bank you can see a low triangular fortress. It is linked to Ali Pasha, a powerful Ottoman-era ruler of the region in the early nineteenth century. Closer to the site, a small cable ferry carries cars and people across the Vivari. Modern study of Butrint began in the 1920s, when an Italian team led by Luigi Maria Ugolini started large excavations. Since 1992, Butrint has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The site is not only ruins. It is also a national park, with wetlands, reed beds and forest. Birds feed in the shallow water, and fishermen work on the lake. If you look out over Lake Butrint, you may see rows of posts in the water. These are used for farming mussels, a local trade that continues today. Butrint is a place where nature and history share the same ground, and neither one fully wins.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Notice the sound of the wind in the trees, and the smell of damp stone and water. Thousands of years of building are around you, but the forest is quietly moving back in. As you walk down toward the gate, look at the stones beside the path. Some are carved, some are plain. All of them were placed by human hands, and many people passed this way before you.
