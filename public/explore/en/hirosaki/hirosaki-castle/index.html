The guide

The story

Listen first to the gravel under your feet. It crunches softly as you cross the old grounds. Around you, the trees lean over dark water. The air here often smells of damp moss and pine. Somewhere ahead, past the moats and the wooden gates, a small white keep stands on a stone base. Welcome to Hirosaki Castle, in the far north of Japan's main island, Honshu.

This is the castle of the Tsugaru clan. They were a family of warlords who rose to power in the late sixteenth century. They held this northern region, also called Tsugaru, for about two and a half centuries. The founder, Tsugaru Tamenobu, planned a castle on this site. He did not live to see it finished. His son, Tsugaru Nobuhira, completed the work in 1611. The town of Hirosaki grew up around these walls.

The castle you see today is not the one Nobuhira built. His keep was much larger, with five storeys. In 1627, lightning struck it. The fire destroyed the tower completely. For almost two hundred years, the castle had no main keep at all. The clan governed from the grounds, but the tallest point stood empty. It is a surprising gap for a castle so closely tied to this city's identity.

The present keep went up in 1810. It is smaller, with three storeys. At the time, the government of the shogun strictly controlled castle building. A new keep was hard to justify. So, according to most accounts, the structure was officially treated as a corner turret, not a main keep. Whatever its official label, it became the keep in practice. And it has survived ever since.

That survival matters. Japan once had many castle keeps. Most were lost to fire, to deliberate demolition after 1868, or to bombing in the Second World War. Today, only twelve original keeps from the feudal age remain anywhere in the country. Hirosaki's is one of them. It is also the only original keep left in the Tohoku region, the whole northeast of Honshu. Many of the castles that tourists visit elsewhere are modern concrete rebuilds. This one is old timber.

Now look closely at the keep, and walk around it if you can. The sides that face the old outer grounds have decorative gables and projecting windows. They were made to impress anyone approaching. The other sides are much plainer. The builders put their effort where people would look. It is a small detail, and most visitors never notice it. Once you see it, the building feels almost like a stage set with a front and a back.

Here is a second surprise. In 2015, the entire keep was moved. Engineers lifted the building and slid it slowly across the grounds — about seventy metres in total. They did this so workers could repair the stone walls beneath it. Those walls had started to bulge outward over the centuries. The Japanese term for this moving method is hikiya. The stone wall project is a long one, spread over many years. Depending on when you visit, you may see the keep in a different spot, or see the repair work itself.

The stone walls deserve your attention too. Look at how the big stones fit together without mortar. The walls curve gently upward, steep at the top and wider at the base. This shape helps them stand through earthquakes. In places, you can see numbers or markings where stones were recorded during the repair. Each stone was measured, so it could go back in the right place.

Beyond the keep, other original structures survive. Several wooden gates still stand, with heavy beams and iron fittings. Three corner turrets also remain from the feudal period. These are low, white buildings at the edges of the grounds. They once let defenders watch the approaches. Today they give the park its quiet rhythm. You move from moat to gate to turret, and the castle slowly reveals its layout as a series of rings.

In 1868, the age of the samurai ended. The new Meiji government took over castle lands across Japan. Here, the grounds were opened to the public as a park in the 1890s. Cherry trees were planted in large numbers around that time. Today the park holds about 2,600 cherry trees. Among them, locals say, is the oldest Somei Yoshino cherry tree in Japan. Somei Yoshino is the pale pink variety most people picture when they think of Japanese blossoms.

In spring, usually late April into early May, the cherry blossom festival fills these grounds. Petals fall into the outer moat and drift together on the water. People here call these floating carpets hana-ikada, which means flower rafts. The moat can turn almost solid pink. Gardeners in Hirosaki are also known for their careful pruning of cherry trees. They use techniques borrowed from apple growing, the main local crop. That care helps the old trees keep flowering.

The castle is not only a spring place. In autumn, the maples turn red and orange along the moats. In winter, deep snow covers the roofs and stone walls. Each February, the grounds host a snow lantern festival. Small lanterns made of snow glow along the paths at night. On clear days in any season, look west for the shape of Mount Iwaki. Locals often call it Tsugaru Fuji, for its smooth volcanic cone.

One classic view is from the red wooden bridge over the inner moat. From there, the white keep rises above the water and the trees. In spring the frame fills with blossom. In winter, the red rail stands out against the snow. Take a moment there. The keep is small compared with the great castles further south. But its size is part of its charm. It feels human, close, and real.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen to the crows, the water, the wind in the branches. These moats were dug more than four hundred years ago. The stones under the keep have been lifted, counted, and set back by careful hands. As you walk out through the gates, notice the old timber beams above your head. They have watched people pass through here for centuries. Today, you are one of them.

What to look for

Decorative gables only on the keep's outward-facing sides.
Curved dry-stone walls rising steeply toward the top.
Fallen cherry petals forming pink rafts on the moat.
Red wooden bridge framing the white keep above water.
Mount Iwaki's volcanic cone on the western horizon.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit late April for the cherry blossom festival.
Walk fully around the keep to compare its faces.
Come in February for the snow lantern festival.
Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and stone steps.
Check locally where the keep currently stands during repairs.
