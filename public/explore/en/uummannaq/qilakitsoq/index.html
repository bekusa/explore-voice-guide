The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, the loudest sound is often the water. Small waves knock against the rocks, and somewhere out in the fjord an iceberg groans as it shifts. The air is cold and very dry. It catches in your throat, and it carries almost no smell at all. That dryness matters. It is the reason a few quiet graves on this stretch of shore became one of the best-known archaeological finds in the Arctic.

This is Qilakitsoq, an old settlement site across the water from Uummannaq, in northwest Greenland. At first glance, there is very little to see. You will find a rocky slope, some low ruins, and a sheltered spot under an overhanging rock face. But in 1972, two brothers out hunting in this area noticed something unusual among the stones. What they found would travel around the world and change how people imagined life in old Greenland.

Under the rock overhang were two graves, covered with flat stones. Inside lay eight people. Six were women, and two were children. One child was a small boy, perhaps around four years old. The other was an infant, only a few months old. They were not mummified on purpose. Nobody embalmed them. Instead, the cold, dry Arctic air and the shelter of the rock slowly dried their bodies out, much like fish or meat drying on a rack.

Scientists later dated the burials to the fifteenth century, around the 1470s. That puts them a few decades before Columbus crossed the Atlantic. These people belonged to the Thule culture. The Thule were the ancestors of today's Inuit in Greenland. They arrived from the west, from Arctic Canada, and they brought skin boats, dog sleds, and a deep knowledge of hunting seals and whales on and under the ice.

Here is the first thing most visitors do not expect. The clothing survived almost as well as the bodies. The women wore anoraks and trousers of sealskin, and some garments were made of bird skins, with the feathers turned inward for warmth. Researchers counted many separate pieces of clothing. The stitching is small and careful. Someone sat in a dim winter house and sewed every seam by hand, with sinew for thread.

The second surprise is invisible to the naked eye. When researchers photographed the women's faces with infrared light, fine lines appeared on their foreheads, cheeks, and chins. These were tattoos. In traditional Inuit culture, women were tattooed with a needle and a thread soaked in soot, pulled under the skin. The patterns may have marked adulthood or beauty. On the dried skin they had faded from sight, but the infrared camera brought them back.

The infant is the face most people remember. A photograph of the baby, with dark wisps of hair, round cheeks, and a fur hood, appeared in magazines around the world in the 1980s. It is hard to look at that small face without feeling something. This was not a pharaoh or a king. This was simply a baby from a hunting family, wrapped up against the cold by people who loved it.

Why were these eight people buried together? Honestly, nobody knows for certain. Researchers studied the bodies closely, looking for signs of illness or injury. Some of the women seem to be related to each other. Beyond that, there are theories, but no clear answer. There is an old Inuit custom, recorded in later times, that a very young child might be buried with its mother if she died. Whether that happened here cannot be proven.

After their discovery, the mummies were studied for several years by teams in Denmark and Greenland. In time, they came home. Today, the best-preserved bodies are on display at the Greenland National Museum in Nuuk, the capital, far to the south of here. So the people themselves are not at this site. What remains is the place where their families chose to lay them down, and the landscape they knew.

Take a moment to look at the ground around you. The Thule people built their winter houses partly into the earth, with walls of stone and turf, and roofs held up by driftwood or whale bone. Over five centuries, many of these houses have collapsed into low mounds and hollows. Grass and moss grow over them now. If you see a shallow depression ringed with stones, you may be looking at the outline of someone's home.

Now turn toward the water and the mountains. Across the fjord rises Uummannaq's mountain, the steep, reddish peak that gives the town its name. The name is usually explained as meaning something like heart-shaped. The people buried here would have seen that same outline every day. They watched the same light change on its face, and they followed the same seasons of open water and solid ice.

Those seasons still shape how you reach this place. In summer, visitors usually come by boat, weaving between drifting icebergs. In winter, when the sea freezes hard enough, travel across the ice by dog sled or snowmobile becomes possible. Either way, the journey itself tells you something. It shows how life here has always depended on reading the ice, the weather, and the water.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Feel that dry, cold air on your face again. It is the same air that kept these eight people intact for more than five hundred years. Look at the rocks, the low mounds in the grass, and the white shapes of ice out in the fjord. Then, as you walk back toward the shore, you may notice how quiet your own footsteps sound here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Greenland National Museum in Nuuk to see the mummies.
Arrange boat or ice transport through local operators in Uummannaq.
Wear windproof layers; the shore here is exposed and cold.
Treat the graves and ruins as burial ground; do not move stones.
Check sea ice and weather conditions before any winter trip.
