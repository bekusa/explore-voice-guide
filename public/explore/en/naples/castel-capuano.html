The guide

The story

Listen first. Scooters buzz past the old city gate. A fruit seller calls out from a cart. Beneath it all is a deeper quiet, the quiet of thick stone. In front of you stands a long, pale fortress, more palace than castle now, with rows of windows and a broad, formal doorway. This is Castel Capuano, at the eastern edge of the historic centre of Naples, and for most of its life it has been a place where people came to be judged.

The building takes its name from its neighbour. A few steps away stands Porta Capuana, the gate where the old road left Naples and headed north toward the town of Capua. For centuries, travellers, merchants and armies entered the city here. Whoever controlled this corner controlled one of the main doors into Naples. So it makes sense that the city's new rulers chose this spot when they wanted a stronghold of their own.

Those rulers were the Normans. They were warriors whose families came from northern France, and in the eleventh and twelfth centuries they built a kingdom across southern Italy and Sicily. The castle went up in the twelfth century, under the Norman king William the First. It is one of the oldest castles in Naples, older than the better-known Castel Nuovo down by the harbour. When you look at the calm façade today, it is hard to imagine it as a military building, but it began as one.

After the Normans came the Swabians, a German dynasty. Its most famous member was the emperor Frederick the Second, a ruler known for his curiosity, his laws and his court full of poets and scholars. Under the Swabians the castle was enlarged and used as a royal residence. Kings slept here, held court here and received visitors here. This corner of the city, which now feels slightly off the main tourist path, was once close to the centre of royal power.

Then power moved. In the thirteenth century the French house of Anjou took the kingdom. Charles of Anjou built a new fortress near the sea, and that is why it is still called Castel Nuovo, the New Castle. Castel Capuano did not disappear. It stayed in use for royal guests, family members and ceremonies. But the heart of the court shifted toward the harbour, and the old castle slowly changed its role.

The great change came in the sixteenth century. Naples was then ruled by Spain, through viceroys sent from Madrid. One of the most powerful was Pedro de Toledo, who reshaped large parts of the city. He gathered the scattered courts of the kingdom into this single building. From then on, Castel Capuano was the palace of justice. Naples people called it the Vicaria, from the name of one of its main courts.

Think about what that meant. Judges, lawyers, clerks and notaries filled the rooms. Petitioners waited on the stairs. The prisons were placed in the lower levels, under the courtrooms. Here is a detail many visitors miss: the word Vicaria became so linked with crowds and noise that in Neapolitan speech it came to suggest chaos and shouting. A building made for order gave its name to disorder.

Look up at the main entrance. Above the doorway you can see a large carved coat of arms. It belongs to the Spanish Habsburg rulers, the emperor Charles the Fifth and his line, who governed Naples in that period. It is a clear message in stone. The law inside this building was the king's law, and the doorway reminded everyone who walked through it where authority came from.

There is a darker story connected to the entrance. According to popular accounts, a stone column once stood near the courts. Debtors who could not pay were shown there in public, as a form of shame. Locals still tell the story with a mix of humour and discomfort. Whatever the exact details, it reminds us that justice here was not only paperwork. It was also public, physical and sometimes harsh.

The inside of the building holds a second surprise. Behind the plain exterior there are richly decorated rooms. The best known is a chapel painted in the sixteenth century, with frescoes covering its walls and ceiling. Other halls hold marble busts of famous lawyers and judges of Naples, rows of serious faces looking down at you. If you can get inside, you will see how much colour and ceremony hid behind these sober walls.

The courts stayed here for more than four hundred years. Only in the late twentieth century did the main law courts move to a modern business district in the east of the city. Since then, the castle has been used for legal training, conferences and cultural events. Access to the interior depends on these events, so the doors are not always open. Even from the outside, though, the building tells its long story clearly.

Now turn around and take in the square. This is a working part of Naples. You will see market stalls, small shops, buses and families walking home. The old gate stands nearby, with its white stone arch between two dark towers. Together, the gate and the castle form one of the oldest scenes in the city, and they still mark the point where Naples meets the road to the north.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the line of windows, the heavy doorway and the coat of arms above it. Kings lived behind these walls. Later, ordinary people came here to face judges, and some never walked out free. Today, the stone is simply warm in the afternoon sun. As you walk away along the busy street, listen again to the traffic and the voices. They are the same kind of sounds that filled this square nearly nine hundred years ago.
