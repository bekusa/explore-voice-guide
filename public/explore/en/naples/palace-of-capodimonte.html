The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The traffic of Naples is down there somewhere, a low hum far below. Up here you hear gravel under shoes, birds in the holm oaks, and wind moving across a long, deep-red façade trimmed in dark grey stone. The air feels a few degrees cooler than in the streets. This is Capodimonte. The name simply means the top of the mountain, and you are standing on it.

The palace began with an inheritance. In the 1730s, a young Bourbon king named Charles arrived to rule Naples. Through his mother, Elisabetta Farnese, he had inherited one of the great art collections of Europe. It had been gathered over generations by the Farnese, a powerful family whose members included a pope. Charles brought these treasures south to Naples. Then he needed a building large enough to hold them.

Construction started in 1738. The site was a hill the royal family already used for hunting, thick with woods and full of birds. The original plan was for a hunting lodge. The plan soon grew into a royal residence and a home for the collection. It also grew slowly — very slowly. Work went on for more than a century. Several kings came and went before the last wings were complete in the nineteenth century.

Look at the walls in front of you. The red plaster is set against bands of piperno, a grey volcanic stone quarried in this region. You will see the same pairing all over Naples. It is almost the city's signature. The building is long and calm, with rows of tall windows and very little decoration. It looks more like a well-ordered administrative palace than a fantasy castle. That restraint is deliberate. The real spectacle was meant to be inside.

And inside, the scale of the collection is hard to take in on one visit. Today the palace is the Museo di Capodimonte, one of the largest art museums in Italy. It opened as a national museum in 1957. The galleries hold paintings from the medieval period through the eighteenth century. Many visitors arrive expecting a regional museum. They leave having seen works they knew only from art history books.

Here is the first surprise for many people. Some of the most important Renaissance portraits anywhere hang in these rooms. Titian painted Pope Paul III, the Farnese pope, more than once, and his portraits of the pope and his family are here. In one, the old man sits hunched and alert, his eyes sharp. Nearby is Parmigianino's portrait known as Antea — a young woman in a rich gown, staring straight at you. Nobody knows for certain who she was. That mystery is part of her pull.

The collection reaches north of the Alps too. Pieter Bruegel the Elder, the Flemish painter of peasants and parables, is represented by two works. One is The Blind Leading the Blind. A line of blind men stumbles forward, and the leader is already falling into a ditch. Each man behind him is one step closer to the same fate. Stand in front of it and follow the line with your eyes. You can see the fall happening in slow motion.

Then there is Caravaggio. His Flagellation of Christ was painted for a church in Naples and now hangs in this museum. Caravaggio worked in the city while on the run after killing a man in Rome. His Naples paintings are dark and direct. Bodies come out of black shadow under a hard, single light. Not far away, you may also find Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith Slaying Holofernes, one of several versions of a scene she returned to again and again.

The second surprise is upstairs, in the royal apartments. These rooms show how the Bourbon court lived, with furniture, chandeliers, and painted ceilings. One small room is covered almost entirely in porcelain — walls, mirrors, and decoration. It is the porcelain salon made for Queen Maria Amalia. It was first installed in the royal palace at Portici, near Vesuvius, and later moved here. Look closely and you will find garlands, fruit, and scenes in an Oriental style, all made of glazed ceramic.

That porcelain is connected to this hill. In the 1740s, King Charles and Maria Amalia founded a royal porcelain factory in the grounds. Its pieces became known simply as Capodimonte porcelain. Even today, the name is linked with delicate figurines and flowers in fine white ceramic. So this hill gave its name to a whole craft. You may notice porcelain displays throughout the apartments, a reminder of that local industry.

The museum does not stop in the past. The top floors hold modern and contemporary art. One work fits this city especially well. Andy Warhol, the American pop artist, made a series of paintings of Vesuvius erupting in the 1980s, and one of them is here. It is loud, bright, and almost cartoonish. After hours among saints and popes, it is a strange and welcome jolt. It also reminds you that the volcano is still a living presence for Neapolitans.

When your eyes are tired, step outside into the royal park, the Real Bosco di Capodimonte. It is enormous, far larger than the palace itself. Long, straight avenues run out through the trees, some of them spreading from one point like the ribs of a fan. Neapolitan families come here to walk, jog, and picnic. On weekends you will hear children, dogs, and conversations in dialect. It feels less like a royal estate and more like the city's back garden.

Before you leave, find a spot near the edge of the hill where the view opens up. On a clear day, the roofs of Naples fall away towards the bay, and Vesuvius sits on the horizon. Listen again to that far-off hum of the city. Notice the smell of pine and cut grass, and the red wall warming in the sun behind you. Kings built this place to look down on Naples. Today anyone can stand here and do the same.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan several hours; the galleries span multiple large floors.
Pick a few key paintings first to avoid gallery fatigue.
Save time for a walk in the royal park afterwards.
Come on a clear day for views towards Vesuvius.
Wear comfortable shoes for long galleries and gravel avenues.
