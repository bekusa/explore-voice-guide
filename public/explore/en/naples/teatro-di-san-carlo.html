The guide

The story

Stand for a moment under the stone portico and listen. Scooters buzz along Via San Carlo. Footsteps echo from the glass arcade of the Galleria across the street. Then you push through the doors, and the noise of Naples simply stops. Inside, the air feels still and warm. The light turns soft and golden. Somewhere beyond the foyer, behind heavy doors, there is a room built for one purpose: to carry a single human voice to every corner of a vast hall.

This is the Teatro di San Carlo, and it carries a remarkable title. It is the oldest opera house in Europe that is still active today. It opened in 1737. That is more than forty years before Milan's La Scala, and more than fifty years before La Fenice in Venice. When people in those cities first heard opera in their great new theatres, Naples had already filled this hall for generations.

The theatre was a royal project. Charles of Bourbon had recently become king of Naples. He was young and ambitious, and he wanted his capital to match the great courts of Europe. An opera house was a statement of power in the eighteenth century, much as a stadium or a museum might be today. So he ordered a grand new theatre, built directly beside his royal palace. The work moved fast. The building rose in a matter of months.

The king chose the opening night with care. The theatre opened on 4 November, the feast day of Saint Charles. That was the king's own name day, and it also gave the theatre its name. So the house is named for a saint, and also, quietly, for the king who built it. The first performance was an opera by Domenico Sarro, a Neapolitan composer of the time. Royal pride and local talent shared that first stage.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The hall around you is not the original one. In 1816, a fire broke out and gutted the interior. For a city that loved music this much, it was a disaster. But the response was astonishing. Under the architect Antonio Niccolini, the theatre was rebuilt in roughly a year. The new hall kept the spirit of the old one, but with fresh decoration and a new sense of grandeur.

Visitors who came soon after were amazed. The French writer Stendhal attended the reopening, and he wrote about it with great enthusiasm. He described a sense of being dazzled, almost overwhelmed, by the room. Even today, when you first walk into the auditorium, it is easy to understand why. The space is designed to make you stop in the doorway and look up.

So let us look. The hall is shaped like a horseshoe. Tier upon tier of boxes rise around you, each one framed in gold. The walls and seats glow in deep red velvet. This horseshoe shape is a classic Italian design. It lets the audience see the stage, but it also lets the audience see each other. In earlier centuries, that second part mattered just as much. A night at the opera was a social event.

Now find the royal box. It sits at the centre of the horseshoe, facing the stage directly. It is larger than the others and crowned with a great gilded crown. From here, the king could see everything, and everyone could see the king. It is said that some boxes once held small mirrors, so people could watch the royal box and react when the king reacted. Whether every story is true or not, the room was clearly built around that one seat.

Raise your eyes to the ceiling. A large painted scene spreads across it, created after the fire by Giuseppe Cammarano. It shows Apollo, the Greek god of music and poetry, with great poets gathered in his company. Then look at the stage curtain, if it is lowered. It is painted too, with a crowd of figures from art and literature. Together, ceiling and curtain tell the audience one clear idea: this room belongs to the arts.

In the early nineteenth century, this theatre was one of the most important musical places in Europe. A powerful impresario named Domenico Barbaja ran the house. He brought the young Gioachino Rossini to Naples, and Rossini wrote several operas for this stage. Later, Gaetano Donizetti worked here too. His opera Lucia di Lammermoor, with its famous mad scene, had its premiere here in 1835. Composers knew that success in Naples could make a career.

The theatre survived more than fire. During the Second World War, bombs damaged parts of the building. Yet the house reopened remarkably quickly once the fighting moved on, and music returned to the hall. That pattern repeats through its long life. Damage comes, and Naples rebuilds. The building you stand in is the result of many hands over many years, all working to keep the same stage alive.

The theatre is also part of a wider stage. Step outside and you are a short walk from the Royal Palace and the wide open space of Piazza del Plebiscito. Across the street rises the Galleria Umberto the First, with its iron and glass roof. This whole area is part of the historic centre of Naples, which is listed by UNESCO, the United Nations cultural agency. The opera house was always meant to sit at the heart of royal Naples.

Today the theatre still presents opera, ballet and concerts, and it has its own ballet school with a long tradition. If you are not here for a performance, guided tours often let you into the auditorium and the royal box. Try to sit, even for a minute, in one of the red seats. Notice how quiet the hall feels, and how a small sound from the stage travels easily to you.

Before you leave, take one last look around the horseshoe. Picture the candles of the nineteenth century, the rustle of silk, the hush before the first note. Then walk back out under the portico. The scooters and the voices of the street will return almost at once. But listen closely as you go. Naples is a city of sound, and this room has been listening to it for nearly three hundred years.
