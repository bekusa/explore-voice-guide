The guide

The story

Start with the light. On the high plateau of central Brazil, the sun hits white stone with very little to soften it. The marble of this palace glows so brightly at midday that many visitors narrow their eyes before they reach the edge of the reflecting pool. The air is often dry here, and the sky feels wide and close. Somewhere behind you, traffic hums along the broad avenues. In front of you, a long, low white building seems to float just above the ground.

This is the Palácio do Planalto, the official workplace of the President of Brazil. The name means the Palace of the Plateau, after the Central Plateau on which the whole city stands. The president does not live here. The official residence is a separate palace by the lake. This building is the office: the place where decrees are signed, ministers are received, and visiting heads of state are welcomed. Think of it as the working desk of the nation.

To understand why it looks this way, you need to know the story of the city around it. For generations, Brazil's capital sat on the Atlantic coast, in Rio de Janeiro. Moving the capital inland was an old dream, written into Brazil's constitution long before anyone built it. In the late 1950s, President Juscelino Kubitschek made the dream real. In only a few years, a capital rose from almost empty savanna. It was inaugurated in 1960, and this palace was among its first great buildings.

Two men shaped what you see. The urban planner Lúcio Costa drew the overall plan of Brasília, a layout that many people compare to an airplane or a bird with open wings. The architect Oscar Niemeyer designed the major public buildings, including this one. Niemeyer loved free, flowing curves, and he worked closely with the engineer Joaquim Cardozo, who made those daring shapes stand up. The city as a whole is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, protected as a complete modernist plan.

Now look at the columns along the facade. They are the signature of this building. Each one is thin where it meets the ground and where it meets the roof, and wider in the middle, with a shape that bends gently like a sail or a folded ribbon. They seem almost to touch the earth on their tips. Because of this, the heavy building looks light. Niemeyer wanted the structure to appear as if it barely rested on the plateau.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. If you have seen the presidential residence by the lake, you may notice its columns look related, but they are not the same. Niemeyer gave each palace its own version of the idea. Walk slowly along the front of this building and watch how the columns change as your angle changes. From the side, they line up into a rhythm of white curves. From straight ahead, they open like a row of arches.

Next, find the ramp. A long, gentle ramp leads from the plaza up to the main entrance. It is not only a practical way in. It is a stage for one of the most important moments in Brazilian political life. On inauguration day, the newly sworn president walks up this ramp in public view, and the presidential sash is passed on at the top. The ramp turns a transfer of power into something the whole country can watch.

In front of the palace lies the Praça dos Três Poderes, the Square of the Three Powers. The name is a lesson in civics. On one side stands this palace, the seat of the executive. Across the plaza is the Supreme Federal Court, the judiciary. Nearby rise the twin towers and bowl-shaped domes of the National Congress, the legislature. Costa placed the three branches of government facing one another across open ground, so that power is balanced in stone as well as in law.

While you are in the plaza, look for the bronze sculpture of two tall, slender figures holding long poles. It is often called Os Candangos, by the sculptor Bruno Giorgi. Candangos is the name Brazilians gave to the workers who came from across the country, especially from the poor northeast, to build Brasília with their hands. They lived in rough camps and worked at great speed. The sculpture is a tribute to them, standing in the heart of the power their labour made possible.

The guards at the palace are another detail worth your attention. The presidential guard includes the Dragões da Independência, the Dragoons of Independence, a historic cavalry unit with ceremonial uniforms that carry the memory of the early nineteenth century, when Brazil became independent from Portugal. Their changing of the guard is a small ceremony, but it brings colour and movement to the clean white lines of the architecture. Watch quietly from the permitted distance and respect the barriers.

This open, symbolic space has also seen hard days. On 8 January 2023, crowds stormed the buildings of all three powers here, including this palace, and caused serious damage to rooms, furniture and works of art. The images shocked the country. Repairs and restorations followed, and the buildings returned to their work. It is a reminder that this plaza is not a museum piece. It is a living place where Brazil's democracy is tested and defended.

Before you leave, take a moment to see the whole composition. Stand back far enough that the palace, the reflecting water, and the open plaza fit into one view. Notice how flat the horizon is, and how the sky takes up most of the picture. Brasília was designed for this kind of distance. The buildings are meant to be seen across empty space, like sculptures on a vast table, rather than squeezed between other streets.

Now look again at those white columns, touching the ground so lightly. Listen to the wind across the plaza and the distant traffic on the avenues. A little over six decades ago, this was open scrubland. Today it is the place where a nation of more than two hundred million people is governed. As you walk away, turn back once more. Watch how the light moves across the marble, and how the palace seems, for a moment, to rise off the plateau.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to avoid the harsh midday glare.
Bring water, sunscreen and a hat for the open plaza.
Check in advance whether guided interior visits are running.
Stand far back across the pool for a full-facade photo.
Combine your visit with Congress and the Supreme Court.
