The guide

The story

Feel the air first. It is heavy with salt and heat, and it settles on your skin almost as soon as you stop walking. Somewhere close by, water laps against stone. The light here is hard and white, and it bounces off pale walls until you have to narrow your eyes. Under your feet the ground is dusty and warm. You are standing at the edge of the Red Sea, in front of a small and modest place of prayer. Its story reaches back further than its size suggests.

First, a little about where you are. Massawa is Eritrea's old port city. It is spread across islands and a stretch of coast, linked by causeways. For centuries, ships carried goods and people between Africa, Arabia, and the wider Indian Ocean through this harbour. Different powers have controlled it, including the Ottomans and later the Egyptians. Their influence is still visible in the old town, with its arches, carved wooden balconies, and thick-walled merchant houses built for the heat.

Now to the name. This building is known as the Mosque of the Companions. In Islam, the Companions are the people who knew the Prophet Muhammad during his lifetime and followed him. Islamic sources describe a difficult period in the early seventh century. Early Muslims in Mecca faced persecution for their new faith. A group of them was told to seek safety across the Red Sea, in the land Arabic sources call Abyssinia — the region of present-day Ethiopia and Eritrea.

This journey is often called the First Hijra, which means the first migration. It happened before the better-known migration of the Prophet himself to Medina. According to the traditional accounts, the refugees were welcomed by a Christian king, remembered in Arabic as the Najashi. He ruled the kingdom of Aksum, a powerful trading state in the Horn of Africa. The story is remembered warmly by Muslims to this day. It is a story of a Christian ruler giving shelter to Muslims in need.

Here is where Massawa enters the story. Local tradition holds that some of these early Companions came ashore on this coast. Locals say they built a simple place of prayer here before travelling on. That is why the mosque carries their name. It is important to be honest about this part. It is a living tradition, passed down and deeply valued, rather than a fact proven by surviving records or excavation.

Because of that tradition, many people describe this as one of the oldest mosque sites in Africa, and some call it the very oldest. Historians are more cautious. Other places in the region, including a site in northern Ethiopia linked to the same migration, also hold strong claims. What is clear is that this coast was one of the first places where Islam touched the African continent. The sea you can hear right now was the road it travelled.

There is one more detail that many visitors miss. Every mosque has a qibla, the direction of prayer. Today that direction points towards the Kaaba in Mecca. But in the earliest years of Islam, Muslims prayed towards Jerusalem. According to one popular account, the original prayer direction here faced Jerusalem, which would fit a very early date. Treat this as part of the legend. It is a striking idea, and it explains why the site matters so much to those who visit.

Now look at the building itself. What you see today is not a seventh-century structure. Like most buildings on this coast, it has been repaired and rebuilt many times. Salt, heat, and time are hard on any wall here. The mosque is small and plain, with whitewashed surfaces and simple openings. There is no grand dome or long courtyard to impress you. Its importance comes from the memory attached to this spot, not from scale or decoration.

Take a closer look at the stone of Massawa in general. Many old buildings in the town are made from coral stone, cut from the reefs and shoreline. It is pale, rough, and full of tiny holes. If you look closely at exposed blocks around the old town, you can sometimes see the patterns of ancient sea life pressed into the surface. The material is light and easy to work, but the salty air slowly eats into it. That is why fresh plaster and patching appear everywhere.

Turn towards the water for a moment. The Arabian Peninsula lies across the Red Sea, on the far side of that horizon. For the refugees in the old stories, that crossing was dangerous and uncertain. For traders over many centuries, it was a regular route. Standing here, you can understand why ideas, languages, and faiths moved so easily between these two shores. The sea did not divide them. It connected them.

Massawa has also lived through hard times in more recent history. During Eritrea's long war for independence in the late twentieth century, the town was heavily damaged. As you walk through the old streets, you may still notice scarred walls and buildings that stand empty or half repaired. Some structures have been restored, while others wait. That mix of damage and survival is part of the character of the town today.

A practical note — this is a place of worship, not a museum. People may come here to pray, and the mosque belongs first to them. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. Be ready to remove your shoes if you are invited inside. If you are unsure whether you may enter, ask politely. A respectful question is usually welcomed, and local people may share their own version of the story with you.

Listen for a moment. If you are here at the right time, you may hear the call to prayer from the town. It travels across the rooftops and the water. In the late afternoon, the hard light softens and turns golden on the pale walls. The heat eases a little, and the harbour grows quieter. These small changes are part of the visit too.

Before you leave, look once more at this small, simple building against the wide blue water. It is easy to walk past without a second glance. Yet the memory kept here goes back to some of the earliest days of a world faith, and to an act of welcome across the sea. As you walk away, notice the coral walls around you, the salt on the air, and the boats in the harbour. The old crossing is still part of this place.
