The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the hum of traffic you can hear the Arkansas River moving past, slow and brown and wide. Above it, a long box of glass and steel reaches out from the south bank. It seems to hang in the air. The light comes off the river and lands on the glass, so the building takes on the colour of the sky. On a grey morning it looks almost silver. At sunset it turns warm and gold.

This is the Clinton Presidential Center in Little Rock, Arkansas. It holds the museum, library and archives of Bill Clinton, the forty-second President of the United States. Clinton grew up in Arkansas and served as the state's governor before he went to the White House in the 1990s. So this is a homecoming of sorts. A national institution stands in the capital of the small Southern state that first sent him into public life.

Look at the shape again. The main hall is long, narrow and lifted off the ground, and its far end extends out toward the water. The designers wanted it to suggest a bridge. That idea is deliberate. During his 1996 campaign, Clinton spoke again and again about building a bridge to the twenty-first century. The architects, the New York firm Polshek Partnership, turned that phrase into steel and glass. The building is a political slogan you can walk through.

Not everyone loved the design at first. Locals sometimes joke that the long, raised box looks like a very elegant mobile home. Clinton himself has been known to laugh along with the comparison. It is worth knowing that joke before you go inside, because once you hear it, it is hard to forget. It also says something about this place. It is serious and grand, but it is also very much part of a town with a sense of humour about itself.

The Center opened in November 2004. The dedication day was cold and soaked with rain. Even so, a large crowd stood outside under umbrellas and plastic ponchos. Several former and current presidents sat on the stage together, and a sitting president spoke kindly about a predecessor from the other party. For many people who were there, the weather is the part they remember best. The rain did not stop. The ceremony carried on anyway.

Inside, the main exhibit hall is the heart of the museum. Down the centre runs a timeline of the Clinton years, one panel for each year of the two terms. Along both sides are alcoves, small bays that each focus on a single theme. You will find the economy, foreign policy, health care, education and more. You can walk the timeline straight through for the big picture. Or you can step sideways into an alcove and slow right down.

Now look up, above the alcoves. Rows of blue archival boxes rise in tiers toward the ceiling. Here is the first surprise. The design of this hall was inspired by the Long Room of Trinity College Library in Dublin, a famous old library hall lined with towering shelves of books. Here, instead of leather spines, you see archive boxes. They are a visible reminder that a presidential library is, at its core, a place that keeps records.

And the records here are vast. The archives hold millions of documents, photographs, emails and objects from the Clinton administration. They are cared for by the National Archives, the federal agency that looks after the official papers of the United States government. Researchers come here to study decisions made during the 1990s. Most visitors never see the research rooms. But every display in the museum depends on that quiet work happening behind the scenes.

One of the most popular stops is the full-scale replica of the Oval Office, the president's working office in the White House. It is furnished as it looked during the Clinton years. Nearby is a replica of the Cabinet Room, where the president met with his senior advisers. Standing in these rooms gives you a sense of scale. The real spaces are smaller than many people expect from television. They feel more like a well-kept office than a throne room.

The museum also holds gifts that foreign leaders and ordinary citizens gave to the president. Some are formal and precious. Others are homemade and personal, made by people who simply wanted to send something. One of the larger objects on display is a presidential limousine, armoured and heavy and very black. Children and grandparents alike tend to stop beside it for a photo. It is a small piece of the machinery that surrounds an American president.

The Center is more than one building. It sits inside a riverside park with lawns, walking paths and planted wetlands near the water. Next door, a former railroad station has been restored. It now houses the Clinton School of Public Service, which trains students for careers in public work. The old station is a nice contrast. Its brick and stone feel solid and rooted, while the museum floats above the ground in glass.

Here is the second surprise. Look east along the river and you will see an old railroad bridge, its steel trusses crossing the water. Trains once ran across it. Today it has been turned into a bridge for people on foot and on bicycles. It connects the park to North Little Rock on the far bank. So the bridge idea does not stop with the museum. There is a real bridge here, and you can walk across it.

If you have time, walk a short way west into the River Market District. This area was once full of warehouses and loading docks, and much of it sat empty for years. The arrival of the Center helped bring new life to this part of downtown. Today you will find restaurants, a farmers market hall and the river trail. The museum changed the way the city faces its own river.

Before you leave, stand on the lawn and look back at the long glass hall. Notice how it points toward the water, and how the old railroad bridge echoes it in the distance. Listen again for the river. Presidents come and go, and opinions about them shift with each generation. The river keeps moving past, the same as it did long before any of this was built. Take one more look, and then follow the path wherever it leads you.
