The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere above you, on a slope of grey scree, a sharp whistle cuts through the air. It is a marmot, a fat golden-brown rodent, warning its family that you have arrived. The air smells of cold stone, wet grass, and the faint sweetness of wild thyme. Underfoot, the path is a mix of loose gravel and polished rock. Welcome to the Vanoise, the high country of Savoie in the French Alps.

This is the oldest national park in France. It was created in 1963, at a time when the Alps were changing fast. Ski resorts were spreading across the valleys, and roads were climbing higher every year. The park was a decision to keep one large piece of these mountains as they were. More than sixty years later, the decision still shapes almost everything you see around you.

Take a look at the shape of the land. The Vanoise sits between two great valleys of Savoie. To the north lies the Tarentaise, the valley of the Isère river. To the south lies the Maurienne, the valley of the Arc. Between them rises a massif of peaks, glaciers, and high plateaus. The highest summit is the Grande Casse, at 3,855 metres. On a clear day, its pale ice face is easy to pick out from many trails.

Now, here is the first thing most visitors do not realise. The park has two parts. There is a protected core, where the strictest rules apply. And there is a much wider surrounding zone, which includes villages, farms, and even some of the best-known ski areas in the world. So the same mountain region holds both quiet wilderness and some of the busiest winter resorts in Europe. The border between them is often just a sign on a trail.

The real reason this park exists has horns. It is the Alpine ibex, a wild mountain goat with long, ridged horns that curve back over its shoulders. By the early twentieth century, hunting had nearly wiped it out across the Alps. A small population survived just across the border in Italy, in a former royal hunting reserve that later became Gran Paradiso National Park. Protecting the ibex on the French side was one of the founding goals of the Vanoise.

It worked. Today ibex are a common sight here, especially in early summer, when they come down to graze on fresh grass. They are surprisingly calm around people. Do not let that fool you, though. These animals can stand on ledges that look impossible, and climb rock faces that would stop a trained climber. If you see one balanced on a cliff, take a moment. You are watching a species that almost disappeared.

That Italian connection still matters. The Vanoise shares a long border with Gran Paradiso, and the two parks are officially twinned. Together they form one of the largest protected mountain areas in Western Europe. The ibex and the chamois, a smaller and shyer mountain antelope, do not care about national borders. They move freely between the two countries. So, in a sense, the wilderness around you does not end at the frontier. It simply continues in another language.

Here is a second surprise. In the late 1960s and early 1970s, a plan to build ski runs on land inside the protected core caused a national argument in France. Many people saw it as a test of whether a national park really meant anything. The protest spread far beyond Savoie, and the project inside the core was dropped. Many historians see that moment as an early turning point for the environmental movement in France.

Now look down at your feet, especially if you are walking in June or July. The meadows here fill with colour. You may see deep blue gentians, small purple orchids, yellow arnica, and, on the higher rocky ground, the pale woolly star of edelweiss. In the core of the park, picking flowers is not allowed. The plants have a very short growing season at this altitude. A single picked flower is one less chance for seeds the following year.

The human story of the Vanoise is older than the park. For centuries, families in these valleys moved their cows and sheep up to high summer pastures, called alpages. You can still see stone huts and old barns along many trails. Some pastures are still used today. The milk from these mountains goes into Beaufort, a firm, nutty cheese that is a pride of Savoie. If you hear cowbells somewhere below you, that tradition is still alive.

The park is also a place for walkers who like to stay overnight. A network of mountain refuges, staffed huts with bunk rooms and shared meals, sits along the main routes. Long-distance trails cross the massif, linking valley villages such as Pralognan-la-Vanoise with high passes and glacier views. Evenings in a refuge are simple. There is a long wooden table, a hot soup, and walkers comparing which animals they saw that day.

The glaciers deserve a closer look too. The Vanoise holds some of the largest glacier areas in the French Alps. But they are shrinking. Old photographs show ice reaching much lower than it does today. On some trails, you can see the bare grey rock and loose moraine, the piles of stone that glaciers push and leave behind, marking where the ice once stood. These mountains show the changing climate very clearly to anyone who stops and looks.

So, before you move on, stand still for a moment. Look at the ridgeline above you and scan it slowly. A shape that looked like a rock may suddenly lift its head. That could be an ibex or a chamois. Listen again for the marmot whistle, the cowbells, the sound of meltwater running under the stones. This landscape was protected so that moments like this one would still be possible. As you walk on, keep your eyes on the high ground... and your steps on the path.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in early summer to see ibex grazing lower down.
Leave dogs at home; they are banned in the core.
Book mountain refuges ahead for overnight stays in summer.
Carry binoculars to scan ridgelines for chamois and ibex.
Pack warm layers; weather changes quickly at high altitude.
