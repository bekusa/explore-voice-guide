The guide

The story

Listen first. Trams rattle along the boulevard behind you, and then, a few steps into the garden, the noise softens. Gravel crunches under your shoes. Ahead, a wide flight of stone steps climbs toward a row of tall columns. The pale facade catches the light, and pigeons settle on the ledges above. This is the Hungarian National Museum, and those steps are among the most meaningful in the country.

The museum began not as a building but as a gift. In 1802, Count Ferenc Széchényi gave his large private collection to the nation. It held books, manuscripts, maps, coins and engravings. His idea was simple and bold. Hungary needed a place to keep its own memory, owned by the public. That collection also became the seed of the National Széchényi Library, which today lives up in Buda Castle.

For decades the collection had no proper home. Then, in the 1830s, construction began on the building you see now. The architect was Mihály Pollack, a leading designer of the period. Work ran from 1837 to 1847. Pollack chose neoclassicism, a style that copies the temples of ancient Greece and Rome. Clean lines, symmetry, columns, a triangular pediment. In that era, this style signalled learning, reason and national ambition.

Look up at the portico, the covered porch at the top of the steps. Tall Corinthian columns hold it up. Corinthian columns are the ones with leafy, carved tops, like bundles of acanthus leaves. Above them sits the pediment, filled with sculpted figures. At the centre, a female figure represents Hungary. Around her stand allegories of the arts and sciences. The message is clear. A nation finds its strength in knowledge.

Now, the steps themselves. On the fifteenth of March, 1848, revolution spread through the streets of Pest. Young radicals called for freedom of the press and an end to old privileges. A crowd gathered here at the museum. Tradition holds that the poet Sándor Petőfi recited his National Song from these steps. Historians still debate exactly where he stood that day. But the story has become part of how Hungarians remember the revolution.

That memory is still alive. Every year on the fifteenth of March, Hungary holds a national holiday. Crowds come to this garden. Many people pin a small ribbon in red, white and green to their coats. Speeches are given from the steps, and the poem is recited again. So if you stand here in spring, you may be standing in the same spot as thousands of people who come to remember.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The building behind the museum, on Bródy Sándor Street, was once the headquarters of Hungarian Radio. On the twenty-third of October, 1956, crowds gathered there to demand that their demands be broadcast. Shots were fired, and the fighting that followed became the start of the armed uprising against Soviet rule. Today that date is also a national holiday. The museum stands at the edge of two of Hungary's turning points.

Walk inside, and the scale changes. The entrance hall is cool and echoing. Then the grand staircase rises in front of you. Lift your eyes to the ceiling. The paintings up there are by Károly Lotz and Mór Than, two of the most important Hungarian painters of the nineteenth century. Their figures show the history and culture of the nation, painted in warm, soft colours. Many people rush up the stairs without looking up. Take a moment.

The permanent exhibitions follow the long story of the land. They begin with the peoples who lived in the Carpathian Basin long before Hungarians arrived. That is the great lowland ringed by mountains, where Hungary sits today. You will find Roman finds, objects from migration-era peoples, and the arrival of the Hungarian tribes. Then the story moves through kings, wars, Ottoman rule, and the Habsburg centuries, toward modern times.

One object deserves special attention. The coronation mantle of the Hungarian kings is kept here. It is a richly embroidered silk robe, very old and very fragile. That is why it is shown in low light, behind protective glass. For many years the Holy Crown of Hungary was also displayed in this museum. In 2000 the crown moved to the Parliament building, but the mantle stayed here.

Do not skip the lower level. The Lapidarium holds carved stones, many from the Roman period. Roman Pannonia, the province that covered this region, had a large town just north of today's Budapest, called Aquincum. Tombstones, altars and inscriptions show how ordinary people lived and mourned here nearly two thousand years ago. It is quiet down there, and the stone feels cool and close.

When you come back outside, walk slowly around the garden. It is a small park, with old trees, benches and statues. Near the front stands a statue of János Arany, one of Hungary's greatest poets and a friend of Petőfi. Students from nearby universities cross the lawn with coffee in hand. The neighbourhood around you is called the Palace Quarter, named for the aristocratic town houses built along these streets in the nineteenth century.

Before you go, turn and face the steps once more. Notice how ordinary they look in daily life. People sit on them to rest, children climb them, tourists take photos. Yet these same stones carry the weight of a nation's founding hopes. As you walk back toward the boulevard and the trams, listen again. The city noise returns, and the museum stays behind you, pale and steady, holding its memory in silence.

Practical tips

Come on 15 March to see the national holiday gathering.
Save energy for the Roman stones in the lower level.
Pause on the grand staircase before heading into galleries.
Rest on a garden bench before walking the Palace Quarter.
Photograph the facade from the garden path for full symmetry.
