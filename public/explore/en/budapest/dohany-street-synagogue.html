The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, trams ring along the Grand Boulevard and traffic hums on Károly Boulevard. In front of you, there is a wall of brick in bands of red and pale yellow. Above it, two slim towers rise, each one topped by a green onion dome and a small golden crown. The street noise does not stop here. But the building in front of you somehow makes it feel farther away.

This is the Dohány Street Synagogue, often called the Great Synagogue. It is the largest synagogue in Europe, and one of the largest in the world. Inside, it holds close to 3,000 seats. The name comes from the street itself. Dohány means tobacco in Hungarian, a small reminder of the trades that once filled this neighbourhood on the edge of the old city of Pest.

The synagogue was built in the 1850s, and it opened in 1859. Its architect was Ludwig Förster, who came from Vienna. He chose a style that we now call Moorish Revival. That means it borrows shapes and patterns from the Islamic architecture of medieval Spain and North Africa. Many Jewish communities in Central Europe liked this style in the nineteenth century. It felt connected to an older, eastern past, and it looked clearly different from the Gothic churches around it.

Look again at the facade. The striped brickwork, the horseshoe arches, and the ceramic details all belong to that Moorish vocabulary. Above the main entrance, a large rose window sits between the towers. Hebrew letters run across the front, carrying a line from scripture. The two towers are worth a slow look. Some people see in them a quiet echo of the two pillars that, according to tradition, stood before the ancient Temple in Jerusalem.

This building tells you a lot about the community that paid for it. Budapest's Jewish population grew fast in the nineteenth century. Many families were merchants, doctors, lawyers, and publishers, and they wanted to be part of modern Hungarian life. The congregation here followed what became known as Neolog Judaism. That was a Hungarian reform movement — more modern than strict Orthodoxy, but still rooted in tradition. The building was meant to say, very publicly, that this community belonged in the heart of the city.

Here is one detail that surprises many visitors. Inside, there is an organ. Most synagogues do not have one, because traditional Jewish law discourages playing instruments on the Sabbath. Here, the organ was part of the Neolog approach. Some famous composers are said to have played it, and the name Franz Liszt often comes up in these stories. Treat that as a well-loved tale rather than firm record. Either way, the instrument shows how far this congregation was willing to go in shaping its own tradition.

When you step inside, the space opens up in a way that feels almost like a great cathedral. There is a long central nave, with galleries along both sides. In the traditional arrangement, women sat upstairs in those galleries, and men sat below. The ceiling and walls carry geometric patterns in gold, deep red, and blue. Light comes in through tall windows and falls across rows of dark wooden pews. At the far end stands the Ark, where the Torah scrolls are kept.

Now, a second surprise, and it is close by. The house where Theodor Herzl was born stood right next to this synagogue. Herzl later became the founder of modern political Zionism, the movement for a Jewish homeland. A plaque marks the connection. Today, the Hungarian Jewish Museum stands on that spot, joined to the synagogue complex. Its collection includes religious objects, textiles, and everyday items from Jewish life in Hungary over many generations.

The complex holds much darker memories as well. During the Second World War, in late 1944, the Budapest ghetto was created in this district, and the synagogue stood at its edge. That winter, conditions inside the ghetto were terrible. Thousands of people died from cold, hunger, and violence. There was no way to take them to a cemetery outside. So they were buried here, in the synagogue's own courtyard. Normally, Jewish tradition does not place graves beside a synagogue. This garden exists because history left no other choice.

Next to the main building, you will find the Heroes' Temple, a smaller synagogue added in the early 1930s. It was built to honour Hungarian Jewish soldiers who died in the First World War. It is a quieter, simpler space, and it is often used for services in the colder months, when heating the great hall is not practical. Walking from the vast main sanctuary into this smaller room feels like moving from a public voice to a private one.

Behind the synagogue lies the Raoul Wallenberg Memorial Park. Wallenberg was a Swedish diplomat who saved many thousands of Budapest's Jews in 1944 by issuing protective papers and sheltering people in safe houses. At the centre of the park stands a sculpture by the Hungarian artist Imre Varga. It is a weeping willow made of metal, and its branches bend low like a tree in mourning. Each leaf carries the name of a victim. When there is a breeze, look closely... the small leaves catch the light one by one.

The building has also been through hard years of neglect. War damage and decades of limited care left it in poor condition. A major restoration came in the 1990s, after the fall of communism. Donations arrived from Hungarian Jewish families and supporters around the world. Among the best-known names linked to that effort was the actor Tony Curtis, whose father came from Hungary. The bright colours you see today are the result of that long work of repair.

Before you leave, stand still in the street once more. Look at the towers against the sky, and then at the ordinary apartment blocks around them. This neighbourhood, the Seventh District, is still home to kosher shops, small prayer houses, and cafes in old courtyards. As you walk away, notice how the striped brick of the synagogue keeps appearing between the buildings. It is not hidden. It was built to be seen — and it is still here.
