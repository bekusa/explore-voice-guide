The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the terrace outside the palace. Below you, the Danube runs wide and grey-green, and the wind comes up off the water carrying the low hum of trams on the far bank. Across the river, the long white front of the Parliament building catches the light. Behind you rise the heavy stone walls of Buda Castle's Royal Palace. Somewhere inside them, behind tall doors and cool marble stairs, hangs the story of Hungarian art.

The Hungarian National Gallery lives inside this palace. It does not sit in a purpose-built museum, but in the rooms of a royal residence. That gives a visit an unusual feel. You walk through spaces that were designed for ceremony and power, and now they hold altarpieces, portraits and landscapes. The museum was founded in 1957 and moved into the castle in the 1970s. Today it fills several wings of the complex.

It helps to know what happened to these walls. The palace you see today grew over centuries, with a major expansion in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Then came the Second World War. During the siege of Budapest in the winter of 1944 and 1945, the castle district was badly damaged, and the palace burned. What stands here now is largely a postwar reconstruction. So the building itself carries the scars and repairs of the twentieth century.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The reconstruction work also uncovered much older layers. Beneath the later palace, archaeologists found remains of the medieval royal castle. Some of that medieval world is part of the gallery's story too. The collection begins not with paint, but with stone. Carved fragments, capitals and figures from medieval Hungary give you a sense of the kingdom long before the Habsburg era.

From stone, the story moves to wood and gold. One of the most memorable sections holds late Gothic winged altarpieces. These are folding panels, painted and carved, that once stood above church altars. Many came from churches in the northern regions of the old Kingdom of Hungary, an area that today lies largely in Slovakia. Stand close and look at the gilding. Look at the faces of saints, carved with real tenderness, and at the small painted scenes on the side wings.

These altarpieces were made to be opened and closed. On ordinary days, the outer panels showed one set of images. On feast days, the wings swung open to show the brightest, most richly gilded scenes inside. When you see one here, try to imagine candlelight, incense and a crowded village church. These objects were not made for a gallery wall. They were made for worship, and that purpose still shows in their scale and colour.

The largest part of the collection covers Hungarian painting from the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. This was a period when national identity mattered deeply. After the failed revolution of 1848, artists turned to history, landscape and peasant life to say something about what it meant to be Hungarian. You will see large historical canvases full of drama, and quieter scenes of village life. Together they form a kind of visual autobiography of the nation.

One name you will meet often is Mihály Munkácsy. He became one of the best-known Hungarian painters of his century, and he built much of his career in Paris. His works tend to be dark and deeply felt, with strong contrasts of light and shadow. Another name is Pál Szinyei Merse. His painting known as Picnic in May is full of bright greens, blue sky and figures resting on the grass. Many Hungarians know it well. It feels almost like a breath of fresh air after the darker rooms.

And here is a second surprise. Szinyei Merse painted in fresh, open colour at a time when many critics at home did not welcome it. Today his bright style looks gentle and natural to us. But in its own time, this kind of painting was a bold choice. As you walk, notice how the palette changes from room to room. You can almost watch Hungarian art open its windows to sunlight.

Look out, too, for Tivadar Csontváry Kosztka. He was a pharmacist who turned to painting with a strong sense of personal mission. His canvases are often huge, with intense colour and an almost dreamlike light. Some visitors find his work strange. Others find it unforgettable. Nearby, you will also find painters connected to the artists' colony at Nagybánya, a town where a group of artists gathered to paint outdoors. Their light-filled landscapes helped shape modern Hungarian painting.

The gallery continues into the twentieth century, with works that reflect war, change and new artistic movements. Rooms here can feel quite different in mood. Styles break apart, forms become simpler, and colour is used in new ways. Even if modern art is not your favourite, it is worth walking through. It shows how Hungarian artists stayed in conversation with the rest of Europe, even through very difficult decades.

Before you leave, think about the building one more time. The palace is crowned by a large dome, and visitors can sometimes reach a viewing terrace near the top with a separate ticket. From up there, the whole city opens out. You can follow the river, the bridges and the rooftops of Pest stretching away toward the horizon. It is a fine way to connect the paintings inside with the real landscape outside.

When you step back out into the open air, pause by the balustrade again. Feel the stone under your hand, warmed or cooled by the weather. Listen to the river traffic and the voices of other visitors on the terrace. The faces, fields and saints you have just seen came from this same country, painted by people who looked at this same river. As you walk down through the castle district, notice the light on the old walls. It may look a little different now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start with medieval stone, then follow the collection chronologically.
Allow at least two hours for the main collection.
Ask at the desk about separate access to the dome terrace.
Visit the river terrace outside before or after your visit.
Wear comfortable shoes for long corridors and grand staircases.
