The guide

The story

Listen first. Under your feet, the deck hums each time a yellow tram rolls past. The Danube moves below, wide and grey-green, carrying a cool wind that seems to come from nowhere. Look along the railing and you will notice something odd. This bridge does not run straight. Somewhere in the middle, it bends.

You are standing on Margaret Bridge, the second permanent crossing ever built across the Danube in Budapest. The first was the Chain Bridge, a short walk downstream. Before these bridges existed, people crossed by ferry, by temporary pontoon bridges, or in harsh winters, on foot over the ice. Buda and Pest were separate towns until 1873. A fixed bridge was a practical need, and it also showed that the two banks were becoming one city.

The bridge was built in the 1870s and opened in 1876. Its design came from a French firm, led by the engineer Ernest Goüin. That French link explains a lot of what you see. The decoration on the piers, the shape of the arches, and the elegant iron lamps all follow the taste of Paris in that era. Budapest was growing fast, and its leaders wanted the city to look modern and European.

Now, that bend. Halfway across, the bridge reaches the southern tip of Margaret Island, where the river splits into two branches. The engineers wanted the piers to stand at right angles to the current on each side. That reduces the force of water and ice against the stone. So the two halves meet at an angle, pointing slightly toward the island. Most visitors walk right across without noticing. Once you see it, though, you cannot unsee it.

At the bend, a short side branch leads down onto Margaret Island itself. This spur was not part of the first bridge. It was added around 1900. Before that, the island was harder to reach and felt more like a private retreat. Today it is a long, green park in the middle of the river, with gardens, running paths, ruins, and a sense of quiet that feels far from the city traffic.

The island and the bridge both carry the name of Saint Margaret of Hungary. She was a daughter of King Béla the Fourth, and she lived in the thirteenth century. According to tradition, her father promised her to religious life during the Mongol invasions of Hungary. She spent her life in a Dominican convent on the island. The ruins of that convent still stand among the trees, a short walk north from the end of this side branch.

Take a moment to look at the piers. On their sides stand sculpted groups of figures, carved by a French sculptor as part of the original design. These figures have weathered, been damaged, and been carefully restored. Above them, ornate lamp posts line the railings. In the evening, when the lamps glow and the river darkens, the bridge looks much as it did in old photographs, despite everything that happened to it.

And a great deal did happen. In November 1944, during the Second World War, German forces had placed explosives on the bridge to prepare it for demolition. Something went wrong. The charges went off by accident in the middle of a busy day, while a tram and many pedestrians were on the span. Part of the bridge fell into the Danube. Many people died, including German soldiers who were working on the bridge. It was one of the worst civilian disasters in the city during the war.

A few weeks later, in January 1945, retreating German troops blew up every remaining bridge across the Danube in Budapest. For a time, the city was again cut in two, just as it had been before these crossings existed. Rebuilding started soon after the war. Margaret Bridge reopened in 1948. Engineers and divers later recovered pieces of the old structure from the riverbed, and some original elements returned to their places.

The bridge you walk on today also reflects a major renovation in the early 2010s. Workers repaired the steel structure, renewed the tram lines, and restored the decorative details, including the lamps and the sculptures. They tried to bring back the nineteenth century character of the bridge while making it strong enough for modern traffic. Watch the trams that cross here. They run along the Grand Boulevard, the great curved ring road of Pest, which continues on the Buda side.

Now turn and look south, downstream. This is one of the best views in Budapest. On the Pest bank, the long Gothic-style Parliament building rises right at the water's edge, with its central dome and many thin spires. Across the river, Buda Castle Hill stretches along the ridge. Further downstream, you can see the Chain Bridge and the green hill of Gellért beyond it. On a clear morning, the whole river corridor opens in front of you.

Each end of the bridge leads to a different world. On the Pest side, the bridge lands at Jászai Mari Square, close to the Parliament and the riverside promenade. On the Buda side, the streets climb toward Rose Hill. Near the Buda end, up a set of steep lanes, stands the tomb of Gül Baba, a sixteenth century Ottoman dervish. It is a small, peaceful site, and a reminder that the Ottoman Empire once ruled this city for more than a century.

Before you go, stand still for a moment near the centre, where the angle is. Feel the slight vibration as a tram passes. Look down at the water, which splits around the island and joins again below you. Then look back at the iron lamps, the stone piers, and the long, gentle curve of the deck. This bridge has fallen and risen again. As you walk off toward either bank, notice how quietly it carries the city across the river, one tram, one cyclist, one walker at a time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk across slowly instead of riding the tram.
Come early morning for clear downstream views.
Use the mid-bridge spur to enter Margaret Island.
Cross at dusk to see the lamps lit.
Combine the walk with the Parliament riverbank.
