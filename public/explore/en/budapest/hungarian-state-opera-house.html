The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on Andrássy Avenue and listen. Traffic hums behind you, and trees line the long, straight boulevard. In front of you, a wall of pale stone rises in arches, columns and balconies. Look at the stone itself. It is warm-toned and finely carved, and in the afternoon light it turns almost honey-coloured. Two seated bronze figures watch the doorway. This is the Hungarian State Opera House, and it has watched this street since the late nineteenth century.

To understand why this building exists, picture Budapest in the 1870s. The city was not even one city yet. Buda, Óbuda and Pest joined together in 1873. Hungary had recently gained a new level of self-rule within the Habsburg Empire. The capital wanted public buildings that matched its new confidence. A grand avenue was cut through Pest towards the City Park. An opera house on that avenue would announce that Budapest belonged among Europe's great cultural capitals.

The architect was Miklós Ybl, one of the most important Hungarian architects of his century. Construction began in 1875, and the house opened in September 1884. Ybl worked in the neo-Renaissance style. That means he borrowed the calm proportions, arches and classical columns of Italian palaces from the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Notice how the façade feels balanced rather than overwhelming. It does not tower over the street. It sits on the avenue with a kind of measured dignity.

That modest scale has a famous story attached to it. Emperor Franz Joseph, who was also King of Hungary, helped fund the project. According to a widely told account, one condition came with the money. The Budapest opera could not be bigger than the opera house in Vienna. So Ybl, the story goes, made it smaller — but richer in detail. Whether every part of the tale is true, the building does feel intimate for an opera house of its importance.

Now look again at the two bronze figures beside the entrance. They show two giants of Hungarian music. One is Ferenc Erkel, who composed the music of the Hungarian national anthem. Erkel was also the first music director of this opera house. The other is Franz Liszt, the virtuoso pianist and composer. Liszt was born in the Kingdom of Hungary and became one of the most celebrated musicians in nineteenth-century Europe. Two very different careers, side by side at the same door.

Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. Lift your eyes to the upper balustrade, the decorative railing along the roofline. A row of statues stands there, looking out over the avenue. They represent famous composers from across Europe. Spend a minute picking them out. And at ground level, look for the sphinxes near the entrance ramps. They are calm, strange creatures for an opera house. They add a touch of mystery to a building that is otherwise very orderly.

Inside, the mood changes. Marble staircases sweep upwards. Gilding catches the light on every surface. The main auditorium is shaped like a horseshoe, a traditional layout for European opera houses. This shape brings the audience close to the stage and helps the sound travel. Rows of boxes curve around the hall in tiers of red and gold. Above them hangs a large chandelier, and the effect at night is warm rather than cold or showy.

Look up at the ceiling of the auditorium. The great painting there is by Károly Lotz, one of the leading decorative painters in Hungary at the time. It shows the gods of Mount Olympus, the home of the ancient Greek gods. Figures float among soft clouds and blue sky. Lotz also worked on other major buildings in Budapest. Here, his ceiling makes the whole hall feel lifted, as if the roof opens onto an evening sky.

One of the boxes has a special status. It is the royal box, placed in the centre and facing the stage. It was intended for the monarch, and its decoration is the richest in the hall. Locals sometimes repeat another popular tale about Franz Joseph. They say he admired the finished building, but could not quite hide his irritation that it was so beautiful. Treat it as a legend. It is a very Budapest kind of joke.

Here is a second surprise. In the late 1880s, a young Austrian conductor became director of this opera house. His name was Gustav Mahler. Today he is known as one of the great symphonic composers. At the time, he was still in his twenties and building his career. He worked here for a few seasons before moving on. Mahler later directed the Vienna opera. So the smaller house on Andrássy Avenue helped train the man who led the larger one.

The building has also lived through difficult times. The twentieth century brought war, occupation and decades of communist rule. Through all of it, opera and ballet continued on this stage. More recently, the house closed for a long and careful renovation. Work began in the late 2010s and the building reopened in 2022. Craftspeople restored gilding, fabrics and decorative surfaces. The stage machinery and comfort for the audience were also brought up to modern standards.

Step back now and take in the avenue around you. Andrássy Avenue is part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Beneath your feet runs the old yellow metro line, the M1. It opened in 1896 and is one of the oldest underground railways in the world. There is a small station called Opera just nearby. Its tiled walls and wooden details are worth a look. The avenue, the metro and the opera house were all part of the same ambitious vision.

Before you leave, stand on the pavement across the street. Watch people pause on the steps, glance up and take photographs. Notice the shadows in the arches and the bronze figures at the door. Listen for the traffic, the footsteps, and perhaps a tourist bus slowing down. This building was made so that a city could see itself as part of Europe's culture. As you walk away down the avenue, look back once. The façade stays steady, quiet and golden in the light.
