The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your shoes. It is a dry, steady crunch, and it is the loudest thing here. Around you, low walls of pale stone rise only to knee or waist height. They mark out rooms, streets and courtyards that people once lived in. Above you is the open sky of northern Budapest, and somewhere behind the trees a suburban train rattles past. This is Aquincum, and you are standing in a Roman town.

Most visitors to Budapest picture Habsburg palaces, the Parliament on the river, and grand nineteenth century boulevards. But long before Buda and Pest existed as names, this stretch of the Danube was a frontier of the Roman Empire. The river marked the edge of Roman power in this region. On the far bank lay lands Rome never fully controlled. That border shaped everything about the settlement you are walking through now.

The name Aquincum is probably older than the Romans. Scholars usually connect it to a Celtic word linked to water. That fits the place well, because this area has many springs, and Budapest is still known for its thermal baths today. Before the legions arrived, a Celtic people called the Eravisci lived in this area. The Romans did not erase them. Over time, local families mixed with soldiers, traders and officials from across the empire.

It helps to know that there were really two Aquincums. First came a large military fortress, home to a Roman legion, which stood a little to the south in what is now Óbuda. Then a civilian town grew up nearby, full of craftsmen, merchants, veterans and their families. The ruins in front of you belong mainly to that civilian town. Here is a detail many visitors miss — the most important military site lies under modern streets and apartment blocks nearby.

In the early second century, Rome divided the province of Pannonia into two parts. Aquincum became the capital of the eastern half, called Pannonia Inferior, or Lower Pannonia. That meant a governor lived here, with offices, staff and ceremony. One early governor was Hadrian, who later became emperor. The town gained higher legal status under him, and later again under Septimius Severus. Each step brought more rights and more pride to its citizens.

Now look along the main street. You can still see large paving stones, worn smooth and uneven by centuries of use. Drains run beneath parts of the street, and you may spot channels cut into the stone. The Romans cared a great deal about water — bringing it in, and carrying it away. Along the road outside the site, a line of stone pillars remains from an aqueduct. It once carried spring water into the town.

Look closely at some of the floors inside the ruined buildings. In places you will see small stacks of tiles or stone, like short columns, standing in rows. This is a hypocaust, a Roman system of underfloor heating. Hot air from a furnace flowed through the space beneath the floor and warmed the room above. On a cold Pannonian winter day, this made a real difference. It is one of the most human details on the site.

The town had public baths, and their remains are some of the largest structures you will see. Baths in a Roman town were not only for washing. People met friends there, talked business, exercised and relaxed. Rooms were kept at different temperatures, from cold to very hot. Walking among these walls, try to imagine steam, echoing voices, wet stone and the smell of oil that bathers rubbed on their skin.

There was also a market hall, a place where food and goods were sold. Nearby you can find the remains of shrines and sanctuaries. The people of Aquincum worshipped many gods, from Roman deities to gods brought by soldiers from the east. One of these was Mithras, a god popular with soldiers, whose followers met in small, dark chambers. Religion here was a mix, much like the population itself.

Now for the surprise most people do not expect. In 1931, archaeologists found the remains of a Roman water organ in Aquincum. It is one of the most important musical instruments to survive from the ancient world. A bronze plate on it dates the gift to the year 228. The organ belonged to a local association whose members also served as fire fighters. You can see the organ and a reconstruction in the museum.

Music was clearly valued here. A gravestone from the area remembers a woman named Aelia Sabina, and it praises her skill as a musician. Small objects like this make Aquincum feel close. In the museum you will also find mosaics, carved tombstones, pottery, jewellery and everyday tools. Look at the faces carved on the funeral stones. They show how people wanted to be remembered — in fine clothes, with family, often with their names and ages written clearly.

The town did not last forever. From the late fourth century, pressure on the frontier grew, and Roman control in Pannonia weakened. During the fifth century, life here faded, and the buildings slowly fell into ruin. Over the centuries, people took the stone for new buildings. Fields and gardens covered what remained. Serious excavation began in the late nineteenth century, and work on the site has continued for generations since then.

Before you leave, walk out past the site and look across the road. There you can see the remains of the civilian amphitheatre, where the people of the town gathered for shows and games. It is smaller than the military amphitheatre in Óbuda, but it still gives a clear sense of shape and scale. Stand in the middle of the oval and look at the low banks around you. Picture them full of people.

As you walk back toward the street, notice how close modern Budapest stands to these old walls. Trains, apartment blocks and traffic surround a town that has been quiet for over fifteen hundred years. The stones at your feet were laid by people who also worried about winter, water and work. Take one last look along the paved street... and listen again to the gravel under your shoes.

Bring water and a hat on sunny summer days.
