The guide

The story

Start at the edge of City Park, where the gravel paths meet the open square. Look up. Two long wings of the building curve gently upward from the ground, like the edges of a shallow bowl. Their surfaces are not smooth. They are covered in a fine metal grid, full of tiny square openings. As the sun moves, the light shifts through them, and the facade changes its look. It feels almost like woven cloth, stretched over a frame.

This is the Museum of Ethnography, known in Hungarian as the Néprajzi Múzeum. Ethnography is the study of how people live: their tools, clothing, homes, rituals and beliefs. This museum keeps the everyday objects of ordinary people, from Hungarian villages and from cultures far across the world. A carved spoon, an embroidered shirt, a fishing net. These are not the treasures of kings. They are the things that people actually held in their hands.

Now look more closely at that metal grid. Here is the first detail many visitors walk past. The pattern is not random. It is built from pixelated folk motifs, taken from objects in the museum's own collection. Flowers, geometric shapes and ornamental borders appear in square pixels, like an old computer image. Up close, you see only small squares. Step back a little, and the designs come together. The building wears its collection on the outside.

The museum is old, even though this building is new. It began in 1872, as the ethnographic department of the Hungarian National Museum. Over time it grew into a separate institution. Its collectors travelled widely in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Some worked in Hungarian villages, recording traditions that were already starting to disappear. Others brought back objects from Asia, Africa, the Americas and the Pacific. Today the collection holds a very large number of items, in the hundreds of thousands.

For much of the late twentieth century, the museum did not have a home of its own. From the 1970s, it lived inside the former Palace of Justice on Kossuth Square, directly opposite the Hungarian Parliament. That grand nineteenth century building has marble stairs and a painted ceiling hall. It was impressive, but it was never designed to store and show a vast ethnographic collection. For decades, staff and visitors spoke of the need for a purpose-built space.

That space arrived here, in City Park, as part of a large cultural project to bring several museums into the park. The building was designed by the Hungarian studio Napur Architect, with Marcel Ferencz as lead architect. It opened to the public in 2022. Its shape answers a difficult question. How do you place a large museum in a public park, without taking the park away from the people who use it?

The answer is on the roof. And this is the second surprise. You can walk up onto it. The two curving wings are covered in grass and planting, and paths lead up the slopes. From the top, you look out over the trees of City Park and the rooftops of the city. Many people come here simply to sit on the grass, without ever going inside. The museum gives the land back to the park.

Because the roof is a garden, much of the museum sits below ground. As you enter, you move down into wide, bright halls. Glass walls bring daylight into the lower levels. Notice how quiet it becomes as you go down. The sound of traffic on the nearby avenue fades. The rooms feel calm and open, with space to move around objects instead of only looking at them behind glass.

The location has its own layered history. Just beside the museum lies the square now named for the heroes of 1956. During the communist period, a huge statue of Stalin stood here. In October 1956, during the Hungarian uprising against Soviet rule, crowds pulled the statue down, leaving only its boots on the plinth. Today the open square carries that memory. The museum stands at the edge of a place where the city once spoke loudly.

Inside, the exhibitions mix Hungarian folk life with objects from around the world. You may see richly embroidered textiles, painted wooden furniture, pottery and costumes from different regions of the country. The colours are often strong: deep reds, blues and blacks, with fine needlework. Look at the stitches if you can. Each one was made by hand, often by women working through long winter evenings. These pieces carry the patience of their makers.

The museum also asks how we look at other cultures. Many objects from distant places arrived during the era of European empires and scientific expeditions. Modern ethnographic museums think carefully about this. Who made these things? How did they come here? What did they mean to the people who used them? Some displays invite you to think about these questions, rather than giving a single simple answer. It is a museum that wants a conversation with you.

The programme changes often, with temporary exhibitions alongside the permanent displays. Some are playful and interactive. Others are quiet and reflective. It is worth checking what is on before you arrive, and allowing more time than you expect. Many visitors plan for one hour and stay for three. There is a shop and a café, and the roof garden is a good place to rest between rooms.

Before you leave, go back outside and stand at a distance from the facade. Watch how the light falls through the small square openings in the metal. Look for a motif you saw earlier inside, on a shirt or a painted chest. Then walk slowly up the green slope, and turn around at the top. Below you are the park, the square, and the city... and all around you, the patterns of people who lived long before.
