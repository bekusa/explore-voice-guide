The guide

The story

Listen first. Just beyond the palace wall, the traffic of central Seoul hums and rushes. Inside, the sound drops away. Your shoes crunch on pale gravel. A wide stone courtyard opens ahead, laid with uneven granite slabs that tilt slightly under your feet. Above the low walls, office towers catch the light. This is Gyeonghuigung, and it is one of the quietest royal spaces in a very loud city.

Gyeonghuigung was one of the Five Grand Palaces of the Joseon dynasty. That kingdom ruled Korea for about five hundred years, until the early twentieth century. Gyeongbokgung was the main royal seat. Changdeokgung and Changgyeonggung served as the eastern palaces. This complex was different. People called it the West Palace, because it stood on the western side of the old capital. For long stretches, kings lived, worked, and died here.

Construction began in the early seventeenth century under King Gwanghaegun, around 1617. At first the palace had another name, Gyeongdeokgung. It received its current name in 1760, during the reign of King Yeongjo. The name is usually translated as the Palace of Serene Harmony. Over the following generations, many Joseon rulers moved between this palace and the eastern palaces. It was a working seat of government, not a holiday retreat.

There is a story behind the choice of this site, and it comes with a twist. According to a popular account, people believed that royal energy gathered here. The land had belonged to a prince from a rival branch of the family. Legend has it that Gwanghaegun built his palace on the spot to claim that energy for himself. If so, the plan failed. Gwanghaegun was overthrown in 1623, and the son of that same rival prince became King Injo.

Now look at the scale of what surrounds you. At its height, the West Palace held around a hundred buildings. Halls, residences, gates, and corridors spread across the slope. Today only a small part stands. What you see is mostly a careful reconstruction from the late twentieth century. So the open space around you is not empty by design. It is the space where a much larger palace once stood.

The great loss came in the early twentieth century, during the Japanese colonial period. Japan annexed Korea in 1910, and the palace grounds were taken over. Most buildings were taken apart. Some were sold, and others were moved to new sites around the city. A school for Japanese students was built on the grounds. For decades, the West Palace almost vanished from the map of Seoul.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Some original buildings did not disappear. They were scattered. The old main gate, Heunghwamun, was moved away and used at the entrance of a Japanese shrine. Later it stood at a hotel. The original throne hall was moved to a Buddhist temple and later served as a university building. So pieces of this palace survive across Seoul, far from the ground where they first stood.

Walk toward the main hall, Sungjeongjeon. This was the throne hall, where the king held formal audiences and state ceremonies. The building rises on a raised stone platform. In front of it, you may see two rows of small stone markers. These show where officials stood, sorted by rank. Higher officials stood closer to the throne. Lower ranks stood farther back. The whole courtyard worked like a map of power.

Now look up at the eaves. The wooden brackets and beams are painted in a style called dancheong. It is a traditional Korean way of decorating wood with bright, layered patterns. Look for deep green, red, blue, and white. The paint was not only decoration. It also protected the wood from rain and insects. On a sunny day, the colours stand out sharply against the grey roof tiles.

Behind the main hall, the ground climbs. Here you will find smaller halls, including Jajeongjeon, where the king met with officials for daily business. Further up stands Taeryeongjeon. It was used to keep a royal portrait of King Yeongjo. Near it, look for a large natural rock. It is often linked to the legend of royal energy on this site. Locals sometimes call it the king's rock.

The slope itself is worth noticing. Gyeongbokgung sits on flat ground with long straight lines. This palace follows the hill. The halls step upward, one level after another. Stone stairs and terraces connect them. From the upper levels, you can look back down over the tiled roofs toward the courtyard. Beyond them, the glass towers of the city fill the horizon.

The neighbourhood around the palace tells its own story. The Seoul Museum of History stands next to the grounds. It covers the life of the city across many centuries, and it pairs well with a palace visit. A branch of the Seoul Museum of Art also sits close by. Because the palace gets fewer crowds than the larger palaces, many people come here simply to sit for a while.

The seasons change the mood of the place. In autumn, the trees on the slope turn gold and red against the dark tiles. In winter, snow settles on the curved roof edges and the courtyard turns white and still. In spring, the grounds feel soft and green. Each season shows a different side of the same few buildings, and the quiet stays the same.

Before you leave, stand once more in the main courtyard. Feel the uneven stones under your feet. Look at the gap between the rebuilt halls and the modern towers behind them. Much of this palace was lost, scattered, and only partly returned. And still, people come back here to walk slowly. As you step out through the gate, listen... the noise of the city is waiting for you again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Pair your visit with the Seoul Museum of History.
Wear shoes with grip for the uneven stone courtyard.
Climb to the upper terraces for views over roofs.
Come on a weekday morning for near-empty courtyards.
Visit in autumn for coloured trees against dark tiles.
