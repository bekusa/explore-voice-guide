The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere behind you, traffic hums along a wide city road. But right here, the noise softens. Low buildings sit apart from one another, and open courtyards let in the sky. The light falls on pale walls, warm red brick, and the dark curved roof tiles of older wooden halls. This is a museum that does not rise up to impress you. It spreads out and asks you to wander.

Welcome to the National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art, Korea. Most people simply call it MMCA. One honest note before we begin: this institution runs several branches. There are sites in Seoul, Gwacheon, and Cheongju, plus a gallery inside Deoksugung Palace. This guide focuses on the Seoul branch, near Gyeongbokgung Palace in the old heart of the city. If you are standing at a different site, the story of the museum still applies.

The museum began in 1969. Its first home was inside the grounds of Gyeongbokgung, the main royal palace of the Joseon dynasty, which ruled Korea for about five centuries. In 1973, the collection moved to a stone hall inside Deoksugung Palace. For a young national art museum, borrowing space in royal palaces was a practical choice. But it also set a pattern. From the start, modern Korean art lived side by side with the country's past.

In 1986, the museum opened a large new building in Gwacheon, south of Seoul. That site sits among green hills, beside a park and a zoo. It is also home to one of the most surprising works in Korean art. Nam June Paik, the Korean-born pioneer of video art, built a tower there from more than a thousand television screens. It is called The More, the Better. If you ever visit Gwacheon, look up through the spiral ramp. The screens climb above you like a flickering column.

The Seoul branch, where we are now, opened in 2013. It arrived late, but it arrived in a remarkable place. This piece of land has carried many layers of Korean history. During the Joseon dynasty, royal offices stood here. In the twentieth century, under Japanese colonial rule, a red-brick hospital building went up on the site. Later, after the Korean War, the same land became a military compound.

That military chapter is the surprise many visitors miss. For decades, this site belonged to the Defense Security Command, a powerful military intelligence unit. During the years of authoritarian rule, it was a place most citizens did not want to enter. Today, the same ground is open to anyone with an interest in art. Families walk through it. Students sketch in the courtyards. The change from a closed security site to a public museum is part of the meaning of this place.

Look for the old red-brick building. It is the oldest modern structure on the site, and the architects chose to keep it rather than tear it down. Its long facade, rows of windows, and solid brick walls feel very different from the clean new galleries. Inside, it now holds exhibition rooms and visitor spaces. The building carries the traces of every use it has had, from hospital to military office to museum.

Now notice how the new buildings behave. They stay low. The designers did not want a tower that would block views of the palace or the mountains behind it. Instead, they used the idea of the madang. A madang is the traditional Korean courtyard, the open yard at the centre of a home where daily life happened. Here, several courtyards link the galleries together. You step out into open air, then back inside, again and again.

This design changes how a visit feels. In many large museums, you move through long corridors and lose track of the outside world. Here, you keep seeing the sky. You may catch a view of a palace roof, or the rocky peak of Bugaksan, the mountain that rises behind Gyeongbokgung. The museum frames the city as part of the experience. Take a moment in each courtyard. Look up, and see what the buildings let you see.

At the heart of the Seoul branch is a tall, bright white hall often called the Seoul Box. It is one of the largest open spaces in the museum. Artists use it for big installations that would not fit in an ordinary gallery. Some years, the space feels almost empty, with a single huge work hanging in the air. Other years, it fills with sound, light, or moving objects. Whatever is there today, stand in the centre and notice the scale.

The Seoul branch also has a habit of large commissions. Each year, a major contemporary artist is invited to create a new work for one of the big galleries, as part of a long-running series supported by a Korean car company. These shows often become the talking point of the art season in Seoul. The museum's programme moves between Korean and international artists, between painting, sculpture, video, architecture, and design.

Another story unfolded in 2021. The family of the late Samsung chairman Lee Kun-hee donated a very large private art collection to the nation. A major part of it went to this museum. The gift included modern Korean paintings and important works by international artists. For Korean audiences, it was big news. Works that had lived in a private collection could now be seen by the public, and exhibitions from that donation have drawn long queues.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot in one of the courtyards. Listen again. You may hear footsteps on stone, a child laughing, wind moving across the open space. Look at the mix around you: red brick from the colonial era, white walls from this century, and dark tiled roofs from an older Korea. Then step back out toward the street. The palace walls, the small galleries, and the cafes of the old neighbourhood are waiting just beyond the gate.
