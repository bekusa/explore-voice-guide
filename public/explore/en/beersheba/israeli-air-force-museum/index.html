The guide

The story

Heat rises off the tarmac in a slow shimmer. Wide desert light falls on rows of metal wings, and the air smells of warm dust and sun-baked paint. Somewhere beyond the fence, the low roar of a jet engine rolls across the plain... then fades. You are standing in an open-air hangar with no roof, at the edge of the Negev Desert, a short drive west of Beersheba.

This is the Israeli Air Force Museum. It sits inside the grounds of Hatzerim, an active air base. That detail matters. The aircraft around you are retired, but the sky above is still working airspace. The Israeli Air Force flight academy is based here, so the sound you heard may well be a student pilot in training. History and the present share the same runway.

The museum takes its name from the base, and many people simply call it the Hatzerim museum. It began in the late 1970s and grew over the following decades. Today it is one of the largest collections of military aircraft in the region. Well over a hundred aircraft stand in long lines across the sand. Most of them are outside, under the open sky — and that is the first surprise for many visitors.

Why outside? Part of the answer is space, and part is climate. The Negev is a dry desert, and dry air is kind to metal. Rust works slowly here. So the planes can sit in the sun for years. Still, the light is harsh, and the paint fades. As you walk, notice how some colours look chalky and soft. The sun has been working on them for a long time.

To understand this collection, it helps to start in 1948. When the State of Israel was declared that year, its air force was tiny and improvised. Pilots flew whatever aircraft they could find, buy, or rebuild. Some planes came from scrap. Some came from abroad in pieces. Volunteers from many countries joined the early squadrons. The oldest aircraft here belong to that chaotic first chapter.

One of the most surprising stories is the Avia S-199. It was built in Czechoslovakia after the Second World War, using the design of the German Messerschmitt fighter. In 1948, Israel bought a small number of them. Pilots did not love it. It was hard to handle on take-off and landing, and they gave it a nickname that means "the mule". Yet these awkward machines flew some of the country's first combat missions.

Look also for the Spitfire. This British fighter is famous from the Battle of Britain. Israel acquired Spitfires in its early years, and one example here carries a striking black and red striped nose. It is linked with Ezer Weizman, an early air force commander who later became President of Israel. The plane is small when you stand beside it. It is easy to forget how little protected a pilot back then.

As you move along the rows, the aircraft grow larger, sleeker, and faster. You are walking through decades of design. Propellers give way to jet engines. Straight wings give way to swept wings and then to the sharp triangle of the delta wing. French Mirage fighters stand here, and so does the Kfir, an aircraft developed in Israel from the Mirage design. The family resemblance is easy to see once you know to look.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. On some fighters, look just below the cockpit. Small painted symbols appear in neat rows. These are kill markings. Each one records an aerial victory credited to that airframe. They are tiny, almost decorative from a distance. Up close, they turn a museum object into a record of real missions and real pilots.

The second surprise is a plane that never went into service. The Lavi was an ambitious Israeli fighter project in the 1980s. Prototypes were built and flown, but the programme was cancelled before production. An example of this aircraft is kept in the collection. It is a glimpse of a future that did not happen — a design that stayed in the hangar of history rather than the front line.

Not every aircraft here is a fighter. Helicopters stand with their long rotor blades drooping in the heat. Transport planes rise above the rows like grey whales. Some of these large aircraft carried soldiers, supplies, and also civilians during rescue and immigration operations. A big transport jet on site is used for exhibits and screenings, so look for an open door. Stepping inside gives you welcome shade and a sense of scale.

The museum is also a place where Israelis come to remember. School groups walk the rows with guides. Families point out the type of aircraft a relative once flew or maintained. Soldiers in uniform visit as part of their training. For many local visitors, this is not only a collection of machines. It is a story about the people who designed them, fixed them, and flew them, often at great cost.

Practical thoughts help here. The site is wide, flat, and exposed, and shade is limited. Morning is usually the most comfortable time to walk. Bring water, a hat, and sunscreen, even in the cooler months. Because this is a military base, follow the signs and the instructions of staff. Some areas may be off limits, and rules about photography near the active base can be strict.

Before you leave, stop for a moment in the open space between the rows. Feel the heat coming up from the ground. Look at the long shadows of the wings stretched across the sand. Listen for the sound of a training jet somewhere overhead. The aircraft around you are silent now. But the sky over Hatzerim is still full of motion, and the story you have walked through is still being written up there.
