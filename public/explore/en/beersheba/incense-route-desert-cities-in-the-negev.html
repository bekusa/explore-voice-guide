The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here the wind makes more noise than anything else. It moves over the limestone and pushes fine dust against your ankles. The light is hard and pale, and the ground underfoot is a mix of gravel and pale chalky rock. It is hard to imagine that this dry country once carried some of the most valuable cargo in the ancient world. Yet camel caravans crossed these hills for centuries, loaded with resins that were worth more than their weight in many other goods.

Those resins were frankincense and myrrh. They came from trees that grow in southern Arabia, in lands that today belong to Yemen and Oman. Temples across the Mediterranean burned them as offerings. Physicians used them in medicine. Wealthy families used them in funerals and perfumes. Demand was enormous, and the supply came from only a few places. So a long overland route developed. It ran north through the Arabian Peninsula and then west across the Negev, toward the ports on the Mediterranean coast near Gaza.

The people who controlled the final stretch of this route were the Nabataeans. They were an Arab people whose capital was Petra, the rock-cut city in what is now southern Jordan. The Nabataeans did not grow the incense. Their wealth came from moving it safely. They knew where water could be found in the desert, and they guarded that knowledge carefully. Caravans needed rest stops, wells, and protection. The Nabataeans built a chain of towns and small forts to provide exactly that.

In 2005, UNESCO inscribed this Negev section of the route as a World Heritage Site. The listing includes four towns — Haluza, Mamshit, Avdat, and Shivta. It also includes a line of fortresses and the remains of ancient farming landscapes. Beersheba, where you may be starting your journey, works as the natural gateway. The sites are spread across the desert to the south and east, so most visitors reach them by car, one or two in a day.

The trade reached its height roughly between the third century before Christ and the second century after Christ. Over the whole network, the route stretched for around two thousand kilometres. A single journey could take weeks. Think about what that meant in practice. Every day, a caravan needed a safe place to stop, and every few days it needed a reliable source of water. The towns you can visit today grew out of those simple, urgent needs.

Water is the real story here. Rain in the Negev is rare, but when it comes, it often arrives in short, heavy bursts. The Nabataeans learned to catch it. They cut cisterns into the rock and plastered them so they would hold water through the long dry months. They built channels along the slopes to guide runoff into those cisterns. Many visitors walk past these features without noticing them. Look for square or round openings in the ground, and for low lines of stones running across the hillsides.

Those low stone lines are part of something else that surprises many people. This desert was farmed. In later centuries, farmers built terraces and small dams across the dry valleys. When a flash flood came, the water spread over the terraces and soaked into the soil. Crops could grow where almost no rain fell from the sky. On the slopes around Avdat and Shivta, you can still trace these old field systems. Seen from above, they look like faint stitching across the landscape.

Avdat is the most dramatic of the four towns. It sits on a high ridge with wide views over the valleys around it. The name is linked to a Nabataean king called Obodas. Some ancient sources suggest he was honoured as a god after his death, though the details are debated. The ruins you see today come from several periods. There are Nabataean remains, later Roman structures, and Byzantine churches built when the population had become Christian. Walking up the ridge, you move through several centuries in just a few hundred metres.

That Byzantine layer is the second surprise. The incense trade faded over time, as sea routes and changing politics shifted commerce elsewhere. But the desert towns did not simply disappear. Under Byzantine rule, many of them grew into farming communities. People built churches, houses, and large wine presses. At Avdat and at Shivta, you can find the stone floors and collecting basins where grapes were pressed. Wine from the Negev region was traded abroad, and it was known in the Mediterranean world as a quality product.

Shivta has a quieter character. It lies far from main roads, and on many days you may share it with only a few other people. The town has no defensive wall. Its houses, streets, and courtyards stand close together, built from the local stone. Three churches stand within the town. In the stillness, it is easy to picture daily life here — doors opening onto narrow lanes, water being drawn from cisterns, the sound of animals in the yards.

Mamshit lies to the east, near the town of Dimona. It is the smallest of the four, but it is compact and well preserved. Its buildings show careful stonework, with arches that once supported flat roofs. Haluza, to the west of Beersheba, was once the largest town in the region. Much of it now lies under sand, and only parts have been excavated. Between and around these towns, the remains of small forts stand along the old line of travel, watching over the passes.

As you explore, pay attention to the stone itself. Most of it is local limestone, pale in the morning and golden in the late afternoon. Notice how the builders used arches to span rooms, because long wooden beams were scarce in the desert. Notice how thick the walls are. They kept the interiors cool in summer and held some warmth on cold winter nights. These were practical solutions, worked out over generations, by people who understood this land very well.

Now stand still once more, wherever you are on this route. Look out at the dry hills and the empty valleys. Somewhere below you, a stone channel still waits for rain. Somewhere nearby, a cistern still holds the shape of the water it once kept. The caravans are long gone, and the incense smoke disappeared centuries ago. But the paths, the terraces, and the walls remain. As you walk back to the road, listen again to the wind — it is the same wind the travellers heard.
