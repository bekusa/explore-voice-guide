The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere ahead of you, small brass cymbals are clashing in a steady rhythm. A drum answers them. Voices rise and fall around two words, repeated again and again: Vitthal, Vitthal. The stone under your bare feet is cool and worn smooth. The air carries marigold, incense, and the green smell of tulsi, the holy basil that pilgrims wear in garlands. You have arrived at the Vithoba Temple in Pandharpur, one of the most loved shrines in all of Maharashtra.

Pandharpur sits on the bank of the Bhima River. Here, locals call it the Chandrabhaga, because the river bends in a curve like a crescent moon. The town grew around the temple, and the temple grew around a single god. His names are many: Vithoba, Vitthal, Panduranga. Devotees often call him Vithu Mauli, which means something close to Mother Vithu. A male god called mother — that tells you a lot about the tenderness people feel here.

Who is he? Vithoba is understood as a form of Vishnu, and closely linked with Krishna. But he does not look like the dramatic Krishna of paintings, playing a flute or driving a chariot. Vithoba simply stands. He is dark, upright, and calm, with both hands resting on his hips. He looks as if he is waiting for someone. And according to the story told in Pandharpur, he is.

Legend has it that a young man named Pundalik lived here, devoted to his elderly parents. One day the god himself came to visit him. Pundalik was busy caring for his mother and father, so he did not stop. Instead, he tossed a brick outside and asked his guest to stand on it and wait. The god was so moved by this devotion that he stood on the brick... and, the faithful say, he is standing there still.

That brick is the key to the whole image. Look for it beneath the deity's feet when you reach the inner shrine. It is a quiet lesson, told in stone. Here, serving the people you love is not a distraction from worship. It is worship. Down by the river you can also find a small shrine dedicated to Pundalik himself, sitting close to the water, and many pilgrims visit it as part of their circuit.

The temple is old. Inscriptions connected to the site suggest it was an important religious centre by at least the thirteenth century, and it has been rebuilt and extended many times since then. What you see today is a complex of halls, gateways, and smaller shrines, rather than one single building. The main eastern gate leads into a series of courtyards, with pillared halls darkened by centuries of lamp smoke and countless hands.

Now, here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. At the main entrance, there is a step known as the Namdev Payari, the step of Namdev. Namdev was a poet-saint of the Varkari tradition. According to a popular tale, he asked to become a step at the god's door, so that the dust from every devotee's feet would fall on him. Pilgrims bow and touch this step before they enter. Watch how many people pause there.

And close to that same entrance is a memorial to another saint, Chokhamela. He was born into a community that caste society treated as untouchable, and for that reason he was not allowed inside the temple during his life. He sang his devotion from outside the walls. His memorial stands at the threshold he could not cross. That placement carries a heavy story, and many pilgrims remember him before they go in.

That story did change. In 1947, the social reformer Sane Guruji held a fast to demand that the temple open its doors to people of all castes. Soon after, the temple was opened to Dalit devotees. Today the shrine is known for something unusual among major Hindu temples. Devotees in the main queue may come close enough to touch the feet of the god and rest their forehead against them. It is a deeply personal moment, and it happens within seconds.

So who are the people around you? Many belong to the Varkari tradition, a devotional movement centred on Vithoba. You can often recognise them. They may wear white, carry orange flags, and have a string of tulsi beads around the neck. They sing abhangas, short devotional poems written by saints like Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Janabai, and Tukaram. These poems are in Marathi, the language of ordinary people, not classical Sanskrit. That choice opened worship to everyone.

The great moment of the Varkari year is the wari, a pilgrimage on foot. Each year, before the festival of Ashadhi Ekadashi in the monsoon season, huge processions walk to Pandharpur over many days. They carry palanquins holding the sandals of saints, including Dnyaneshwar from Alandi and Tukaram from Dehu. Hundreds of thousands of people walk together, singing the whole way. When they arrive, this town becomes a sea of white clothes and saffron flags.

As you move toward the inner shrine, you will also find a separate shrine to Rukmini, whom devotees lovingly call Rakhumai. She is honoured as the consort of Vithoba. Many pilgrims visit both, one after the other. Notice the small offerings sold in the lanes outside: tulsi garlands, sugar sweets, and a black, fragrant powder called bukka. Pilgrims often mark their foreheads with it, and you may see dark smudges on faces all around you.

Take a moment in one of the open courtyards. Look up at the carved gateways and the towers rising above the walls. Look down at the stone floor, polished by bare feet over hundreds of years. Even on an ordinary day, this place is rarely silent. Someone is always singing, someone is ringing a bell, someone is calling out the god's name. Faith here sounds less like a whisper and more like a family gathering.

When you leave, walk slowly toward the river. Follow the lane down to the ghats, the stone steps that lead to the water. Watch the people bathing, the small boats, the flowers floating past. Listen once more for the cymbals behind you. Somewhere back there, a dark god is still standing on his brick, hands on his hips — patient, and waiting for the next person to arrive.

Before you go

Practical tips

Expect very long queues to touch the deity's feet.
Leave shoes at a stand before entering the temple area.
Avoid the Ashadhi Ekadashi crowds unless you want the festival.
Dress modestly with shoulders and knees covered.
Visit the river ghats after the temple for a quieter moment.
