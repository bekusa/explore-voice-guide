The guide

The story

The floorboards creak softly under your feet. Morning light comes through tall windows and falls across rows of wooden shelves. The room smells of old paper, polish, and a little dust. It feels like any small-town library, quiet and patient. Then you look down. A thin black line runs across the floor, straight as a ruler. On one side of that line, you are standing in Canada. On the other side, you are in the United States.

This is the Haskell Free Library and Opera House. It sits on the border between Stanstead, in the Canadian province of Quebec, and Derby Line, in the American state of Vermont. The two towns grew up side by side, and for generations their streets ran into each other with little fuss. This building was designed to belong to both. It was not built to mark a divide. It was built to share one roof between two communities.

The story begins with a family. Carlos Haskell was a local businessman who worked in the lumber trade. After his death, his widow, Martha Stewart Haskell, decided to build something in his memory. She was joined in the project by their son. Construction began around the start of the twentieth century, and the building opened a few years later, in the early 1900s. It was a gift to the people on both sides of the line.

Martha Haskell chose the location with care. She placed the building directly across the border, on purpose. Her idea was simple. Children and families from both towns should be able to read, learn, and enjoy music together, under one roof. In an age when the border was a quiet formality, this was a generous and practical vision. Today, with modern border controls, that vision feels much more unusual.

Take a moment to look at the building from outside, if you have not already. It is a solid, handsome structure, typical of grand public buildings from that period. Notice the stonework near the base and the careful brick above it. This corner of Quebec and Vermont is famous for its granite quarries, and stone has long been part of local building. The tower and the turrets give the library a slightly romantic, storybook look.

Now, the line itself. The international boundary was surveyed long before this building existed, so the architects had to work around it. Inside, the line is painted or taped across the floor so you can see exactly where it falls. Many visitors stand with one foot in each country and take a photograph. Here is the first surprise. The main entrance is on the American side. Most of the books, however, sit on shelves in Canada.

That odd split has given rise to a well-known local joke. People say this is the only library in the United States with almost no books, and the only opera house with no stage. Why? Because the stage, like the bookshelves, stands in Canada. The audience mostly sits in the United States. So when a performer sings upstairs, the music crosses an international border before it reaches the listeners.

The opera house is on the upper floor. It is a small, intimate theatre with a horseshoe of seating and a balcony. The style is late Victorian, with warm wood, decorative plasterwork, and the feeling of a grand hall shrunk to village size. Over the decades it has hosted concerts, plays, community events, and local celebrations. It was never meant to rival a big city stage. It was meant to bring culture to a rural place.

Downstairs, the library still works as a real library. Local residents borrow books here every week. Staff help readers from both countries, and the collection includes material in both English and French. This mix of languages reflects the region itself. Stanstead is in a mostly French-speaking province, while Derby Line is in an English-speaking state. In this building, the two languages sit on the same shelves.

Here is a second surprise. For many years, people simply walked in and out with little attention to the border. Over time, and especially in recent years, the rules became stricter. Visitors coming from Canada are now expected to follow specific procedures, and the border agencies on both sides watch the area closely. If you leave the building, you must return to the country you came from, unless you pass through an official crossing.

The building has also taken on a quieter, human role. Because people from both countries can stand in the same room, it has sometimes become a meeting place. News reports have described families who could not easily travel across the border meeting here, briefly, inside the library. They could stand together for a short time, under one roof, on a floor marked by a line. It is a moving reminder of what this place can mean.

Both countries recognise the building's importance. It is a National Historic Site in Canada and is listed on the National Register of Historic Places in the United States. That double recognition fits a building with a double identity. Local people care for it as part of everyday life, not only as a monument. You might see a child choosing a picture book while a tourist photographs the line a few steps away.

As you finish your visit, pause near that black line once more. Listen to the gentle sounds of the room: pages turning, a chair moving, quiet voices in two languages. When you step outside, look along the street at the houses and shops of both towns. They still lean toward each other, as they always have. The border is real. But inside this library, it is just a line on a wooden floor.
