The guide

The story

The first thing you notice up here is the wind. It comes over the ridge in hard, cold gusts, and it carries almost no smell at all. Only thin, dry air and the crunch of gravel under your boots. The light is very sharp at this altitude. Shadows have clean edges, and the sky looks darker blue than it does in the valley below. And there, on its stone pedestal, a bronze figure stands against the wind with one hand raised.

This is Christ the Redeemer of the Andes. The statue stands on the high border pass between Chile and Argentina, roughly 3,800 metres above sea level. You have climbed here from the town of Los Andes, up through the long Aconcagua valley. Behind you is Chile. In front of you, the road falls away into Argentina. The line between two countries runs right across this windy saddle of rock, and the statue sits almost on top of it.

To understand why a statue stands in such a hard place, we need to go back to the end of the nineteenth century. Chile and Argentina share one of the longest borders in the world, and much of it runs along the spine of the Andes. For decades, the two countries disagreed about exactly where that line should fall. By the 1890s, the dispute had become serious. Both nations were buying warships and building up their armies. Many people on both sides feared a war.

War did not come. Instead, the two governments turned to arbitration. They asked the British crown to help settle the boundary, and in 1902 the two countries signed a set of agreements known as the May Pacts. These treaties eased the arms race and opened a path to a peaceful border. For many people in both countries, it felt like a narrow escape. They wanted a lasting symbol of that moment, and they chose to place it here, on the pass itself.

The idea gained strength through religious and civic leaders. A bishop from the Cuyo region of Argentina, on the eastern side of the mountains, supported the project. So did an Argentine woman named Ángela de Oliveira Cézar de Costa, who worked to raise support and funds. The sculptor was the Argentine artist Mateo Alonso. He designed a standing Christ, holding a cross in one hand and raising the other in blessing over both nations.

Here is a story you will often hear. The bronze for the statue is said to come from old cannons, melted down and turned from weapons into a figure of peace. It is a powerful image, and it is told in many guidebooks. But treat it with some care. The detail is widely repeated, and historians do not all agree on how much of the metal truly came from guns. Either way, the meaning was clear to the people who built it.

Now think about the work of getting it here. The statue was cast in Buenos Aires, far away on the Atlantic coast. It travelled west by train toward the mountains. After the rail line ended, the pieces had to go up the last steep stretch by mule and by hand. Picture the workers in this thin air, hauling heavy bronze up rough tracks. The statue was finally unveiled in March of 1904, with crowds from both countries gathered at the summit.

Take a moment to look closely at the figure. The bronze has turned dark with age, and in places you can see streaks and patches from more than a century of weather. Snow, ice, strong sun, and wind all hit this metal year after year. The pedestal below is made of stone, broad and heavy, built to hold the statue steady against the gusts. Walk around it slowly. The view changes on every side.

There is one inscription here that many visitors miss. A plaque carries a solemn vow. In plain words, it says the mountains themselves would crumble into dust before Chileans and Argentines broke the peace they swore at the feet of this Christ. Read it while you stand with the real mountains all around you. The promise feels much heavier when you can see the peaks it names.

And here is a second surprise, this time a piece of folklore. According to a popular tale, people in Chile once complained that the statue had its back turned to their country. Someone, the story goes, answered with a joke: Christ was facing Argentina because the Argentines needed more watching over. Nobody can say who first told it. But locals on both sides still enjoy the story, and it shows the friendly rivalry between the two nations.

Today, most traffic does not pass this point at all. Far below your feet, a road tunnel runs through the mountain. Trucks and buses travelling between Santiago and Mendoza go through it and never see the statue. To reach this summit, you need the old gravel road that winds up over the pass. That road is usually only open in the warmer months, and snow can close it quickly. So the people you meet up here have chosen to come.

Look out at the landscape before you leave. The mountains here are bare and steep, in shades of rust, grey, ochre and purple. There are almost no trees, only rock and scree and, for much of the year, snow. Somewhere to the north rises Aconcagua, the highest mountain in the Americas. On a clear day, the ranges seem to go on without end. At this height, every small movement takes a little more breath than usual.

On your way back down to Los Andes, the road passes some places worth a pause. On the Chilean side, the highway drops through a long chain of tight hairpin bends, a famous stretch of switchbacks. Near the top lies a high lake with deep blue-green water, and a ski area beside it. Lower down, the valley slowly turns green again, with poplars, vineyards, and small farms. The change from bare rock to orchards happens over just a few hours of driving.

Before you go, stand still for a moment beside the pedestal. Feel the cold wind on your face and listen to it moving over the stones. Look at the raised bronze hand, and then at the open land on both sides of the line. More than a century ago, two countries chose to put a sign of peace in this empty, difficult place. As you walk back to the car, glance over your shoulder. The figure will still be there, dark against the sky.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check road status first; snow often closes the summit road.
Bring a warm, windproof layer even in summer.
Walk slowly; the thin air tires visitors quickly.
Bring your passport since you approach the border crossing.
Carry water and sunscreen for the strong high-altitude sun.
