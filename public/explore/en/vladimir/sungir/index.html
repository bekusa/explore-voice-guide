The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through the grass. Somewhere below, water runs over stones where a small stream meets the Klyazma River. Under your feet is soft, heavy clay, the kind that once fed a brick factory. Nothing here looks dramatic. There is no tower, no gate, no golden dome. And yet this quiet bank on the edge of Vladimir holds one of the most remarkable discoveries in the study of early humans.

This is Sungir, an Upper Palaeolithic site. That phrase simply means the late Old Stone Age, the time when modern humans spread across Europe and the great ice sheets still reached far south. The people who camped on this slope lived roughly thirty thousand years ago or more. Vladimir's white stone cathedrals are less than a thousand years old. Sungir is about thirty times older than the city's oldest church.

The discovery began by accident. In the mid-1950s, workers were digging clay here for bricks. An excavator operator noticed bones in the soil, large bones that clearly did not belong to cattle or horses. They belonged to mammoths. Archaeologists were called, and soon the archaeologist Otto Bader began systematic excavations. His work, and the work of colleagues who followed him, continued here over many seasons and several decades.

At first the finds were the ordinary treasures of a hunters' camp. Stone tools. Hearths. Bones of mammoth, reindeer, horse and arctic fox. These were people of the cold steppe, a dry, open grassland with few trees and long winters. They followed the herds, worked stone with great skill, and returned to this river bank again and again. On its own, this would make Sungir an important site. But the ground held something far more personal.

In 1964 the team uncovered a grave. An adult man lay on his back, his body covered in rows of tiny beads carved from mammoth ivory. There were thousands of them, close to three thousand. They seem to have been sewn onto his clothing — a tunic, trousers, perhaps a cap. Around his arms he wore bands of polished ivory. Near his waist lay many pierced teeth of the arctic fox, likely once part of a belt.

Here is the first surprise most visitors never hear. Because the beads stayed in place as the clothing decayed, researchers could trace the outline of the garments themselves. In effect, the beads drew a picture of what he wore. Scholars noticed that his outfit looked rather like the fur clothing of Arctic peoples in recent centuries. Hood, shirt, trousers and boots... a design for survival that may be very, very old.

Then came a second grave, and it was even more astonishing. Two children were buried together in one long, narrow pit, head to head. One was a boy, one a girl, both young. Together they were covered with around ten thousand ivory beads. Beside them lay long spears made from mammoth tusk. Ivory grows in a curve, so making those spears straight required real skill — perhaps soaking or heating the ivory, though nobody knows exactly how.

The children's grave held more. There were ivory discs, small carved animals, and pins. The girl's grave goods included a figure that many describe as a horse. And there was a human thigh bone from an adult, laid in the grave and packed with red ochre. Red ochre is a natural earth pigment, rich in iron, and it appears in burials across the ancient world. Why that bone was placed there remains an open question.

Think about the labour in those beads. Each one had to be cut, shaped and drilled with stone tools. Researchers have experimented with making copies, and their estimates suggest a single bead could take around an hour. Multiply that by thousands. These burials represent many months of skilled work, perhaps by many hands. For people living on the edge of the ice, that was an enormous investment. It tells us these individuals mattered deeply to their community.

Here is the second surprise. In 2017, a team of geneticists published a study of ancient DNA from the Sungir individuals. Many people had assumed the people buried here were one close family. The results suggested otherwise. They were not close relatives — no closer, it seemed, than distant cousins. The researchers proposed that these small hunter groups were part of wider social networks, with people moving between bands. That may have helped them avoid the dangers of inbreeding.

So why were children given such rich burials? We do not know, and honest archaeologists say so. Some suggest status passed down through families, even in a world without kings. Others point to ritual, or to a special event we cannot see. The two children also showed signs of health problems, and the boy and girl may have died at the same time. Every idea is a reconstruction. The ground keeps its silence on the reasons.

Today the site itself is modest. You stand on a slope above the river valley, at the eastern edge of the modern city. A memorial stone marks the place. Much of the original ground was excavated or quarried long ago. The original finds are not on display here in the field. Many of them are held by museums, and you can see reconstructions of the burials and clothing in exhibitions in Vladimir and in Moscow.

Before you leave, look down toward the water. The Klyazma still flows along the same valley, though its course and its banks have changed. Imagine smoke rising from a hearth on this slope, the shapes of mammoths far off on the open grassland, children running in heavy fur. Then turn toward the city behind you, with its traffic and church bells. You are standing on one thin layer of time — and beneath it lie many more.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit museum reconstructions in Vladimir to see the burials.
Wear sturdy shoes; the slope can be muddy clay.
Combine with a trip toward Bogolyubovo along the Klyazma.
Bring a phone map; the site has little signage.
Come in calm weather to enjoy the open valley views.
