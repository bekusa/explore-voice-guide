The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your feet on the Sacred Way. The path climbs in a slow zigzag up the mountainside. The air carries the smell of dry pine and warm stone. Then, at the first sharp bend, a small building comes into view. It looks like a temple in miniature, pale and compact. Its marble catches the light differently from the grey limestone around it. This is the Athenian Treasury, and it is one of the few buildings at Delphi that you can see standing almost whole.

First, a word on what a treasury was. In the ancient Greek world, Delphi was the home of Apollo's oracle. People came from across the Mediterranean to ask the god questions about war, marriage, harvests and new colonies. City-states competed to show their gratitude and their wealth here. Many of them built small, temple-like storehouses along the Sacred Way. Inside, they kept their offerings to Apollo. Think of each treasury as a mix of a safe, a shrine and a billboard.

The billboard part matters. These buildings lined the route every pilgrim walked. A visitor climbing toward the Temple of Apollo passed the treasuries of rival cities one after another. Each building had to make a statement about the city that paid for it. Athens chose a prime spot, right at a bend where the path turns. Anyone walking up the slope would face this building head on. The location was not an accident. It was a carefully chosen stage.

Now look at the building's shape. It follows the Doric order, the oldest and plainest of the classical Greek styles, with sturdy columns and simple, cushion-like capitals. At the front, two columns stand between the ends of the side walls. Architects call this layout "in antis", meaning between the wall ends. Behind them is a single closed room. There is no grand interior. The whole design puts its energy into the outside, where people could see it.

The material is marble from the island of Paros, in the Aegean Sea. Parian marble was prized in antiquity for its fine grain and its glow. Shipping it here took real effort. The stone had to cross the sea and then climb the steep road up from the coast. Using it for a small storehouse was a deliberate display of resources. Step closer and compare it with the local limestone of the terraces. The difference in colour and surface is easy to see.

When exactly was this treasury built? That question is still debated. The ancient travel writer Pausanias visited Delphi in the second century after Christ. He wrote that the Athenians paid for it with spoils from the Battle of Marathon, in 490 BC. At Marathon, Athens defeated a Persian invasion force. Some modern scholars accept that link. Others argue, based on the style of the sculpture, that the building is a little earlier. Both views have serious support.

Whatever the exact date, Marathon is written into the site itself. Look along the south side of the building, beside the path. There you will find a long, low base set against the terrace. An inscription on it records an Athenian dedication from the spoils of the Persians at Marathon. Statues or trophies once stood on top. Today only the base remains. It is easy to walk past, so take a moment to find it.

High on the building ran a frieze of square sculpted panels called metopes. There were about thirty of them. They showed the labours of Heracles, the strongman of Greek myth, and the deeds of Theseus, the legendary hero-king of Athens. Placing Theseus beside Heracles was a quiet piece of political messaging. It presented the Athenian hero as equal to the most famous hero in Greece. The panels you see on the building now are casts. The original carvings are in the Delphi Archaeological Museum nearby.

Here is something many visitors miss. The walls of this treasury are covered in writing. Lean in and look at the marble blocks. You will see lines of carefully cut Greek letters. Over the centuries, the walls served as a public notice board. Inscriptions record honours granted by Delphi to Athenians, and other official texts. The building became an archive in stone, with each new decree added to the space that remained.

And one of those texts is truly rare. Among the inscriptions found on this treasury were hymns to Apollo with musical notation carved above the words. They date from the second century before Christ. These are among the most complete pieces of ancient Greek music that survive anywhere. Scholars have used them to reconstruct how the melodies may have sounded. The originals are in the museum today. Still, it is remarkable to stand where ancient songs were literally written on the wall.

The building you see now is a reconstruction, and that is part of its story. French archaeologists excavated Delphi in the late nineteenth century. They found the treasury collapsed, its blocks scattered on the slope. In the early twentieth century, they rebuilt it, largely using the original marble pieces. The city of Athens helped to fund the work. Look closely and you can see where newer stone fills gaps between the ancient blocks. The patches are slightly different in tone.

Now turn around and take in the setting. Above you rise the Phaedriades, the tall grey cliffs that frame the sanctuary. Below, the slope drops toward a valley filled with olive trees, running down to the sea. In the morning the cliffs stay in shade. Later in the day the light moves across the marble and warms it. Swallows often cut across the air between the ruins. The setting has changed less than the buildings have.

Before you continue up the Sacred Way, look once more at this small building. It was made to impress, to remember a victory, and to keep gifts for a god. Later it held laws, honours and even music. Today it holds your attention for a few quiet minutes. As you walk on toward the Temple of Apollo, glance back at the bend. You will see the treasury exactly as ancient pilgrims saw it, facing the path.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Delphi Archaeological Museum to see the original metopes.
Come early morning before tour groups reach the Sacred Way.
Wear shoes with grip for the steep, uneven stone path.
Stand below the bend to see the pilgrims' original view.
Bring water; the sanctuary slope offers very little shade.
