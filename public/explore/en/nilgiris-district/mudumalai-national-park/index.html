The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry teak leaf, as wide as a dinner plate, drops onto the road with a hollow crack. Somewhere off to the left, a langur barks a short, hoarse warning. The air smells of warm dust, wild grass, and the faint sweetness of flowering shrubs. The light comes through the canopy in broken patches, gold on the red earth. You are in Mudumalai, and the forest is already watching you.

This park sits in the Nilgiris district of Tamil Nadu, in southern India. It lies at the foot of the Nilgiri Hills, below the cool tea country around Ooty. Here the land drops, warms, and opens into rolling forest. The name Mudumalai is often translated as the ancient hills. Whatever its exact origin, the phrase fits. These slopes feel old, patient, and very much alive.

Now for the first surprise. Mudumalai is not an island of forest. It sits close to the point where three Indian states meet: Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and Karnataka. Across its borders lie Bandipur National Park in Karnataka and the Wayanad forests in Kerala. Together they form one continuous green landscape. An elephant walking here does not know about state lines. It simply follows water, grass, and memory.

That larger landscape has a name. It is part of the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve, which was the first biosphere reserve declared in India, in 1986. A biosphere reserve is a protected region where people and nature are managed together, rather than fenced apart. Mudumalai itself was protected as a wildlife sanctuary as early as the 1940s, making it one of the older sanctuaries in the country. In 2007 it was declared a tiger reserve.

Look around at the trees and you will see the forest change with the land. In wetter pockets, the woods grow tall and leafy, with teak, rosewood, and bamboo. In drier parts, the trees thin out and the ground turns to grass and thorny scrub. Each type of forest feeds different animals. This mix is a big reason why so much wildlife lives in such a compact area.

The animal everyone hopes to see is the Asian elephant. This region holds one of the most important elephant populations in Asia. You may see a family group crossing the road, the adults closing in around a calf. You may only see the signs they leave behind: snapped bamboo, stripped bark, or a fresh pile of dung on the tarmac. Even without a sighting, you are standing on an elephant highway.

There is much more to watch for. Herds of chital, the spotted deer, graze in the open clearings. Larger sambar deer move more quietly near the forest edge. The gaur, sometimes called the Indian bison, is the biggest wild cattle in the world, dark and heavily muscled, with pale lower legs that look like white socks. Wild dogs called dhole hunt in packs. And tigers and leopards live here too, though they rarely show themselves.

One quieter animal deserves a moment. High in the canopy, look for a flash of maroon and cream. That is the Malabar giant squirrel, a squirrel almost the length of your arm, tail included. It leaps between branches with surprising grace. Many visitors drive past without noticing. If you hear a loud, rattling call from the treetops, stop and scan the branches. The squirrel is often the source.

Running through the park is the Moyar River. In the dry months it may shrink to pools and channels between pale rocks. After the monsoon it runs full and brown. The river is a lifeline. Animals gather near it at the edges of the day, when the heat eases. On the riverbank at Theppakadu sits one of the most famous places in the park.

This is the Theppakadu elephant camp, one of the oldest working elephant camps in Asia. Here, trained elephants live alongside their keepers, known as mahouts, and their assistants, called kavadis. Many keepers come from local tribal communities with generations of knowledge about elephants. You may see the elephants bathed in the river, scrubbed down, and fed. The bond between elephant and keeper is easy to see. It is built over many years.

And here is the second surprise. This camp reached a global audience through a short documentary called The Elephant Whisperers. The film follows a couple, Bomman and Bellie, as they raise an orphaned baby elephant. In 2023 it won the Academy Award for Best Documentary Short. Suddenly, people around the world knew this small riverside camp. The film showed the patient, everyday care behind every rescued calf.

The forest is not empty of people. Several tribal communities live in and around the reserve, and their ties to this land go back much further than any park boundary. Nearby, small villages such as Masinagudi serve as bases for visitors. You will notice that life here moves with the forest. Roads may close at night to protect wildlife. Drivers slow down at crossings. The forest sets the rules.

The best way to explore is on the forest department safari, in an official vehicle with a guide. Early morning and late afternoon bring the most movement. Keep your voice low and your eyes on the edges, where forest meets clearing. Do not leave the vehicle, and never feed or chase animals. The rewards here come to the patient. A long wait by a quiet pool can give you more than a fast drive.

Before you leave, pause for a moment. Listen again to the forest around you. Perhaps a peacock is calling from somewhere unseen. Perhaps the wind is moving through the bamboo with a soft wooden knock. Look down at the road and notice the tracks pressed into the dust. Something large walked here not long ago. As you move on, keep watching the trees. Mudumalai is often still watching you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book the official forest department safari for wildlife access.
Come early morning or late afternoon for animal movement.
Keep voices low and stay inside safari vehicles.
Bring binoculars to spot squirrels and birds in canopy.
Check road closure rules before planning any night travel.
