The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the trees, the River Tay runs fast and dark over its stones. Rooks call from the tall branches above the lawn. Then the grey walls come into view, roofless and open to the sky. Grass grows where people once stood to pray. Out here, a sound carries further than you expect... a footstep on gravel, a door closing somewhere inside the choir.

This is Dunkeld Cathedral. It stands on the north bank of the Tay, at the edge of a small town in Highland Perthshire. Part of the building is a ruin. Part of it is still a working parish church. That split is the first thing to understand about this place. One half carries the damage of history. The other half carries on as a living building, with services, hymns and a congregation.

The story here starts long before these walls. Christian worship on this spot goes back to an early monastery, in the age of the Picts. The Picts were the people who lived in northern and eastern Scotland before the kingdom of Scotland took shape. Dunkeld sat at an important point, where the Highlands meet the gentler lowland country. A church here was a statement of power as well as faith.

In the mid-ninth century, the chronicles connect Dunkeld with Kenneth MacAlpin, a king often remembered as an early founder of the Scottish kingdom. According to these sources, he brought relics of Saint Columba here. Columba was the Irish monk who founded the monastery on the island of Iona. Viking raids made the western coast dangerous, so a safer home inland made sense. For a time, that made Dunkeld one of the most important religious centres in the land.

The building in front of you is much later. The cathedral was built in stages, roughly from the thirteenth century to the early sixteenth century. The choir, the eastern end, is the oldest major part. The long nave and the great tower came later, in the fifteenth century. If you look closely, you can see the change in styles. Builders over almost two and a half centuries added, adjusted and finished what earlier masons had started.

Now walk into the roofless nave. The tall pointed arches stand in rows, but they hold up nothing. The windows have lost most of their stone tracery, the decorative stonework that once held the glass. This damage came with the Scottish Reformation in 1560, when the country broke with the Catholic Church. Images, altars and furnishings were removed. Over time the nave lost its roof and was left to the weather. Today Historic Environment Scotland looks after the ruin.

Here is a detail many visitors walk past. Stand back and look at the great west window, at the far end of the nave. It does not sit neatly in the middle of the wall. It is pushed slightly to one side, to make room for the tower beside it. The medieval builders solved a practical problem in plain sight. And they left an odd, lopsided beauty behind.

The choir was saved. It was repaired and roofed, and it became the parish church, which it still is today. Step inside and the mood changes at once. The air is still. The light falls through the windows onto pale stone and dark wood. In here, look for the tomb of Alexander Stewart, a son of King Robert the Second. History remembers him by a fierce nickname, the Wolf of Badenoch.

The Wolf earned that name. In 1390 he burned the town and cathedral of Elgin, far to the north, after a quarrel with its bishop. Yet here he lies, inside another cathedral, in full armour carved in stone. Look at the effigy, the stone figure on the tomb. His hands are together, his face turned upward, a lion at his feet. It is a calm figure for such a violent man... and that contrast is part of the story.

Dunkeld had a quieter, more scholarly side too. One of its bishops, in the early sixteenth century, was Gavin Douglas, a poet. He translated Virgil's Aeneid, the great Latin epic about the hero Aeneas, into Scots. Scots is the language closely related to English that people spoke across lowland Scotland. His translation is a landmark of Scottish literature. So the same building holds the memory of a fire-raising lord and a careful scholar of words.

The town itself saw fighting in 1689. After a Jacobite victory at Killiecrankie, just up the valley, Jacobite forces attacked Dunkeld. The Jacobites supported the exiled Stuart king, James the Seventh. A government regiment, the Cameronians, held the town. The streets burned, and much of old Dunkeld was destroyed, but the defenders did not give way. Their commander, William Cleland, was killed in the fighting. He was buried in the cathedral nave.

This is the second surprise. Look carefully around the grounds and inside the buildings for carved stones much older than the cathedral. Some of these are early medieval sculpted stones from the Pictish era. One well-known example shows rows of figures, often described as apostles. These stones survived the monastery, the rebuilding, the Reformation and the fire. They are the oldest voices here, older than any arch you can see.

Before you leave, walk down toward the river. The lawns slope gently to the water, and tall trees frame the ruin. Many of these woods were planted by the Dukes of Atholl, who owned the estate around Dunkeld. They were enthusiastic planters of larch, a tree new to Scotland in the eighteenth century. Downstream, the stone road bridge over the Tay was built in the early nineteenth century by the engineer Thomas Telford.

So take one last look. The open nave, with the sky for a ceiling. The roofed choir, still in use. The river moving past, as it did when the first monks arrived. As you walk back toward the town, listen again for the water and the rooks. They were here before the stone, and they will likely be here long after.

Practical tips

Combine your visit with a woodland walk along the Tay.
