The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement for a moment and listen. Behind you, Xujiahui roars with traffic, shopping malls and the hum of the metro below your feet. In front of you, two spires of red brick and grey stone rise against the Shanghai sky. The noise does not stop here. The cathedral simply stands inside it, calm and upright, as if it had been waiting for the city to grow up around it.

This is Saint Ignatius Cathedral, the seat of the Catholic diocese of Shanghai. Many locals simply call it the Xujiahui Cathedral, after the neighbourhood it anchors. It is named for Ignatius of Loyola, the sixteenth-century Spanish soldier turned priest who founded the Jesuit order. That detail matters, because this entire corner of Shanghai was shaped by the Jesuits for more than two centuries.

To understand why a Gothic cathedral stands here, start with the name of the district itself. Xujiahui means, roughly, the meeting place of the Xu family. The family's most famous member was Xu Guangqi, a scholar and senior official of the Ming dynasty. He was one of the first prominent Chinese converts to Catholicism. He worked closely with the Italian Jesuit Matteo Ricci, and together they translated the first books of Euclid's geometry into Chinese.

Xu Guangqi is buried not far from here, in a park that carries his name. His descendants kept the faith, and the land around the family estate became a natural base for the Jesuits when they returned to Shanghai in the nineteenth century. Over the following decades they built a whole Catholic world in this place. There was a school, an orphanage, a printing workshop, an observatory, a library — and finally, this cathedral.

Construction began in the first decade of the twentieth century, and the church was completed in 1910. The design follows the Gothic Revival style. That is a nineteenth-century European fashion that copied the pointed arches and tall towers of medieval cathedrals. Look at the façade and you will see the classic vocabulary: pointed windows, a large rose window at the centre, and buttresses that brace the walls from outside. It was built to impress, and for many years it was among the largest churches in East Asia.

Now look more closely at the materials. The walls are red brick, trimmed with lighter stone. In the afternoon sun the brick turns warm, almost copper. Under a grey Shanghai sky it looks darker and heavier, more like a northern European church. Photographers often come back at different hours just to see this change. The two spires are the strongest lines in the composition, and they pull the eye straight upward.

Here is the first thing many visitors do not realise. The spires you see today are not the originals. During the Cultural Revolution in the 1960s, Red Guards attacked the cathedral. They tore down the spires, smashed the stained glass and damaged much of the interior. For years afterwards the building was not used as a church at all. It served other purposes, including storage, while religious practice in China was suppressed.

The cathedral reopened for worship around the end of the 1970s, as China's policies began to change. The spires were rebuilt in the early 1980s. So when you look up at them, you are looking at a repair — a deliberate act of restoration after a period of deep loss. The brick below and the towers above belong to two different eras, joined into one silhouette.

The second surprise is inside, in the windows. The original stained glass was destroyed, and for a long time the windows held plain glass. In the early 2000s, new stained glass was installed. Instead of copying European designs, many of the new panels blend Christian scenes with Chinese visual elements. Look for familiar Bible stories rendered with Chinese motifs and lettering. It is a quiet statement that the faith here is not simply imported, but local.

If the doors are open and you are allowed inside, notice the scale of the nave — the long central hall where the congregation sits. Rows of tall columns carry the pointed arches overhead. The light enters in coloured patches and moves slowly across the stone floor. On a Sunday you may find Mass in progress, often well attended. Visitors are usually expected to keep quiet and avoid photography during services, so watch the signs and follow the lead of the staff.

Step back outside and look at the wider neighbourhood. The cathedral is only one survivor of the old Jesuit complex. Nearby stands the Xujiahui Library, once known to foreigners as the Bibliotheca Zi-Ka-Wei, which held a remarkable collection of books in Western and Chinese languages. The Jesuits also ran a weather observatory here. Its reports once helped ships along the China coast prepare for typhoons. Some of these old buildings can still be visited.

This mix is what makes Xujiahui unusual. In one small area you can trace a story that runs from a Ming dynasty scholar, through French Jesuit missionaries, through revolution and destruction, to a modern district of glass towers and department stores. The cathedral sits at the centre of that story. It is not a museum piece. It is a working church, with weddings, baptisms and daily prayer, in one of the busiest commercial hubs of the city.

Before you leave, turn around once more and look at the cathedral from across the street. Notice how the brick holds the light, how the rebuilt spires line up with the old walls. Listen again to the traffic, the voices and the footsteps around you. Then, as you walk away toward the metro or the shops, glance back one last time. The two spires will stay in view for a while — a fixed point in a city that never stops changing.
