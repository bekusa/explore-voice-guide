The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the paving stones of People's Square. Traffic hums along the wide avenue behind you. In front of you, a wall of glass catches the sky, and above it, a sweeping white roof curves upward at both ends, as if lifted by an invisible hand. This is the Shanghai Grand Theatre. Before we talk about who built it, just notice how light it looks. A building this large should feel heavy... and somehow, it does not.

That sense of lightness is deliberate. The theatre was designed by the French architect Jean-Marie Charpentier, working with Chinese partners. It opened in 1998. At that time, Shanghai was changing faster than almost any city on earth. Towers were rising across the river in Pudong. The city wanted a stage that could host the great opera houses and orchestras of the world. It also wanted a building that looked forward, not backward — and this was the answer.

Look up at the roof again. Many people say it looks like a bowl turned toward the sky. Some describe it as a vessel ready to receive gifts from heaven. Others see a crown, or the upturned eaves of a traditional Chinese hall, simplified into one clean gesture. The architect played with these readings on purpose. The curve feels modern. Yet the idea of a roof that rises at its edges is very old in Chinese building.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. The building combines a square base with a curved, rounded roof. In classical Chinese thought, there is a well-known idea — heaven is round, and earth is square. You can find this pattern in temples, coins, and city plans across China. The Grand Theatre carries that ancient idea in glass and steel. Once you see it, you will notice the same pairing in other buildings around this square.

In fact, turn your head and look across People's Square. Not far from here stands the Shanghai Museum building, with its round top sitting on a square body. It follows the same old idea of round heaven and square earth. Standing between these two buildings, you are inside a small conversation about Chinese identity. One building uses the shape of an ancient bronze vessel. The other uses a floating modern roof. Both say something about where Shanghai comes from.

Now think about the ground under your feet. Long before this was a square of fountains and lawns, this area was a horse racecourse. It was built in the nineteenth century, during the era of the foreign concessions, when British and other foreign communities controlled large parts of the city. Crowds gathered here to watch races and to see and be seen. After 1949, the racecourse was transformed into public space. The theatre now stands on land that has changed its purpose many times.

Let us come closer to the glass. The walls of the theatre are made largely of transparent panels. During the day, they reflect clouds, trees, and passing buses. You can see the shapes of the lobby inside — staircases, balconies, and people moving between floors. The architect wanted the building to feel open to the city, not closed behind thick stone. A theatre is usually a private world. Here, the boundary between the street and the stage feels thin.

And this brings us to the second surprise. The theatre changes completely after dark. When the lights come on inside, the glass walls glow like a lantern. The white roof seems to hover above a box of warm light. From across the square, you can see the outlines of people inside, walking up to their seats. Many locals say this is the best way to see the building — not in the afternoon sun, but at night, glowing from within.

Inside, the theatre holds several performance spaces. The main hall is large and built for the big productions — full opera, ballet, symphony concerts, and touring musicals from Broadway and London's West End. Smaller halls host chamber music, drama, and more intimate shows. Over the years, many of the world's best-known companies and orchestras have performed here. For many Shanghai families, this is where they saw their first ballet or heard their first live orchestra.

The programme also mixes East and West. On one evening, you might find an Italian opera or a Russian ballet. On another, you might find Chinese classical music, or a modern Chinese dance production. Peking opera — the traditional Chinese theatre form, with painted faces, bright costumes, and high, sharp singing — also has its place in the city's cultural life. This mixture fits Shanghai well. For more than a century, the city has been a meeting point between Chinese and foreign culture.

If you are not attending a show, you can still enjoy the building from the outside. Walk slowly around its edges. Notice how the roof looks different from every angle. From the front, it looks wide and calm. From the side, the upturned edges become sharper and more dramatic. The plaza in front gives you space to step back. It is one of the few places in central Shanghai where a major building has room to breathe around it.

As you walk, listen to the square itself. You may hear the splash of fountains, a street musician, or groups of older residents chatting on the benches. People's Square is one of the busiest crossings in the city. Several metro lines meet underground, and thousands of people pass through every day. The theatre sits in the middle of this daily rush, a quiet white shape above the noise of a working city.

Before you leave, take one more look at the roof. Watch how the light moves across its curved white surface as clouds pass. Notice the reflections in the glass below — perhaps you can even see yourself, small against the building. Then turn around and look at the city behind you: the towers, the trees, the traffic. This theatre was built as a stage for the world's performers. But standing here, you are part of the scene too.

Practical tips

Book performance tickets well ahead for major touring shows.

Pair your visit with a walk through People's Square.
