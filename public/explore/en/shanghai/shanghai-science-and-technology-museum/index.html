The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go inside. The traffic on Century Avenue runs wide and steady. There is wind across the open plaza, and the soft echo of footsteps on pale stone. In front of you, a long, low building curves away. Its glass and metal catch the Pudong sky, and somewhere inside a giant sphere waits. Welcome to the Shanghai Science and Technology Museum, one of the largest science museums in China.

First, a little about where you are standing. Thirty-odd years ago, this side of the Huangpu River was mostly farmland, warehouses and low workers' housing. In 1990, the Chinese government launched the Pudong New Area as a showcase for the country's opening to the world. Towers, highways and metro lines followed at remarkable speed. This museum belongs to that story. It was planned as a statement that Shanghai would build knowledge, not only skyscrapers.

The museum opened to the public at the end of 2001. But here is the first surprise. A few weeks before the public came in, these halls hosted world leaders. In October 2001, Shanghai held the leaders' meeting of APEC, the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum. The main sessions took place inside this building. So, before children ever pressed its buttons, presidents and prime ministers walked these same corridors.

Now look at the shape of the building. It does not rise straight up like the towers of Lujiazui to the west. Instead, it spreads low and climbs gently in a spiral form. The design is often described as a symbol of growth, a line that starts from the ground and keeps rising. From the plaza, notice how the roof lifts toward one end. It feels less like a box and more like a movement caught in steel.

Step inside, and the central hall opens up around you. The most memorable feature here is the great glass sphere. It sits in the atrium like a planet or an egg, depending on who you ask. Light falls through the glass roof and moves across its surface during the day. Many visitors walk straight past it toward the exhibits. Take a minute instead. Stand underneath and look up. The scale of the hall becomes clear only from here.

The museum is organised into a series of themed zones. Rather than one long timeline, it works like a set of separate worlds. Some focus on life on Earth. Others cover the human body, information technology, robots, space and the forces of physics. You do not need to see everything. In fact, very few people manage it in one visit. It is better to choose two or three zones and give them real attention.

One of the most popular areas is the rainforest. Here the museum recreates a tropical forest environment indoors. You walk among tall plant forms, rock faces and running water. The air feels different, and the light drops to a greener tone. It is a good reminder that China itself contains rainforest, in the far south-west of the country. Many visitors from abroad do not expect that.

Nearby, the animal halls take a quieter, older approach. Rows of preserved animals stand in displays arranged by habitat. Some visitors find these halls a little old-fashioned. Others love them for exactly that reason. There is something calm about standing face to face with a large cat or a bird of prey, frozen in mid-step. Children often go silent here, which in a science museum is a rare and special sound.

The technology zones are louder and brighter. Here the museum turns playful. You will find hands-on stations where you can test reflexes, play with light, or watch machines respond to your movements. The robot area has long been a favourite with families. Robots in these displays have been shown playing music, solving puzzles and interacting with visitors. Exhibits do change over time, so treat what you see today as the museum's current snapshot of the future.

Here is the second surprise. The museum is also known for its large-format cinemas. Several theatres show science and nature films on very large screens, including dome and three-dimensional formats. These usually need a separate ticket. Many visitors only discover this at the door, when the shows have already sold out. If a film interests you, check the schedule early in your visit, then plan the rest of your route around it.

The museum also has a wider family. It manages the Shanghai Natural History Museum, which moved in 2015 to a striking new building in Jing'an, west of the river. Together, the two institutions form one of the largest public science organisations in the country. If today's visit leaves you curious about fossils and dinosaurs, that sister museum is the natural next step.

Before you leave, there is one more local layer worth knowing. Below your feet runs Metro Line 2, with a station named after the museum itself. For years, the underground shopping area by the station became famous for a different reason. It filled with small stalls selling bags, watches, clothes and electronics, and bargaining was part of the experience. The market has changed and shrunk over time. Still, it remains a strange, very Shanghai contrast: serious science above ground, haggling below.

So take one last look as you walk out onto the plaza. Behind you, the glass sphere holds the afternoon light. In front of you, Century Avenue runs straight toward the towers of Lujiazui. Century Park spreads out nearby, with lawns, trees and quiet water. A generation ago, almost none of this existed. The building at your back was made to ask a simple question about that change... what comes next? As you walk on, notice the city around you. In a way, it is the museum's largest exhibit.
