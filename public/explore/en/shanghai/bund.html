The guide

The story

Stand at the railing and face the water. The river moves slow and brown below you. Boats slide past, and their horns sound low and long. Behind you rises a wall of old stone buildings, pale in the daylight. Across the water, towers of glass climb into the sky. You are standing on the seam between two centuries. Few places hold such a view.

This is the Bund. It runs along the western bank of the Huangpu River, in the center of Shanghai. The wide walkway stretches for about one and a half kilometers. People come here to walk, to rest, and simply to look. The view changes through the day, from soft morning haze to the bright lights of night. It rewards a slow, unhurried visit.

The name comes from an old word for an embankment, a raised bank along muddy water. Long ago, this strip of land lay soft and wet beside the river. Foreign traders arrived in the 1840s, after the city was opened to outside trade. They drained the ground and built along the shore. Year by year, the muddy bank became a proud street of banks and trading houses.

By the early twentieth century, this was a money center for much of Asia. Some called it the Wall Street of the East. Banks, customs houses, and shipping firms lined the road. Each one wished to look strong and lasting. So they built in heavy stone, with thick columns, round domes, and tall bronze doors. Their architects came from Britain, France, and beyond.

Look closely at the buildings now. No two are quite the same. You will see Greek columns and rounded arches. You will see clean flat lines from the 1920s. One roof rises in a green pyramid. Another holds a clock tower that still keeps time. Stone lions and carved flowers decorate the lower walls. Together they form an open-air museum of styles.

The old Customs House holds that clock. It was finished in 1927. Its bells were modeled on the famous ones in London. They once rang across the whole district, and workers set their watches by the sound. Beside it stood a great bank, opened in 1923. Its dome was raised to show power and trust. For decades these two were the proudest addresses on the street.

Near one grand doorway sits a pair of bronze lions. They were cast in the 1920s for the bank behind them. Over the years, countless hands have touched their paws. The metal there is rubbed bright and smooth. People say a single touch brings good luck. Reach out as you pass, if you wish to join them.

Now turn and face the river once more. On the far bank lies Pudong. Fifty years ago, this side held farmland and low warehouses. Today it is a forest of towers. One looks like a giant bottle opener. Another carries round spheres on a long shaft. A third twists slowly as it climbs out of sight. This contrast is the whole point of the Bund.

The promenade itself rests on a raised platform. It was lifted and widened to hold back the river in flood. From up here, your eye runs clear in both directions. Benches invite you to sit and watch the water. Cargo ships, ferries, and tour boats share the busy channel. Tugboats push long lines of barges upstream. Ferry whistles echo off the stone behind you.

Come back after dark to see it all change. The old buildings glow with warm yellow light. Across the water, the towers flash and shift in color. The river holds both pictures at once, doubled on its surface. Crowds gather along the railing with cameras raised. This is one of the best loved evening walks in the whole city.

The walk is free and open to all. There are no gates and no tickets here. Come at dawn, and the path is almost yours alone. Come at night, and you join a cheerful crowd. Early morning light is kindest for photographs of Pudong. The old stone faces glow warmest in the last hour before dark.

Think for a moment of all who passed here before you. Traders in stiff collars and sailors off the ships. Families out walking on a quiet Sunday. Students, soldiers, and travelers from far away. For more than a hundred years, this has been a place to see and be seen.

Stand quietly for a moment before you go. Behind you, stone from one century. Before you, glass from another. Between them runs the slow brown river, older than both. Shanghai built its fortune on these banks, and lost and rebuilt it more than once. The water, though, keeps moving, the way it always has.
