The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, the forest reaches you through sound. There is the dry rustle of sal leaves underfoot, the sharp call of a peacock somewhere out of sight, and the steady hum of insects in the tall grass. The air is damp and green, and it smells of earth and river water. Here, at the edge of the Himalayan foothills, the land is flat and wide. Yet on a clear day, you can sometimes see the blue line of distant mountains to the north.

You are standing in Gorumara National Park, in the Jalpaiguri district of northern West Bengal. This corner of the state belongs to a region called the Dooars. The name is often linked to the word for doors or gateways, because these plains were once the passages into Bhutan and the hills beyond. It is a land of tea gardens, fast rivers and forest. Gorumara sits right in the middle of it, a relatively small park with a large reputation.

What makes this place special is its shape. Gorumara is not one single kind of landscape. It is a patchwork. Dense stands of sal trees, tall and straight with large leaves, give way to open grassland. Rivers cut through both, leaving sandy banks and shallow channels. This mix matters. Grazing animals need the grass, larger animals need the shade of the forest, and nearly everything needs the water. Where these three meet, wildlife gathers.

The animal most visitors hope to see is the Indian one-horned rhinoceros. It is a heavy, armoured-looking creature, with folds of thick grey skin that look almost like plates. This species was once hunted close to extinction across much of its range. Today, Gorumara is one of the few places in West Bengal where it survives. If you see one grazing quietly at the edge of the grass, take a moment. You are looking at a conservation success, still fragile, still being protected.

The rhino is not alone. Asian elephants move through these forests, sometimes in family groups, sometimes as solitary bulls. The gaur, often called the Indian bison, also lives here. It is one of the largest wild cattle in the world, dark and muscular, with pale lower legs that look as if it is wearing white socks. Many first-time visitors are surprised by its size. Deer graze in the open spaces, and wild boar push through the undergrowth.

Now look up. Gorumara is also a place for birds. Peafowl are common, and the males are easy to spot in their bright blue and green. Hornbills, with their heavy curved beaks, sometimes fly across the canopy with a loud whooshing sound from their wings. Kingfishers wait along the riverbanks. Even if the large animals stay hidden on your visit, the birdlife alone can fill a morning. Bring patience, and let your ears guide your eyes.

Water shapes everything here. The Murti and Jaldhaka rivers run through and around the park. They come down from the hills to the north, cold and clear, carrying stones and sand. In the dry months they spread into shallow braided channels. During the monsoon they rise quickly and can change their course. Animals come to these rivers to drink and to cool down, which is why many of the best viewing points face the water.

Those viewing points are a big part of the Gorumara experience. Visitors do not usually walk freely through the forest. Instead, guided safaris take you to watchtowers built at the edge of grassland or water. One of the best known is the Jatraprasad watchtower. It is named after a famous elephant who served the forest department here, and locals still speak of him with affection. Small details like this tell you how close the people of this region feel to the animals.

When you climb a watchtower, the experience changes. Down on the ground, the forest feels close and enclosed. Up on the platform, the view opens out. You look across a wide stretch of grass toward a salt lick or a pool. Then you wait. Conversation drops to whispers. Everyone watches the tree line. Sometimes nothing appears for a long time. And then, very slowly, a shape steps out of the shadows. That moment of waiting is part of the reward.

The park also sits within a living landscape. Just outside its boundaries, you will see tea gardens with neat rows of low green bushes and tall shade trees. Villages and small towns surround the forest. Lataguri, close by, serves as a common base for visitors, with lodges and safari counters. Elephants do not always respect boundaries, and people here live alongside wild animals in ways that are not always easy. Protecting this forest depends on these communities as much as on any fence or ranger.

In some places near the park, local communities share their culture with visitors. Evenings may bring music and dance performed by people whose families have lived in the Dooars for generations. These traditions are older than the national park itself. They remind you that this forest was never empty wilderness. It has always been a place where people and animals shared the land, sometimes peacefully, sometimes in conflict.

The forest here has been protected in stages over many decades. It was first set aside as a sanctuary and later raised to the status of a national park. Over that time, careful management helped the rhino population recover. Forest guards patrol against poaching, and grassland is managed so that grazing animals have enough to eat. None of this is visible at first glance. But the rhino you may see today is the result of many years of quiet, steady work.

Before you leave, pause once more. Notice the light falling through the sal leaves, soft and broken into patches. Listen again for the peacock, the insects, the distant run of the river. Look at the line where the forest meets the grass. Somewhere in that shade, animals are watching you just as carefully as you watched them. As you walk back toward the road, let the forest stay with you for a few steps longer.
