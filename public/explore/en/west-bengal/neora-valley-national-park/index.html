The guide

The story

Listen first. Water drips from a moss-heavy branch somewhere above you. A bird calls once, then stops. The air is cool and damp, and it smells of wet bark, fern and soil that has not seen full sunlight in a long time. Mist moves between the trunks so slowly that you only notice it when a tree you could see a moment ago is gone. This is Neora Valley National Park, in the hills of northern West Bengal.

The park sits in the Kalimpong hills, east of the better-known town of Darjeeling. It was declared a national park in 1986. On a map it is not large, roughly ninety square kilometres. Yet those kilometres climb a long way. The lower edges hold warm, broadleaf forest. The upper ridges rise above three thousand metres, where the air is thin and the winters bring snow. In a single long walk, the forest around you can change completely.

The valley takes its name from the Neora River. The river begins high in these hills and runs south through steep, wooded slopes. For a long stretch there are no towns along its banks, no farms and very few roads. That emptiness is the point. Much of the valley is difficult to reach, and that difficulty has protected it. People often describe Neora as one of the least disturbed forests in eastern India — and when you stand here, the quiet makes that easy to believe.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. At the top of the park sits a high ridge known as Rachela, the highest point of the protected area. From that ridge, the borders of three places meet. West Bengal lies on one side, the Indian state of Sikkim on another, and the kingdom of Bhutan on the third. On a clear morning, you look across a landscape that belongs to three different governments, yet the forest itself does not change at the line.

Now look at the trees nearest to you. In the middle altitudes, the forest is a mix of oak, maple, magnolia and tall rhododendron. Many trunks carry a thick coat of moss. Small ferns grow from that moss, and orchids grow among the ferns. A single branch can hold a whole garden of plants that never touch the ground. Botanists call these plants epiphytes — plants that live on other plants, taking water from the air and the rain.

In spring, the rhododendrons change the colour of the slopes. Their flowers appear in deep red, pink and pale cream, depending on the species and the altitude. Higher up, dense stands of bamboo crowd the ground beneath the trees. Bamboo matters here for a reason that has nothing to do with scenery. It is food, shelter and cover for the animal that most visitors hope to see.

That animal is the red panda. It is small, about the size of a house cat, with rust-red fur, a white face and a long striped tail. Neora Valley is one of the places in India where it still lives in the wild. Red pandas spend much of their time high in the trees, and they are shy and well hidden. Most visitors never see one. Knowing it may be somewhere above you, curled on a mossy branch, changes how you look at every tree.

The red panda is not alone. Wildlife surveys have recorded the clouded leopard here, a secretive cat with large, cloud-shaped markings on its coat. The Himalayan black bear moves through these forests too, along with several kinds of deer and wild goat-antelope. You are very unlikely to meet the larger animals on a day visit. What you will hear, almost certainly, is birds. The valley is known among birdwatchers for its wide range of Himalayan species.

Most people enter the park from the hill village of Lava, on its western side. Lava sits on a ridge, and on cold mornings the village wakes slowly under cloud. A Buddhist monastery stands in the village, and its prayer flags carry the wind's colour across the hillside — blue, white, red, green and yellow. Many travellers spend a night here before going into the forest. The other main approach comes from the lower, warmer side of the hills, near the tea and forest country to the south.

Visiting Neora is not like visiting a city park. Entry is controlled by the forest department, and you need a permit to go in. Vehicles and walkers follow set routes, and a local guide usually accompanies groups deeper into the forest. The park also closes for part of each year, during the heavy monsoon rains, when roads wash out and the forest needs rest. These rules exist because the valley is fragile. They are a big part of why it still looks this way.

The weather shapes every visit. On one morning, you may see far across the ridges toward the snow peaks. An hour later, cloud can close in until you see only the next few trees. Rain can arrive in any season. The path underfoot is often soft with leaf litter, and in damp places it is slippery with mud and roots. Walk slowly. The slower you move, the more the forest shows you — a beetle on a leaf, a moving branch, a sudden flash of a bird's wing.

There is one more thing worth knowing. The forests of the Neora valley do more than shelter animals. They catch rain and mist and release the water slowly into streams. People in the towns and villages below depend on that water. So when you hear water running through the moss here, you are hearing something that reaches far beyond the park boundary — into kitchens and fields many kilometres away.

Before you move on, stand still for one more minute. Look up into the canopy, where the light turns green as it passes through the leaves. Notice the moss on the nearest trunk, how thick and soft it is. Listen for the river, or for a bird you cannot name. When you walk back toward the road, watch how the forest thins and the sky opens again. You are leaving one of the quietest places in these hills — and that quiet is the thing worth carrying home.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrange your forest department entry permit before arriving.
Stay overnight in Lava for an early morning start.
Avoid planning a trip during the monsoon closure.
Wear waterproof shoes with good grip on muddy trails.
Hire a local guide for walks beyond the entry zone.
