The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across an open courtyard of grass. Somewhere, a crow calls from a broken arch. Under your feet, the ground is soft and green, but all around you stand rows of dark stone pillars and walls of weathered brick. You are in Pandua, north of Malda town in West Bengal. This is the Adina Mosque — or what remains of it. And what remains is still enormous.

It helps to know where you are standing in history. In the fourteenth century, Bengal was not ruled from Delhi. It was an independent sultanate, with its own coins, its own court, and its own ambitions. For a time, Pandua was its capital. The rulers of this period belonged to the Ilyas Shahi dynasty, a line of sultans who held Bengal for much of that century and beyond.

The man behind this mosque was Sultan Sikandar Shah, the second ruler of that dynasty. An inscription connected to the building dates its construction to the 1370s. That was a confident moment for Bengal. The sultanate had resisted armies sent from Delhi. Building a mosque on this scale was a political statement as much as a religious one. It told the world that Bengal was a power in its own right.

Here is the first surprise. When it was finished, the Adina Mosque was among the largest mosques ever built on the Indian subcontinent. Many historians describe it as the largest of its time in the region. Look at the length of the courtyard around you. Try to picture every bay of it filled with worshippers on a Friday. In fact, the name itself is often linked to the Persian word for Friday — the day of congregational prayer.

The plan is simple and very old. A huge open courtyard sits at the centre. It is surrounded on all sides by covered aisles, held up by rows of pillars. This kind of layout is called hypostyle, which simply means a roof supported by many columns. Originally, those aisles carried hundreds of small brick domes. Most of the domes are gone now. What you see today are the pillars and walls that once held them up.

Turn toward the west. That is the direction of Mecca, and that side is the prayer hall — the heart of the building. In the middle of it, there was once a tall central nave, covered by a great barrel vault. A barrel vault is a long, curved roof, like the inside of a tunnel. That vault has collapsed. Earthquakes and centuries of neglect brought much of the roof down. But the massive opening of the central arch still frames the sky.

Now look closely at the stone. Much of the mosque is built of brick, but the lower walls and many pillars are faced with dark stone, often basalt. Here is the second surprise. Some of these stones clearly came from older buildings. On certain blocks, you can find carved figures and motifs that belong to earlier Hindu or Buddhist temples. Reusing older material was common in medieval architecture across many cultures. Here, it means the walls carry traces of more than one religious past.

Walk along the western wall and look at the prayer niches. These niches are called mihrabs. They show worshippers which direction to face. At Adina, the mihrabs are carved in stone with great care. You will see layered arches, hanging lamp motifs, and fine bands of floral and geometric pattern. The carving is crisp in some places and softened in others. Rain, monsoon heat, and time have all done their work on it.

One of the most unusual features is a raised platform inside the prayer hall, often called the Badshah-ka-Takht, meaning the King's Throne. This was a royal gallery. It allowed the sultan and his household to pray apart from the crowd, lifted above the main floor. Look for the pillars that hold this platform up. They are short, heavy, and close together. A royal space inside a public mosque says a lot about how this sultan wanted to be seen.

Just beyond the western wall, there is a small square chamber. Many accounts identify it as the tomb of Sikandar Shah himself. If that is correct, the builder lies beside his own great project. It is a quiet room compared with the scale of the courtyard. Stand at its threshold for a moment. The contrast between the vast public space and this small private one is easy to feel.

The mosque did not stay at the centre of power for long. The capital of the sultanate later moved back to Gaur, further south. Pandua slowly lost its importance. Over the centuries, jungle and farmland crept close to the ruins. Today the site is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India, which has cleared and stabilised much of it. The green lawn you see is a modern addition. In its working years, this courtyard would have been paved and crowded.

As you walk, notice how the light changes the place. In the early morning, the dark stone looks almost black, and the brick turns a deep orange. By midday, the shadows under the remaining arches become sharp and short. Families from nearby towns often come here on holidays. Children run between the pillars. Couples sit on the steps. The mosque is a ruin, but it is not empty. It is still a place where people gather.

There is something honest about a building like this. Its roof is gone. Its domes are gone. Yet the rhythm of its pillars still tells you exactly how it worked. Row after row, bay after bay, the structure explains itself. You do not need the full roof to understand the scale of the ambition. The plan alone shows you what a fourteenth-century Bengali sultan believed his kingdom could achieve.

Before you leave, stand once more in the open courtyard. Face the great broken arch of the prayer hall. Listen to the wind in the grass and the birds in the stone. Six centuries ago, this space was filled with voices answering a single call to prayer. Today it holds sky, light, and a few quiet visitors. As you walk out, look back at the long line of pillars. They are still standing in their rows... still waiting for a roof that is no longer there.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning for cooler air and softer light.
Wear comfortable shoes for uneven stone and grass.
Carry water; there is little shade in the courtyard.
Dress modestly out of respect for the religious site.
Combine your visit with the ruins of nearby Gaur.
