The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind you, footsteps tap along the top of the Roman wall. In front of you, pale grey granite rises in two tall towers. The stone feels cool even on a warm Galician afternoon, and in the soft northern light it can look almost silver. You are standing before the Cathedral of Santa María of Lugo. For more than eight hundred years, people have come to this spot to worship and to rest.

First, a quick look at the setting. The cathedral sits right against the inner side of Lugo's Roman walls. They are the only Roman city walls in the world that still circle a town completely. UNESCO added them to its World Heritage list in 2000. The church and the wall stand so close together that you can walk the rampart and look straight across at the towers. Two thousand years of history share one small corner here.

The story of the present building begins in the twelfth century. In 1129, a master builder known as Raimundo of Monforte signed an agreement to work on a new cathedral. It was built in the Romanesque style. That is the medieval style of thick walls, rounded arches and solid, earthbound weight. Work on great churches like this took generations. So the builders who finished it were not the ones who started it. You can see this in the stone itself, because the styles change as you walk through.

The facade in front of you looks nothing like the twelfth century. It is Neoclassical, a style from the eighteenth century that borrowed the calm columns and clean lines of ancient Greece and Rome. The design came from Ventura Rodríguez, one of the leading architects of the Spanish court. It replaced an older entrance. Some visitors feel these sober towers clash with the medieval church behind them. Others think they look right beside the Roman wall. Notice which side of that debate you are on.

Now walk around to the north side. This is where many visitors find the oldest treasure, and many walk past it. Over the Romanesque doorway, Christ sits in majesty inside an almond-shaped frame. Look just below him. A carved stone hangs down from the middle of the doorway like a pendant. On it is a scene of the Last Supper. A carving that hangs in the air like this is rare. Early sculptors usually kept their figures firmly attached to the wall.

Step inside, and let your eyes adjust. The nave is long and narrow, and the light falls in slowly from above. This is the Romanesque core of the cathedral, with heavy piers and rounded arches. Your footsteps echo off the granite. As you move toward the far end, the architecture changes. The east end opens into a curved walkway behind the main altar, called an ambulatory. It was rebuilt in the Gothic style, with taller, pointed arches that pull more light into the space.

Here is something that makes this cathedral different from almost any other. For centuries, Lugo has held a special privilege. The consecrated host, the bread of the Catholic Eucharist, stays on display day and night, without pause. This tradition is so central to local identity that it appears in heraldry. The coat of arms of Lugo shows a chalice and host. Many people also link the chalice on the Galician coat of arms to this cathedral, and to the old title of Galicia as the kingdom of the sacrament.

Near the east end, look for the chapel of the Virgin of the Big Eyes, in Spanish the Virgen de los Ojos Grandes. The name comes from a medieval statue of Mary with large, wide-open eyes. The chapel around her is Baroque, the rich and theatrical style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. It was designed by Fernando de Casas Novoa. He is the same architect who created the famous Obradoiro facade of the cathedral in Santiago de Compostela. Here you can see his work on a smaller, more personal scale.

The Baroque chapel is full of movement. Gilded wood curls and twists. Carved clouds and angels seem to float. It is a deliberate contrast with the plain granite outside. Baroque artists wanted the faithful to feel overwhelmed, almost carried upward. Take a moment to compare it with the Romanesque nave you just walked through. One space is calm and heavy. The other seems to move. Both were built for the same purpose, only centuries apart.

Before you leave, find the choir stalls. These carved wooden seats were made for the clergy, who sat here to sing the daily prayers. The stalls are the work of Francisco de Moure, a Galician sculptor of the early seventeenth century. Look closely at the figures and panels. Each seat carries its own carved scene. Woodcarvers of this period were proud craftsmen, and the small details often repay a slow look more than the grand design does.

This cathedral is also a stop on the road to Santiago de Compostela. The route called the Camino Primitivo, or Original Way, passes through Lugo. Pilgrims have walked it since the Middle Ages, crossing the mountains of Asturias and Galicia. You may see walkers with backpacks and scallop shells, the traditional pilgrim symbol, resting on the steps. For many of them, Lugo is one of the last cities before Santiago. Some come in to have their pilgrim passport stamped.

Think about everything this one building holds. The twelfth-century core is Romanesque. The east end is Gothic. The chapels are Baroque, and the facade is Neoclassical. A cathedral like this is less a single design than a long conversation between generations. Each age added what it thought was most beautiful. Each age also respected enough of the past to keep it standing. That is why you can read the history of Spanish architecture in one walk from the door to the altar.

As you step back outside, turn and look at the towers once more. Then lift your eyes to the Roman wall beside them. Perhaps climb its steps and walk a short stretch, looking down on the roofs of the cathedral. Listen for the bells. Notice the moss in the joints of the granite and the people crossing the square below. The stone around you has heard prayers, pilgrims and city life for centuries. Today, for a few minutes, it has heard your footsteps too.
