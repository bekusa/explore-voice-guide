The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. You may hear water moving slowly through an irrigation channel, a donkey cart on a dirt track, and white egrets lifting off a field of clover. The ground here is soft and dark. It smells of wet earth and green crops. Around you, the land of the eastern Nile Delta is almost perfectly flat. Then you notice the low, gentle rises in the fields. Those small mounds are the reason you are here.

You are standing at Tell el-Dab'a, in Egypt's Sharqia Governorate. In Arabic and in archaeology, a tell is a mound built up by people. Each generation lived on the rubble and rubbish of the generation before, so the ground slowly rose. Under these quiet farm fields lies one of the most important cities of the ancient Mediterranean world. The Egyptians called it Hut-waret. Greek writers later called it Avaris. For a time, it was the capital of a foreign dynasty that ruled northern Egypt.

It is hard to imagine today, but water once shaped everything here. An eastern branch of the Nile, known as the Pelusiac branch, flowed past the town. That river branch has long since silted up and disappeared. In ancient times, though, it made Avaris a harbour town. Boats could travel from here toward the Mediterranean Sea. Roads ran east toward Sinai and the lands of the Levant, the coast that today includes Lebanon, Syria and Israel. This position at a crossroads made the town rich, and it also made it different.

The town grew during Egypt's Middle Kingdom, nearly four thousand years ago. Many of its residents came from the Levant. Archaeologists found houses built in Levantine styles, and burials that followed Levantine customs rather than Egyptian ones. Here is a detail that surprises many visitors. In front of some tombs, excavators found buried donkeys. They were placed there as part of the funeral, perhaps as honoured animals of trade and travel. This was not an Egyptian tradition at all. It came from the Bronze Age world to the east.

Over time, these communities gained political power. The rulers who emerged are known as the Hyksos. The name comes from an Egyptian phrase meaning rulers of foreign lands. During the period that historians call the Second Intermediate Period, Egypt was divided. The Hyksos controlled the north, roughly in the seventeenth and sixteenth centuries before Christ, and Avaris was their seat of power. Later Egyptian writers described them as invaders. The archaeology tells a more gradual story of settlement, trade and growing influence.

The Hyksos are often credited with bringing new technology into Egypt. The horse-drawn chariot is the most famous example. Scholars also link this period to the spread of the composite bow, a powerful bow made from layered materials, and new methods of working bronze. Historians still debate the details of who introduced what, and when. But the broad picture is clear. Avaris was a place where Egyptian and Near Eastern worlds met. Ideas, goods and people passed through it.

The Hyksos also adopted a local god. The town god of Avaris was Seth, an Egyptian deity of storms, deserts and foreign lands. The newcomers appear to have connected Seth with the storm god of their own homeland, often called Baal. This pairing is a good example of how cultures blended here. A foreign ruling class did not simply erase Egyptian religion. It found an Egyptian god that fitted its own beliefs, and it gave that god a place of honour.

Hyksos rule did not last. Kings from Thebes, far to the south, fought to reunite the country. The campaign was completed by the pharaoh Ahmose, who besieged and took Avaris. We know some of this from an unusual source. An officer named Ahmose, son of Ebana, recorded his military career in his tomb at Elkab in southern Egypt. He describes fighting at Avaris. The fall of the city marks the start of the New Kingdom, the age of Egypt's greatest empire.

After that, Avaris faded from memory. For centuries, nobody was sure where it had stood. Beginning in the 1960s, an Austrian archaeological team led by Manfred Bietak excavated at Tell el-Dab'a. Layer by layer, they showed that this was the lost city. Their work became one of the longest-running excavations in Egypt. It changed how historians understand the Hyksos, turning a shadowy enemy from Egyptian texts into real households, workshops and cemeteries.

Now for one of the most remarkable discoveries. In a palace area of the early New Kingdom, excavators found thousands of fragments of painted plaster. When specialists pieced them together, the paintings did not look Egyptian. They showed scenes in the style of Minoan Crete, the Bronze Age culture of the Aegean Sea. Some show acrobats leaping over bulls. Scholars still debate why these paintings were here. Perhaps there were diplomatic marriages, or travelling artists. Either way, they show that this flat corner of the Delta had links across the sea.

A darker find came from pits near a Hyksos-period palace. Archaeologists uncovered a number of human right hands, buried deliberately. Later Egyptian texts and temple scenes describe soldiers cutting a hand from fallen enemies, so that the dead could be counted and rewards could be given. The finds at Avaris are among the earliest physical evidence of this practice. It is a grim reminder that this peaceful farmland was once a place of war and of a hard-won power.

The story of this land did not end with the Hyksos. A few kilometres away, near the village of Qantir, Ramesses the Second later built his great capital, known as Piramesse. Builders reused the old region, and the worship of Seth continued. So the ground around you carries more than one capital. One foreign dynasty and one of Egypt's most powerful pharaohs both chose this corner of the Delta. Both understood the value of a site between the Nile and the roads to Asia.

As a visitor, it helps to know what to expect. There are no tall temples here and no grand columns. Most excavated areas have been studied, recorded and covered again with earth to protect them. What you see are fields, mounds, canals, palm trees and village houses. This is working farmland, and people live and work around the site. Walk respectfully, stay on paths, and do not pick up anything you find in the soil. Every small piece of pottery is part of the record.

Before you leave, look across the fields one more time. Notice how the land rises just slightly under the crops. Notice the line of a canal, perhaps following old paths of water. Somewhere beneath your feet are streets where Egyptians and Levantine traders once argued, bargained and buried their dead. The egrets will keep feeding. The farmers will keep working. And the old city will stay quietly under the green fields, waiting.

What to look for

Low mounds rising gently above the flat farm fields.
Irrigation canals cutting straight lines through green crops.
Small pottery fragments scattered in the dark soil.
Date palms marking the edges of village lanes.
White egrets feeding in flooded or freshly watered fields.
Before you go

Practical tips

Check site access with local antiquities officials before travelling.
Visit in early morning to avoid the strong midday heat.
Wear closed shoes for muddy paths along field edges.
Bring plenty of water; few facilities exist near the site.
Combine your visit with nearby Qantir, site of Piramesse.
