The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere out on the water, a ship's horn sounds, low and long. A gull answers it. The wind off Tokyo Bay carries salt and a faint smell of diesel from the working piers. Above all of it, two pale towers rise from the harbour. Fans of steel cables spread down from each one toward a long, flat deck. This is the Yokohama Bay Bridge. From a distance it looks light, almost like a drawing. Up close, it is a road for some of the heaviest traffic in Japan.

The bridge opened in 1989, near the end of Japan's long economic boom. It crosses the mouth of Yokohama's inner harbour. One end lands at Honmoku Pier, on the city side. The other end lands at Daikoku Pier, a large artificial island of warehouses, cranes and container yards. Before the bridge, freight between these docks had to go a longer way around. The bridge gave the port a direct link high above the shipping lanes, so that road traffic and ship traffic no longer had to meet.

Look at the shape of the structure. This is a cable-stayed bridge. That means the deck does not hang from two big curving cables, like on a classic suspension bridge. Instead, many straight cables run directly from each tower down to the deck. Each cable holds up one section of the road. Seen from the side, the cables form two great triangles. The main span between the towers is about 460 metres. When it opened, that made it one of the longest spans of its type in the world.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Bay Bridge is really two roads stacked one on top of the other. The upper deck carries the Shuto Expressway, the toll highway network of the Tokyo region. This section is part of the Bayshore Route, which runs along the edge of Tokyo Bay. The lower deck was built with the bridge, but for many years it stayed empty. Ordinary road traffic only began using it in 2004, as part of National Route 357.

So when you look at the deck, try to picture two layers of movement. On top, trucks and cars move fast toward Tokyo and the airports. Below them, slower local traffic moves between the piers. The lower level is enclosed by the frame of the bridge itself. From the shore you can see the deep, box-like profile of the deck. That depth is not only for strength. It is also the space that makes the second road possible.

The towers deserve a closer look. Each one is shaped like a tall, narrow letter H, with a crossbeam near the top. They rise well over one hundred and fifty metres above the water. For a port city, height has a practical meaning. Ships need to pass beneath. The deck was set high enough for the large vessels of the 1980s. But ships kept growing. That decision, made decades ago, still shapes the harbour today.

In recent years, some of the largest cruise ships in the world have become too tall to pass under the bridge. They cannot reach the older passenger terminal at Osanbashi Pier, deeper inside the harbour. Instead, the city built cruise facilities at Daikoku Pier, on the outer side of the bridge. So the bridge now works as a kind of gate. Smaller ships sail through to the city centre. The giants stop outside and look in. It is a rare case where a bridge decides where travellers arrive.

Daikoku Pier has its own story. In early 2020, the cruise ship Diamond Princess was held there during one of the first major quarantines of the COVID-19 pandemic. For weeks, news cameras around the world showed the ship sitting near the bridge. Many people outside Japan first saw the Bay Bridge in those images. Today the pier is busy again with containers, cars waiting for export, and passengers. Life at the docks moves on quickly.

The pier is also known for something very different. Under the tangle of elevated ramps sits the Daikoku Parking Area, a rest stop on the expressway. On some evenings, car enthusiasts gather there to show modified and classic vehicles. Photographs of these meetings travel far online. Police sometimes close the area when crowds grow too large. If you drive across the bridge with a local, you may hear stories about it. It is one of the more unexpected corners of Japanese car culture.

For many years, people could also walk inside the bridge. A pedestrian walkway called the Sky Walk ran along the lower level, with an observation space looking out over the bay. It closed in 2010, after visitor numbers fell. The structure for walking is still part of the bridge, and access has been offered again in some forms since then. If you are curious, check locally before you plan around it. Arrangements here have changed more than once.

As evening comes, watch the bridge change. After dark, lights pick out the towers and the line of the deck. The white steel turns gold, blue or other colours, depending on the season and on special events. The lights repeat in the black water below. From the waterfront parks, the bridge becomes part of a long chain of night lights. You can follow it from the port cranes to the Ferris wheel and towers of Minato Mirai, the newer harbour district.

The Bay Bridge also has a neighbour. Look further along the coast, toward Tokyo, and you may see another cable-stayed bridge, the Tsurumi Tsubasa Bridge. It opened a few years later on the same expressway route, with an even longer single span. Together, the two bridges show a period when Japan built boldly along its industrial shoreline. Each one connects new land made from the sea. The bay you see today is very different from the coast of a century ago.

Now look back at the bridge in front of you. Notice how the cables catch the light differently as the clouds move. Listen for the faint hum of traffic far above the water. A ship may be sliding under the deck right now, its crew small against the steel. When you walk away along the harbour, turn around once more. From each new angle the two triangles of cable shift and overlap. The bridge stays in the view for a long time. It is part of nearly every picture of the port.

What to look for

Before you go

Practical tips

Watch the bridge from Osanbashi Pier for a wide view.
Stay past sunset to see the bridge lights come on.
Take a harbour cruise to pass directly under the deck.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed waterfront.
Check locally whether walkway access inside the bridge is running.
