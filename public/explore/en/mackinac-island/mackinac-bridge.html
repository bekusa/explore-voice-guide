The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes off the water here almost all the time. It carries the cool, mineral smell of fresh lake water, and on a clear day it carries something else too: a faint, steady hum from the west. That sound is traffic, moving across a long pale line that seems to float above the Straits. If you are on Mackinac Island, you can see it from the shoreline, low on the horizon, with two tall towers rising out of the blue.

That line is the Mackinac Bridge. Before we go further, a small surprise. The name ends in the letters a-c, but locals say it like "Mackinaw." The spelling comes from French, and the sound comes from older Anishinaabe and English usage. Say "Mackinack" here and someone will gently correct you. Now you know before they do.

The bridge crosses the Straits of Mackinac, the narrow channel where Lake Michigan meets Lake Huron. On the south side is Mackinaw City, on the tip of Michigan's Lower Peninsula. On the north side is Saint Ignace, the gateway to the Upper Peninsula. For most of Michigan's history, these two halves of the state were separated by this water. They were one state on paper, but two very different worlds in daily life.

Before the bridge, crossing meant taking a ferry. In summer that was simple enough. But in busy seasons, cars could wait in lines for hours to board. In deep winter, ice closed in across the Straits, and the crossing became slow, uncertain, or impossible. People had talked about a fixed crossing for generations. Many engineers said the job could not be done here. The water was deep, the winds were fierce, and the ice was powerful.

The man who proved them wrong was the engineer David Steinman. Construction began in the mid-1950s, and the bridge opened to traffic in November 1957. Thousands of workers took part. They sank foundations into the lakebed, raised the steel towers, and spun the cables wire by wire. The work was dangerous. Five men lost their lives during construction, and they are remembered by people in the region to this day.

So why did Steinman's design succeed? Part of the answer comes from a failure elsewhere. In 1940, a suspension bridge at Tacoma Narrows in Washington State twisted in the wind and collapsed. Engineers everywhere learned from that disaster. Here, the deck was built with a deep, stiff steel truss underneath. Open spaces in the structure let the wind pass through instead of pushing against a solid wall. The bridge does not fight the wind. It lets the wind move through it.

Here is the second surprise. The bridge is designed to move. In strong winds, the middle of the deck can shift sideways by a noticeable distance. In heat and cold, the steel grows and shrinks. Drivers rarely feel this, but it is part of the plan. A rigid bridge would be under more stress. A bridge that can flex a little is a bridge that lasts.

The numbers are worth a moment. From one end to the other, the whole crossing runs about five miles, or roughly eight kilometres. The central suspension span, between the two main towers, is 3,800 feet long. When it opened, it was among the longest suspension spans in the world. The towers rise more than 550 feet above the water. From the island, they look slim and delicate. Up close, they are enormous.

Look at the colours. The towers are painted a soft ivory, and much of the rest of the structure is green. Against summer water, the effect is calm and clean. Against a grey November sky, the bridge almost disappears into the clouds. Painting a structure of this size is never really finished. Crews work on it season after season, because wind, water, and winter wear down every surface.

If you drive across, you may notice something unusual. The inner lanes are made of open steel grating. Look down, and you can see the water far below through the gaps. The tires make a different, humming sound on that surface. Some people love it. Others find it unsettling. For nervous drivers, the bridge has long offered a service where a staff member drives your car across for you. It is a quiet kindness that many travellers never hear about.

There is one day each year when people cross on foot. Every Labor Day, the American holiday at the start of September, the bridge hosts a public walk. The tradition began soon after the bridge opened. Tens of thousands of people now take part. Families, couples, and lifelong residents walk the full length from one peninsula to the other. For many Michigan families, it is a ritual passed from grandparents to grandchildren.

Locals often call the bridge "Mighty Mac." The nickname says something about how people here feel. This is not only a road. It changed the Upper Peninsula. It made it easier for families to visit each other, for goods to move, and for travellers to reach the forests, lakes, and small towns of the north. It also brought more visitors to places like this island, where cars are still not allowed and horses still carry people along the streets.

That contrast is worth noticing from where you are. Behind you, you may hear horse hooves and bicycle bells. Ahead, across the water, a steady line of cars and trucks moves across a steel span. Two ways of travelling, side by side, in one view. If you stay into the evening, watch the bridge when its lights come on. The cables trace a long, gentle curve against the darkening sky.

Take one more look before you turn away. Find the two towers. Follow the cables down toward each shore. Notice how small the passing trucks look against them. Then listen again for the wind, the water against the rocks, and that distant hum. The Straits kept these two shores apart for centuries. Now a single line of steel holds them together, and it moves gently in the same wind that is touching your face.

Practical tips

Pronounce it "Mackinaw" despite the spelling ending in -ac.
Watch from the island's western shoreline for the clearest view.
Stay past sunset to see the bridge lights switch on.
Ask about the driver assistance service if heights unsettle you.
Plan around Labor Day if you want to walk across.
