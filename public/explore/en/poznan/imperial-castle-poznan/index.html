The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the walls for a moment and press your palm against the stone. It is rough, grey, and cool, even on a warm afternoon. Above you, heavy arches and small, deep windows rise toward a square tower. Trams rattle past on the avenue behind you. The building looks old, almost medieval. But it is not. This castle is barely more than a century old, and that is the first surprise of the place.

Welcome to the Imperial Castle in Poznań. It was built in the first decade of the twentieth century, between 1905 and 1910. At that time, Poznań was not part of an independent Poland. The city belonged to the Kingdom of Prussia, inside the German Empire. Its German name was Posen. The castle was raised as a residence for the German emperor, Kaiser Wilhelm the Second. It was the last imperial castle built in Europe.

The architect was Franz Schwechten, a Berlin designer who had already worked for the emperor. He chose a neo-Romanesque style. Romanesque is the heavy, round-arched building style of European churches and castles from around the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Neo-Romanesque means a later revival of that look. Schwechten used it on purpose. Thick walls and rounded arches suggested ancient power, as if German rule here had deep roots in the distant past.

That message was the whole point. The castle was the centrepiece of a new district that planners laid out on land once occupied by old city fortifications. Around it rose grand public buildings, wide streets, and green spaces. Historians often call this area the Imperial District. Together, the buildings were meant to show the Polish population that the city was German and would stay German. The architecture carried a political argument in stone.

Now look up at the tower. If you know old photographs of the castle, it may seem a little short to you. That is the second surprise. The tower you see today is lower than the original. It was cut down during the Second World War, when the castle was rebuilt for a new and darker purpose. We will come to that story in a moment. First, consider how quickly the emperor's plan fell apart.

The Kaiser did not enjoy his castle for long. In 1918, the First World War ended, and the German Empire collapsed. That winter, the people of the region rose in the Greater Poland Uprising, and Poznań became part of a reborn Polish state. The building made for a German emperor now stood in a Polish city. In the years between the wars, it served the new state and the city in several roles, including official and academic uses.

Then came 1939. Nazi Germany invaded Poland and annexed this region. The occupiers decided to turn the castle into a residence for Adolf Hitler. Work went on during the war years, and Albert Speer, Hitler's architect, was involved in the project. Interiors were remodelled. The tower was lowered. The former chapel was converted into a study for Hitler himself. Historical sources agree that he never actually came to use it.

So the building carries two layers of imperial ambition, one on top of the other. The first came from the Kaiser, the second from the Nazi regime. Each tried to use the castle to declare ownership of this city. Neither lasted. In early 1945, the Red Army fought the German garrison for Poznań in a long and destructive battle. The castle survived, though it was damaged. Much of the surrounding city was not so lucky.

After the war, Poznań had to decide what to do with a building linked to two occupations. Tearing it down was one option. Instead, the city repaired it and slowly gave it a new life. Over the following decades, the castle became a cultural centre. Today it is known locally simply as Zamek, which is the Polish word for castle. Inside, there are cinema screens, exhibition halls, concert spaces, and rooms for workshops and theatre.

This is the part many visitors miss. A building made to impress people from above now belongs to ordinary residents. Students meet friends in the cafés. Families come for film screenings. Artists show new work in rooms once planned for emperors and dictators. If you walk inside, notice how normal the activity feels. That ordinary use is perhaps the most quietly powerful thing about the place. The castle no longer gives orders. It hosts conversations.

Before you leave, walk a short distance to the square beside the castle. There you will find two tall steel crosses, joined together. This is the monument to the Poznań protests of June 1956. In that month, workers marched through the city against the communist government, demanding bread and freedom. The protest was crushed by force, and people were killed. The monument went up in 1981, during the time of the Solidarity movement. Its location here is not an accident.

Think about the line of history you can see from this one spot. A Prussian castle built to say this land was German. A Nazi residence built to say the same thing again. And beside them, a memorial to Poles who stood up to their own communist rulers. In a few steps, you cross the whole twentieth century of this city. Few places in Europe show these layers so close together, in plain view, on a single street corner.

As you turn to go, look back once more at the grey stone and the shortened tower. Listen to the trams, the voices of students, perhaps music drifting from an open door. The walls were designed to look ancient and permanent. In the end, they were only borrowed by the powers that built them. What remains is a city that kept the building and made it its own. Carry that thought with you as you walk on into Poznań.
