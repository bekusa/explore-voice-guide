The guide

The story

Stand near the Tigris south of Baghdad and listen. Water moves slowly here, brown and heavy with silt. Date palms rattle in the wind. The ground underfoot is fine and pale, almost like flour, and it rises into the air with every step. Somewhere in this flat country, under layers of river mud, lies a city that once decided the fate of empires. Its name was Opis.

Here is the first surprise. Nobody is completely sure where Opis stood. Scholars have argued for generations about its exact location. Most place it on the Tigris, close to where the Diyala River joins it, not far from the later cities of Seleucia and Ctesiphon. But no grand gate or museum sign marks the spot. Opis is a city you meet through texts, through landscape, and through imagination.

In the old Akkadian language of Mesopotamia, the city appears under a name close to Upi. Akkadian was the language of Babylon and Assyria, written in wedge-shaped cuneiform on clay. Opis is simply the Greek form. That double name tells you something. This was a place that both Mesopotamian scribes and Greek historians cared about, a crossroads where very different worlds kept running into each other.

Why here? Look at the geography around you. The Tigris and the Euphrates come closest together in this part of Iraq. A city on this stretch of the Tigris could watch river traffic, roads from the Iranian plateau to the east, and routes toward Babylon in the south. Whoever held this ground held a door into the heart of Mesopotamia. Armies understood that, again and again.

The most famous moment came in 539 before the common era. Cyrus the Great, founder of the Persian Empire, marched against Babylon. Near Opis, his forces met the Babylonian army of King Nabonidus. A clay record called the Nabonidus Chronicle describes the clash. It reports a Persian victory and heavy losses for the Babylonians. Within weeks, Babylon itself passed into Persian hands. A great empire changed masters, and the turning point happened on this riverbank.

The Greek historian Herodotus also knew this river country. He described the Gyndes, usually identified with the Diyala, flowing into the Tigris, and the Tigris running past Opis. He tells a vivid story too. According to his tale, one of Cyrus's sacred white horses drowned while crossing the Gyndes. In anger, the king ordered his army to split the river into hundreds of small channels. Treat this as a legend, not history. But it shows how strongly these rivers lived in the ancient mind.

A century and a half later, Greek soldiers walked through this landscape. Around 400 before the common era, a mercenary army known as the Ten Thousand marched home after a failed rebellion in Persia. Their officer Xenophon wrote about the journey in a book called the Anabasis, which means the march up country. He mentions Opis as a large city, and nearby he describes a long defensive wall. Standing here, picture those tired men in bronze and wool, dust on their faces, far from the Aegean Sea.

Now comes the second surprise, and perhaps the most human story of all. In 324 before the common era, Alexander the Great came to Opis. His Macedonian veterans had followed him to India and back. When Alexander announced that older soldiers would be sent home, and showed growing favour to Persian troops, the army turned against him. The men shouted at their king. It was one of the most serious mutinies of his reign.

The Roman era historian Arrian, writing centuries later, describes what happened next. Alexander shut himself away for days and refused to see the Macedonians. He began giving command roles to Persians. In the end, the soldiers came to his tent, weeping, and begged forgiveness. Arrian records a long speech by Alexander, though historians doubt that the words are exact. Still, the outline of the event is widely accepted.

The quarrel ended with a great banquet of reconciliation. Macedonians and Persians are said to have shared the feast, with prayers for harmony between the peoples. Some modern writers have seen this as a dream of unity. Others see it as practical politics, a way to hold a huge empire together. Either way, a banquet on the Tigris near Opis became a famous scene in the story of Alexander. Less than a year later, he was dead in Babylon.

After Alexander, the centre of power shifted a short distance away. Seleucia on the Tigris rose as a major Hellenistic city, and later Ctesiphon became a capital of the Parthians and Sasanians. Opis slowly faded from the record. Rivers in Mesopotamia also change course over time, carrying silt and burying old banks. A city can disappear here not only through war, but simply through water and mud, year after year.

So what can you actually see? Look at the low mounds that sometimes rise from the plain. Archaeologists call these mounds tells. Each one can hold layers of mudbrick houses, broken pottery, and ash from ancient hearths. Notice the irrigation canals, too, cutting straight lines across the fields. Farmers here still manage water much as their distant ancestors did. The fields of green and the dusty margins mark the line between irrigated land and desert.

Pay attention to the light as well. In the morning, the river can look silver under a hazy sky. By late afternoon, the dust turns gold, and the palms throw long shadows over the water. The sounds are simple: wind, birds along the reeds, perhaps a pump engine humming beside a canal. These are not ancient sounds exactly. But the rhythm of river, field, and sky would feel familiar to anyone who lived at Opis.

Before you move on, take one last look at the Tigris. The same river carried boats past Babylonian walls, Persian camps, and Macedonian tents. Kings argued here, armies fell here, and a famous reconciliation took place on these banks. Today, there is no ruin that clearly carries the name. There is only this wide, quiet plain... and the river, still moving south, slowly, as it always has.
