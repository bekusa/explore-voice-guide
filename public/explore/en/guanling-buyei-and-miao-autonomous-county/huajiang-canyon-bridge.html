The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. Up here it moves differently — a steady push that comes up out of the gorge and presses against your jacket. Below the railing, the air seems to drop away forever. Far beneath you, a thin green thread of river curls between grey cliffs. Somewhere down there, the water is loud and fast. From this height, you hear almost nothing at all. Only the wind, the hum of traffic, and your own breathing.

You are standing on the Huajiang Canyon Bridge, in the mountains of Guizhou Province in southwest China. The bridge crosses the Huajiang Grand Canyon, a deep gash cut by the Beipan River. On one side lies Guanling, a county shared by the Buyei and Miao peoples. On the other side lies Zhenfeng. For centuries, this canyon separated communities that could see each other, but could not easily reach each other.

Here is the number that brings most visitors to this spot. The road deck sits roughly 625 metres above the river. That makes it the highest bridge in the world when it opened in 2025. To picture it, imagine stacking more than one and a half Empire State Buildings beneath your feet. Or simply look down — and let your stomach tell you the rest.

This is a suspension bridge. Two enormous towers rise on either rim of the canyon. Between them, steel cables sweep down in long curves and hold the deck in the air. The main span between the towers stretches about 1,420 metres. That is well over a kilometre of road with no support beneath it, only the cables above. Watch the cables closely as you walk. They carry the entire weight in a single, clean line.

Now think about what it took to cross this gorge before. A traveller had to wind down one cliff on narrow switchback roads, cross the river at the bottom, and climb back up the other side. Reports put that journey at around two hours. Today a car crosses in roughly two minutes. For farmers, traders, and families on both rims, that change is not a statistic. It is a new shape for everyday life.

Here is the first surprise. This bridge is not alone. Guizhou is often called a bridge province, and engineers sometimes joke that it is a museum of bridges. Many of the world's highest bridges stand within this one province. Before Huajiang opened, the record belonged to the Duge Bridge, also in Guizhou. The landscape explains why. Mountains cover most of the province, and rivers have cut deep valleys through them.

That landscape has a name — karst. Karst is limestone country, where rain and rivers slowly dissolve the rock over millions of years. The result is steep peaks, sudden cliffs, caves, and sinkholes. Look at the canyon walls around you. The pale grey stone, the vertical faces, the green shrubs clinging to ledges — all of this is karst. It is beautiful to look at. For road builders, it is a very hard place to work.

Building at this height brought problems that most bridges never face. Wind in a deep canyon can be strong and unpredictable. Fog rises from the river and hides the far rim for hours. Workers lifted huge sections of steel deck into position high above the water. Engineers relied on careful testing, modern monitoring, and precise lifting systems. The finished structure is designed to stay calm and stable in the gusts you feel on your face right now.

Here is the second surprise. This bridge was built not only for traffic, but also for visitors. Planners designed the area around it as a place to stop, look, and explore. Viewing areas near the towers and the canyon rim let people take in the scale of the span. Local reports have also described adventure attractions linked to the bridge. Check on site what is open today, because the offerings continue to develop.

Take a moment to look beyond the steel. The hills on both sides belong to Buyei and Miao communities. The Buyei are known for indigo-dyed cloth and for songs sung back and forth between groups. The Miao are known for elaborate silver ornaments and fine embroidery. You may see these traditions in nearby villages, markets, and festival clothing. The bridge links these mountain towns more closely to the wider region.

If the weather is clear, the light here changes the whole scene. In the morning, the sun lights one canyon wall while the other stays in shadow. Later in the day, the cables catch the light and shine against the darker rock. On misty days, the deck can seem to float above a sea of cloud. Neither condition is better. Each one shows a different side of this place.

Listen again for a moment. The sound of the river does not reach this height. Instead, you might hear birds circling below the deck, or the soft ring of the wind across the cables. Many visitors fall quiet when they first look over the edge. This kind of height does that. It makes people slow down, and it makes the world below look very small and very far away.

Before you leave, find the thin line of the river one more time. That water cut this canyon slowly, grain by grain, over a very long time. The bridge above it went up in only a few years. Both are here together now — the patient river and the fast work of people. As you walk away, turn back once, and watch the span shrink into the mountains.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a windproof layer; gusts rise strongly from the gorge.
Check the weather; fog can hide the canyon for hours.
Visit early for side light across the canyon walls.
Secure phones and hats tightly before leaning near railings.
Ask on site which viewing areas and attractions are open.
