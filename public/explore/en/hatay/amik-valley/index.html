The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of these fields and listen. The wind moves across land so flat it seems pressed down by a giant hand. Cotton, wheat and vegetables run to the horizon in long, straight rows. Mountains rise on the far edges, blue in the haze. The soil under your boots is dark and heavy. Pick some up and it crumbles, rich and slightly damp. This is the Amik Valley, and almost everything about it, from its shape to its history, begins with water.

The valley sits in Hatay, Turkey's southernmost province, close to the Syrian border and not far from the old city of Antakya, the ancient Antioch. Three rivers matter here. The Orontes, known locally as the Asi, flows through the region. The Afrin and the Karasu come down from the north and east. For thousands of years, these waters met in the low heart of the plain and spread out into a wide, shallow lake.

Here is the first thing most visitors never realise. A large part of the ground you are looking at was once underwater. Lake Amik, sometimes called the Lake of Antioch, filled the centre of this basin, with marshes around its edges. Reeds grew thick along the shore. Fishermen worked its waters, and water buffalo grazed in the wet margins. Then, during the twentieth century, engineers drained it. Canals and drainage works pulled the water away, and the lakebed slowly became farmland.

The reasons were practical. Marshes bred mosquitoes, and malaria was a serious problem across the region. Farmers wanted more land, and the lakebed soil was extremely fertile. The result is the agricultural landscape around you now. But the change came at a cost. The lake had been an important resting place for migrating birds, and wetland life across the plain shrank. Some older residents still remember the water, the reed boats, and the sound of birds at dawn.

Now look more carefully at the horizon. Scattered across the plain are low, rounded hills that do not look quite natural. They are not natural. Archaeologists call them tells. A tell is a mound built up over centuries as people lived in the same place, built mud-brick houses, let them collapse, and built again on top. Layer by layer, entire towns rose above the plain. The Amik Valley holds a remarkable number of these mounds.

In the 1930s, a team from the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago surveyed this plain in detail. The archaeologist Robert Braidwood led much of that work. They recorded many mounds and dug into several of them. From the pottery and objects they found, they built a long sequence of phases stretching from early farming villages into later periods. This became known as the Amuq sequence. For decades, scholars across the Near East used it as a reference point for dating their own finds.

That is the second surprise. This quiet farming plain, which few tourists cross on purpose, helped shape how archaeologists understand the early history of the whole region. When researchers in other countries compared their pottery, they often compared it to what was found here. The fields of the Amik became, in a sense, a measuring stick for the ancient world.

One of the most important mounds is Tell Atchana, the site of ancient Alalakh. In the Bronze Age, Alalakh was a royal city, a centre of trade and power between larger empires. The British archaeologist Leonard Woolley excavated here in the years around the Second World War. His team uncovered palaces, temples and clay tablets written in cuneiform, the wedge-shaped script of ancient Mesopotamia. One famous find is a carved statue of a king named Idrimi, covered in an inscription telling his life story. That statue is now in the British Museum in London.

Not far away lies Tell Tayinat, another major mound. In the Iron Age, it was the capital of a small kingdom in this plain. Excavations have uncovered a temple and palace buildings. In 2009, archaeologists found a clay tablet there recording a loyalty oath imposed by the Assyrian king Esarhaddon, a powerful ruler from northern Mesopotamia. The tablet shows how this valley sat inside the struggles of great empires. Kings far away cared very much who controlled these fields.

Why did so many powers want this place? Look at the geography around you. The plain sits at a crossroads. Routes ran from Anatolia in the north down to Syria and the Levant, and from the inland regions toward the Mediterranean coast. The soil fed large populations. Water was plentiful. For anyone moving armies or trade goods through this corner of the world, the Amik was hard to avoid. Many different peoples passed through, settled, and left their layers in the mounds.

The valley also carries recent memory. In February 2023, powerful earthquakes struck southern Turkey and northern Syria. Hatay was among the hardest-hit provinces, and Antakya suffered enormous damage. Towns and villages across this plain were affected too. As you travel here, you may see rebuilding, temporary housing, or empty spaces where buildings once stood. It is worth moving through these communities with patience and respect. People are still putting their lives back together.

As you look around today, notice how the ancient and the ordinary sit side by side. A tractor works a field at the foot of a mound that holds thousands of years of human life. Irrigation canals cut straight lines where a lake once spread. Storks or herons may still stand in a wet ditch, a small reminder of the old wetlands. Village roads follow the edges of fields that farmers have worked for generations.

Before you leave, take one more slow look across the plain. Find a low mound on the horizon and picture the town buried inside it. Then imagine the lake, flat and silver, covering the fields in front of you. Listen to the wind in the crops, and feel the weight of that dark soil underfoot. Every civilization that passed through here depended on the same ground. As you walk back toward the road, you are crossing it too.

What to look for

Low rounded mounds rising unnaturally from the flat fields.
Straight drainage canals crossing the former lakebed.
Dark, heavy soil where the ancient lake once lay.
Mountain ridges framing the plain on its far edges.
Herons or storks standing in wet ditches beside fields.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Hatay Archaeology Museum in Antakya to see valley finds.
Bring binoculars for herons and storks along the canals.
Come in early morning when haze over the plain is lightest.
Check access locally before visiting excavation sites like Tell Atchana.
Travel respectfully through villages still recovering from the 2023 earthquakes.
