The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Above you, iron and glass hold back the London sky. Below, your footsteps ring on hard floors. Somewhere close, a child is ringing a bell on an old bus. The air smells faintly of polish and warm metal. Light falls through the roof in long pale bands. This is the London Transport Museum, in the heart of Covent Garden. And this hall was not built for buses at all. It was built for flowers.

This building began life in the Victorian era as part of the Covent Garden market. For generations, porters carried crates of cut flowers through here before dawn. The market was the noisiest, busiest food and flower exchange in the city. In 1974, the traders moved south of the river to Nine Elms, where there was more space for lorries. The old halls fell quiet. Then, in 1980, the transport collection moved in. The iron columns that once framed flower stalls now frame trams and trains.

Look up at the roof structure before you look at anything else. Slender cast iron supports a wide span of glass. Victorian engineers loved this combination. It gave them light without heavy walls, and it could be made in a foundry and assembled on site. The same thinking shaped the great railway stations of the age. So, in a quiet way, the building itself belongs in the collection. It comes from the same century and the same skills as the machines it holds.

The story inside starts with the horse. Before engines, London moved on hooves. Horse-drawn omnibuses crowded the streets in the nineteenth century, and the noise and dirt were constant. Here you can see how these vehicles worked. Passengers sat inside or climbed to the open top deck, exposed to rain and soot. Picture the streets outside filled with them — wooden wheels on stone, the shouts of drivers, and the smell of thousands of horses.

Then came the idea that changed everything. In 1863, the Metropolitan Railway opened between Paddington and Farringdon. It was the first underground railway in the world. And it ran on steam. Think about that for a second. Coal-fired locomotives, pulling carriages through tunnels under the city. The tunnels filled with smoke. Passengers came out with smudged faces. Yet people rode it in huge numbers, because it was faster than any street.

One of the treasures here is a real steam locomotive from the Metropolitan Railway. It is a short, sturdy engine, painted and polished, and it looks almost friendly. It is easy to walk past it as just another big machine. But stand beside it and try to imagine it under the ground, in the dark, hissing smoke into a brick tunnel. Engineers designed ways to reduce the smoke, but they never solved the problem completely. Electricity did that later.

Electric trains arrived underground at the end of the nineteenth century. The deep tube lines followed, bored far below the streets through London clay. On the surface, electric trams and later trolleybuses spread through the suburbs. The collection traces this shift from muscle to steam to electricity. Look closely at the seats, the straps, the wooden panels. Each change in design tells you something about who was travelling, and how many of them there were.

Here is something many visitors miss. The look of London transport was designed as carefully as the machines. In the early twentieth century, the Underground hired the calligrapher Edward Johnston to create a clear lettering style. His typeface, with its calm rounded shapes, is still used across the network today. A manager named Frank Pick pushed for good design everywhere — in stations, signs, and posters. He believed public transport should be beautiful as well as useful.

That belief produced the famous Tube map. In the early 1930s, an engineering draughtsman called Harry Beck drew a new kind of map. He ignored real distances. Instead, he used straight lines, set angles, and evenly spaced stations, much like an electrical circuit diagram. At first, officials were unsure. Passengers loved it. Nearly every metro map in the world now follows his idea. Look for how simple it is — and how much it leaves out on purpose.

The museum also holds a remarkable poster collection. For decades, London transport commissioned artists to make posters that encouraged people to travel. Some showed the countryside at the end of the line. Others showed parks, theatres, or a quiet weekend away. Many are bold and modern in style. They were meant to sell journeys, but today they read as a record of how the city saw itself. You will notice the shop sells many of these designs as prints.

This is a place to touch and climb, not only to look. Several vehicles let you step on board. Sit on a bench seat. Hold a pole. Look out of a window at the hall instead of a street. Children often head straight for the driving cabs and the bells. Adults, interestingly, tend to go quiet on the older carriages. Something about the narrow space makes the past feel close. People sat exactly where you are sitting.

The story does not stop with the past. Later galleries look at how a city of millions keeps moving — the crowds, the planning, and the challenges ahead. And not everything is here. Much of the wider collection is kept at a separate depot in Acton, in west London. It opens to visitors only at certain times. So what you see in this hall is a careful selection from a much larger archive of vehicles, signs, and objects.

When you step back out into the piazza, take a breath. Covent Garden is still full of movement — performers, shoppers, and people hurrying to the Underground station a short walk away. Look at the station sign if you pass it. You might now notice the lettering, the red and blue circle, the colours of the lines. The museum ends here, but the collection keeps running. All around you, the city is still in motion.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early on weekdays to avoid school-holiday family crowds.
Climb aboard the open vehicles for the best sense of scale.
Check the website for occasional open days at the Acton depot.
Save time for the poster prints in the museum shop.
Walk to nearby Covent Garden station to see the design live.
