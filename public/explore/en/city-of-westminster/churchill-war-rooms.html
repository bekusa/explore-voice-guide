The guide

The story

Leave the daylight of King Charles Street behind, and the air changes. It is cooler down here, and still. The ceilings are low, and painted steel girders run overhead. The walls are cream and green, a colour scheme meant to keep tired people calm. Somewhere a ventilation fan hums. You are standing beneath the government offices of Whitehall, in the rooms where Britain ran its war against Nazi Germany.

This is the Churchill War Rooms, today part of Imperial War Museums. In the late 1930s, officials feared that bombing would destroy central London within days of a new war. They needed a protected place where ministers and military chiefs could keep working. So they converted the basement of a large government building near Saint James's Park. The rooms became operational in late August 1939, only days before Britain declared war.

The first thing to understand is that this was never a deep bunker. It sits just below street level. To strengthen it, engineers later poured a thick layer of concrete above parts of the complex, known simply as the Slab. It gave real protection against blast and smaller bombs. But historians generally agree it would not have survived a direct hit from a very heavy bomb. The people who worked here carried that knowledge with them every day.

Walk on, and you reach the Cabinet War Room. A square of tables faces one wooden armchair at the end. That was Winston Churchill's seat. During the Blitz, the long German bombing campaign against British cities in 1940 and 1941, the War Cabinet met here when the raids made the rooms above too dangerous. Look at the red fire buckets, the ashtrays, the notepads. Everything is set out as if the ministers have just stepped out.

Here is the first surprise. When the war in the Pacific ended in August 1945, the staff simply switched off the lights and left. Many rooms were locked and kept almost exactly as they were. Maps stayed on the walls. Pins stayed in the maps. Much of what you see in the Map Room is not a careful reconstruction. It is the room that people walked away from, preserved for decades in the dark.

The Map Room was the heart of the complex. Officers from the army, navy and air force worked here in shifts, day and night. Their job was to collect the latest information and turn it into a clear picture for Churchill, the King and the chiefs of staff. Coloured telephones line one desk, each one a direct line to a different service. The big wall maps show convoy routes across the Atlantic, marked with thousands of tiny pin holes.

Those pin holes matter. Each one marks a position that someone updated by hand, often at three in the morning, often with bad news. Look closely and you can see how the paper has worn thin in busy shipping lanes. A small personal detail survives here too. According to the museum, a lump of sugar was found hidden in a desk drawer, kept back by an officer from his strict wartime ration. It is a quiet reminder that these were ordinary people under pressure.

Further along the corridor is one of the most curious doors in London. It looks like a private lavatory, with a lock that shows vacant or engaged. Staff assumed it was Churchill's personal toilet, a rare luxury underground. In fact, it hid the Transatlantic Telephone Room. Behind this door, Churchill could speak on a secure line to President Roosevelt in Washington. The scrambling equipment was so large that it was housed elsewhere in London, in the basement of the Selfridges department store.

Now the bedroom. Churchill had a small room here, with a single bed, a desk and a map of the British coastline. Visitors often imagine him sleeping here night after night. The truth is different. He used the room for work and naps, and he did make broadcasts to the nation from the complex. But he seems to have slept here only on a handful of nights. He much preferred the flat above ground, and he was known to climb onto the roof to watch the raids himself.

Other rooms tell you about the people who stayed. Typists, clerks, officers and guards worked long hours without sunlight. Some slept in cramped dormitories even further down, in a low sub-basement. In the corridor, look for a board that once told staff the weather outside. Staff said that during heavy raids someone would set it to a joking word such as windy. It was a small piece of humour from people who could not see the sky for days at a time.

After the war, the rooms stayed closed to the public for many years. Only occasional official visitors came down. Imperial War Museums took over the site and opened it to the public in 1984. In 2005, a large museum about Churchill's whole life was added in the middle of the complex. It covers his childhood, his long political career, his failures and his successes. It also includes a long interactive table that you can explore by touch.

That museum does not only celebrate him. It also presents the debates. Churchill made decisions that many people still criticise, in Britain and around the world, especially across the former British Empire. A good way to visit is to hold two ideas at once. These rooms show real courage and hard work under danger. They also show how much power a few people in one basement held over millions of lives far away.

Before you leave, stop for a moment in one of the quieter corridors. Notice how close the ceiling feels, and how little sound reaches you from the street above. Then climb back up into the light of Westminster. Turn towards Saint James's Park, with its trees and pelicans and open water. It is only a few steps away. For six years, the people below could not see it at all.
