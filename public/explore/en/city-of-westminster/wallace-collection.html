The guide

The story

Push open the door from Manchester Square and the city noise drops away almost at once. Soft carpet takes the place of pavement. The air smells faintly of polish and old wood. Gilded frames catch the light from tall windows, and somewhere ahead a clock ticks under a glass dome. You are standing inside a private London townhouse. It still feels like one, and that is the first thing to notice about the Wallace Collection.

The building is Hertford House. It was built in the late eighteenth century, originally for the Duke of Manchester. That is why the quiet square outside carries his name. Later the house passed to the Seymour-Conway family, the Marquesses of Hertford. Over several generations they gathered one of the most remarkable private art collections in Europe. Today it hangs in the rooms where the family once lived and entertained.

The central figure in this story is the fourth Marquess of Hertford. He was a quiet, private man who spent most of his life in Paris, not London. He bought paintings, furniture, porcelain and armour on a grand scale. He had a particular love for French eighteenth-century art, which was out of fashion at the time. Many of the treasures around you crossed the Channel because of his very personal taste.

When he died in 1870, his fortune and much of his collection passed to Richard Wallace. Wallace had worked for him for many years as his secretary. He is widely believed to have been the Marquess's son, born outside marriage. Wallace was made a baronet and brought much of the art to London. He also changed this house to display it. So the name on the door belongs to the heir, not to the man who bought most of the art.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. Richard Wallace stayed in Paris during the siege of the city in 1870 and 1871. He spent large sums helping its hungry and wounded people. Afterwards he paid for a series of cast-iron public drinking fountains across Paris. Parisians still call them Wallace fountains, and many still give water today. So this quiet London museum has a cousin on many street corners in France.

After Sir Richard died, his widow, Lady Wallace, left the collection to the British nation. The bequest came in 1897, and the museum opened to the public in 1900. Her wishes were clear. The collection was to stay together, as a whole, in central London. For that reason, the Wallace Collection feels unlike a typical gallery. The rooms are hung densely, almost as a wealthy family would have arranged them.

Now to the paintings. The most famous face in the house belongs to The Laughing Cavalier by Frans Hals, the Dutch portrait painter. Look closely and you will find a surprise. He is not really laughing. He wears a confident half-smile, with his moustache curled upward. Nobody knows for certain who he was, or whether he was a cavalier at all. The title was added long after Hals painted him, but it stuck.

The story goes that the fourth Marquess bought this portrait at a Paris auction in the 1860s. He reportedly outbid a member of the Rothschild family, and the high price made headlines. Stand in front of the canvas and study the sleeve. It is embroidered with bees, flames, arrows and lovers' knots. Hals painted these tiny symbols with quick, loose brushstrokes. From a distance, though, every thread looks sharp.

For many visitors, the real heart of the house is French. Find The Swing by Jean-Honoré Fragonard. A young woman in a pink silk dress flies upward on a swing in a leafy garden. One small shoe has slipped off and sails through the air. Below her, a young man hides in the bushes and gazes up. It is playful, a little scandalous, and painted with a feathery lightness. Many people recognise it from posters without knowing where it lives.

The collection goes far beyond paintings. It holds one of the finest groups of Sèvres porcelain anywhere. Sèvres was the French royal porcelain factory, famous for deep blues, soft pinks and heavy gilding. You will also see furniture made for the French court, with brass and tortoiseshell laid into wood in elaborate patterns. Some pieces are linked to the circle of the last French royal family before the Revolution.

Then comes the second big surprise. Downstairs and in several galleries, there is a large collection of arms and armour. You will find European suits of armour, swords, helmets and shields. There are also weapons from the Middle East and South Asia, decorated with gold and precious stones. It sits strangely beside the delicate porcelain upstairs. Yet it reflects the same collector's appetite for beautiful, finely made objects of every kind.

In the middle of the house, the old open courtyard now has a glass roof. Light pours down onto a café and restaurant. Its sculpture and pale stone walls create a calm break between galleries. This is a good place to sit and let your eyes rest. A collection this dense can feel like a rich meal. A pause here helps you return to the rooms with fresh attention.

As you move on, take a moment to look beyond the famous names. Notice the clocks, the snuffboxes and the miniature portraits in their cases. Look at the curtains, the silk on the walls and the way daylight falls across the gilded frames. These rooms were made for living, not only for display. Then step back out into Manchester Square, where the plane trees move overhead and Marylebone carries on with its quiet afternoon.

Look closer

What to look for

Embroidered bees and flames on the Laughing Cavalier's sleeve.
The flying pink shoe in Fragonard's The Swing.
Deep blue Sèvres porcelain trimmed with heavy gold.
Brass and tortoiseshell patterns inlaid into French furniture.
Jewelled hilts on swords in the oriental armour displays.
Before you go

Practical tips

Start with the Laughing Cavalier before the galleries fill up.
Rest in the glass-roofed courtyard café between gallery floors.
Visit the armour galleries if travelling with restless children.
Allow at least two unhurried hours for the main floors.
Combine your visit with a walk along nearby Marylebone High Street.
