The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go inside. Buses hiss to a stop along Baker Street. Footsteps drum the pavement. Above it all, the brick front of a narrow Georgian townhouse stays still and quiet. Black railings guard the steps. A small round plaque by the door carries a number that millions of people know by heart... 221B. You are standing at the most famous address in detective fiction.

Here is the first surprise. Sherlock Holmes never lived here, because Sherlock Holmes never lived anywhere. He was invented by a Scottish doctor named Arthur Conan Doyle. Holmes first appeared in print in 1887, in a story called A Study in Scarlet. In that story, Doctor John Watson needs cheap lodgings. A friend introduces him to Holmes, and the two men agree to share rooms at 221B Baker Street.

The second surprise is about the number itself. When Conan Doyle was writing, Baker Street did not run high enough to reach 221. The address was fiction, chosen for a street that existed. The street was later extended and renumbered, and the address became real. But this house does not sit at the true postal position of 221. The plaque by the door is a tribute, not a strict street number.

That did not stop the letters. For decades, people from around the world wrote to Mister Holmes at Baker Street. They asked him to find lost relatives and stolen property. Some asked for his autograph. Others simply wanted to know if he was real. For many years, the building that held the official 221 address belonged to the Abbey National building society. Staff there answered the post on the detective's behalf.

The museum opened in 1990. Its owners took a Victorian townhouse and arranged it as the stories describe. The house is not a gallery of glass cases. It is a lodging house, laid out room by room, as if the tenants have just stepped out. You may see staff in Victorian costume at the door. They help to set the scene before you even cross the threshold.

As you climb toward the first floor, count the stairs. In one of the stories, Holmes tests Watson. He asks how many steps lead up to their rooms. Watson has climbed them hundreds of times, but he has no idea. Holmes knows the answer... seventeen. His point is simple. Watson sees, but he does not observe. Visitors here often count under their breath on the way up.

The first floor holds the heart of the house — the sitting room and study that Holmes and Watson shared. Look at how full it is. Books and papers crowd every surface. There is a fireplace, armchairs pulled close to the hearth, and the clutter of a working mind. The window looks down onto Baker Street. In the stories, Holmes often watches the street from here and reads the lives of strangers below.

Take your time with the small objects. The stories mention many personal details. Holmes plays the violin. He smokes a pipe and fills the room with tobacco smoke. He runs chemical experiments that make the air sharp and unpleasant. He keeps a magnifying lens close at hand. The rooms gather objects like these, and much of the pleasure is in matching them to scenes you half remember from the books or the films.

The next floors continue the story. One room belongs to Doctor Watson, the steady narrator who tells almost every tale. There are also hints of Mrs Hudson, the landlady who puts up with gunshots, strange visitors and late-night experiments. Climb higher and you meet wax figures of characters from the stories. Among them is Professor Moriarty, the criminal genius whom Holmes calls the Napoleon of crime.

The house itself deserves attention. The rooms are small and the staircases are steep and narrow. This is a real domestic building, not a purpose-built museum. In the nineteenth century, many London houses like this one were divided into rented rooms for single gentlemen, students and professionals. Holmes and Watson are exactly that kind of tenant. So, as you squeeze past other visitors on the stairs, you feel the scale of their world.

Why does this fictional man still pull crowds to a real street? Part of the answer is the method. Holmes solves crimes by noticing small things — mud on a boot, a worn sleeve, a scratch on a watch. He looks closely and he reasons step by step. Readers loved that idea, and real investigators admired it too. The stories helped shape how the public imagined detective work for generations.

When you leave, walk a few minutes to Baker Street Underground station. It belongs to the earliest underground railway in the world, which opened in the 1860s. Look closely at the walls of some platforms. The tiles carry small silhouettes of a man in profile, with a pipe and a hunting cap. Outside the station stands a bronze statue of the detective, wrapped in his long coat, keeping watch over his old neighbourhood.

Before you go, stand back across the road and look at the house once more. The black door. The railings. The windows on the first floor, where a fictional man once watched a real street. Then look at the people around you — a delivery rider, a tourist with a map, someone in a hurry. Try the Holmes test. What can you tell about them? Notice one small thing... and you have learned the lesson of 221B.
