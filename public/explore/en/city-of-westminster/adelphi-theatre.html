The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the Strand and listen. Buses grind past. Taxis brake and pull away. Somewhere behind you a street performer is tuning a guitar. In front of you, a pale, flat facade rises above the pavement. It has clean vertical lines and lettering in a style that feels made for the age of jazz. This is the Adelphi Theatre. People have come to this spot to be entertained for more than two centuries.

The story begins with a most unlikely fortune. In the early nineteenth century, a merchant named John Scott made his money selling a laundry product. It was a blue dye that made white linen look whiter. Scott had a daughter, Jane, who wanted to perform. So he did what a wealthy and devoted father might do. He built her a theatre. It opened in 1806 under the name Sans Pareil, a French phrase that means 'without equal'.

Jane Scott was not just a figure on the stage. She wrote many of the pieces herself. These included melodramas, burlettas and spectacles, which are light musical plays with songs. At that time, London's largest theatres held special licences for serious spoken drama. Smaller houses like this one had to work around those rules with music, dance and spectacle. That limit shaped the theatre's character. From the start, it was a place for popular entertainment.

In 1819, after the Scotts had sold the theatre, it took a new name, the Adelphi. The word comes from Greek and means 'brothers'. It is a nod to the Adam brothers. These Scottish architects had built a grand riverside development called the Adelphi just south of here in the eighteenth century. Most of their terrace is gone today. Yet the name lives on in this street and in the theatre in front of you.

Through the nineteenth century, the Adelphi became known for one kind of drama in particular. London audiences spoke of 'Adelphi melodrama'. This meant plays with clear villains, wronged heroines and dramatic rescues. Stage versions of novels by Charles Dickens also appeared here. They were adapted for an audience that knew his characters well. The Victorian crowd that filled these seats wanted to feel strong emotion. Here, they got it.

Now for the first surprise. The building you see is not the original. The theatre has been rebuilt and remodelled several times over its long life. The current version dates from 1930. It was designed in the Art Deco style, which was the fashionable look between the two world wars. Look at the strong straight lines and the geometric detail. They were meant to feel modern, fast and new. That is a striking contrast with the Georgian and Victorian buildings that once stood on this site.

The second surprise waits around the back. Walk to the rear of the theatre and you reach Maiden Lane, a narrow street that runs parallel to the Strand. This is where the stage door is. In 1897, one of the most popular actors of the day, William Terriss, arrived here for an evening performance. At the stage door, he was stabbed by a man named Richard Archer Prince. Prince was a struggling actor with a grudge.

Terriss died of his wounds. London was shocked. The actor had been a favourite with audiences, and his death made headlines across the country. Prince was found to be insane and was sent to an asylum, not to the gallows. The event left a mark on theatre history that has never quite faded. Many people who walk down Maiden Lane today have no idea what happened at this quiet doorway.

And here, history gives way to folklore. Legend has it that Terriss never really left. Over the years, some performers and staff have told stories of strange lights, footsteps and an unexplained presence in the theatre. Some accounts place a ghost at the nearby Covent Garden Underground station too. None of this can be proven, of course. But it is part of the Adelphi's identity, and theatre people love a good ghost story.

In the twentieth century, the Adelphi became a home for big musicals. In the 1980s, a revival of the musical Me and My Girl enjoyed a long run here. It sent audiences into the street doing a cheerful dance called the Lambeth Walk. Later, Andrew Lloyd Webber's Sunset Boulevard opened on this stage. Then a revival of Chicago stayed for years. The theatre's ownership is linked to the Lloyd Webber organisation, which helps explain its strong ties to major musicals.

Think for a moment about how the theatre sits in its neighbourhood. The Strand has long been one of London's main roads. It links the City of London with Westminster, the old centre of royal power and government. Theatres, hotels and restaurants grew up along it because people were always passing through. Just behind you, Covent Garden spreads north with its market halls and narrow lanes. To the south, the land drops gently towards the River Thames. You are standing right in the middle of London's entertainment district.

If you go inside for a show, notice how the Art Deco style continues past the front doors. Look for the curving lines in the auditorium, the stylised decoration and the sense of order and calm. The design aims to make you feel part of something modern and glamorous. Even if you are just passing, the front entrance at night is worth a pause. The lit signs and the crowd waiting outside capture the feeling of London's West End.

As you leave, take a final look at the facade. Picture Jane Scott singing on a smaller stage, two centuries ago, in a theatre her father built with laundry money. Picture the Victorian crowds, the melodrama, the shock of that night in Maiden Lane. Then come back to the present... the buses, the voices, the lights. If you walk down Maiden Lane, slow down at the stage door. Few people do.
