The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the traffic noise from Piccadilly, there is a softer sound here. It is the wind moving through the high canopy of plane trees, and the crunch of gravel under your feet. Look down at the grass. It rolls gently away in every direction, and something is missing. There are no flower beds. No tulips in neat rows, no rose gardens, no bright borders. Just trees, lawns and the light coming through the leaves. Welcome to Green Park, the quietest of central London's royal parks.

That simplicity is the first surprise. Its larger neighbours, Hyde Park to the west and Saint James's Park to the east, have lakes, statues and formal planting. Green Park has none of that. It is mostly open meadow under mature trees. In spring, the grass fills with daffodils, which have been planted in great drifts. For the rest of the year, the park stays green and grey and gold. The name is not poetic decoration. It is a plain description of what you see.

Why no flowers? Londoners love a story, and one old tale has a royal scandal behind it. Legend has it that King Charles the Second picked flowers here for a woman who was not his wife. His queen, Catherine of Braganza, found out. In her anger, she ordered every flower in the park pulled up, and none were ever planted again. It is a good story. Historians treat it as folklore, not record. But visitors still repeat it, and it fits the place a little too well.

What we do know is that Charles the Second shaped this land. In the seventeenth century, after the monarchy returned to England, he enclosed the area and made it a royal park. He liked to walk here, and it linked his palaces and hunting grounds across the west of the city. Before that, this was open meadow on the edge of London. There is another local tale, again unproven, that the ground was once a burial place for lepers from a nearby hospital. Some people say this is the real reason flowers never grew well here.

Now look along the southern edge of the park. That long, straight road is Constitution Hill. It runs from Hyde Park Corner towards Buckingham Palace. The name probably comes from the king's habit of taking his constitutional, his daily walk, along this route. Kings and queens have ridden and driven along this road for centuries. In the nineteenth century, several attempts on Queen Victoria's life took place near here, as her carriage passed. Today, on many mornings, you may see soldiers on horseback moving along it towards the palace.

In the eighteenth century, Green Park had a darker reputation. Its open ground and its distance from busy streets made it a known place for duels. Gentlemen came here at dawn to settle arguments of honour with swords or pistols. The park was also known for highwaymen and thieves after dark. It is hard to picture that now, with office workers eating lunch on the grass. But the quiet that you feel today is the same quiet that once made the park useful for private, dangerous business.

Here is a second surprise. This peaceful meadow once hosted one of the loudest events in London's history. In 1749, a huge fireworks display was held here to celebrate the end of a European war. The composer George Frideric Handel wrote music for the occasion, now known as the Music for the Royal Fireworks. Crowds poured in to watch. The evening did not go as planned. A large wooden structure built for the show caught fire. The music survived far better than the building did.

Turn towards the eastern side of the park. The path running along that edge is called Queen's Walk. It was laid out in the eighteenth century for a royal queen who enjoyed strolling here. Behind it stand some of the grandest private houses in London. Their gardens and back terraces face the park. Spencer House, built for the Spencer family, is one of them. Further south, you can see the pale stone of Lancaster House. Walk slowly along this path and look up at the windows. You are seeing the side of London that most passers-by never notice.

Now head towards the corner near Buckingham Palace. There you will find Canada Gate, a set of tall ornamental gates with gilded details. They were given by Canada in the early twentieth century, as part of the memorial to Queen Victoria that stands in front of the palace. Nearby is the Canada Memorial, made of granite and set at an angle in the ground. Water flows over its surface. Look closely and you will see bronze maple leaves set into the stone. It honours Canadians who served in the two world wars.

At the opposite corner, near Hyde Park Corner, stands a very different monument. This is the Bomber Command Memorial. It honours the aircrews of the Royal Air Force who flew bombing missions in the Second World War. Many thousands of them never came home. The memorial was unveiled in 2012 by Queen Elizabeth the Second. Inside a classical stone pavilion stand bronze figures of an aircrew, just back from a mission. Their faces look tired and watchful. It is one of the most moving places in this part of London.

One more thing lies under your feet, and almost nobody thinks about it. A lost river called the Tyburn once flowed across this land on its way to the Thames. Today, it runs hidden in culverts and pipes underground. Some people say the gentle dips and hollows in the lawns follow its old route. As you cross the park, notice how the ground rises and falls. London is full of buried rivers like this one, flowing in the dark beneath the streets.

If you want to slow down, look for the striped deckchairs. They are green and white, and in the warmer months they appear across the lawns. You can rent one for a while and sit beneath the trees. This is one of the oldest pleasures of London park life. Office workers, tourists and families all share the same grass. On summer evenings, the light turns soft and low through the plane trees. The sound of traffic fades behind you, and the park feels much larger than it really is.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look around you. To one side, the palace. To another, the busy line of Piccadilly and the grand hotels. Above you, the wide crowns of the plane trees, their bark peeling in patches of cream and grey. Green Park asks for nothing. It has no great show, no flowers to admire, no lake to circle. It simply gives you space and shade in the centre of a crowded city. As you walk out, listen again for the wind in those leaves... and carry a little of that quiet with you.
