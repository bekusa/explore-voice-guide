The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, traffic hums along the road at the edge of Regent's Park. In front of you, the sound drops away. Trees shift in the wind, a pigeon lands on warm stone, and footsteps cross a quiet courtyard. Then your eye goes up, to a golden dome resting against the London sky, and a slender white minaret rising beside it. This is the London Central Mosque, also known as the Regent's Park Mosque. For many British Muslims, it is simply the mosque at the park.

Before we go further, notice where you are standing. This is not a crowded city street. The mosque sits on the western edge of one of London's Royal Parks, with lawns and canal water close by. Elegant cream terraces from the Regency era line the neighbourhood. These are the stucco houses of the early nineteenth century that give this part of Westminster its calm, ordered look. Into this very English setting, the mosque adds a dome and a minaret. The contrast is deliberate, and it works.

The story of this place begins long before the building you see. In the early twentieth century, Muslims in London had nowhere central to pray together. They were students, sailors, diplomats and traders from across the British Empire. A community grew, and so did the wish for a proper mosque in the capital. The idea came up again and again. But land in central London is never easy to find, and for years the plan stayed only a plan.

Then came the Second World War. During those years, the British government gave its support to the project. Historical accounts record that the War Cabinet set aside money for a mosque site in London. Part of the reason was political. Britain had millions of Muslim subjects and allies, and a gesture of respect mattered. In 1944, King George the Sixth opened the Islamic Cultural Centre on this site. At first, it used an existing villa in the grounds.

Here is something many visitors miss. The building you are looking at is much younger than the institution behind it. For more than thirty years, worshippers prayed in older buildings on this land while the community raised funds and argued over designs. The plans that finally went ahead came from the British architect Sir Frederick Gibberd. He was chosen through a design competition. The mosque was completed and opened in the late 1970s, after decades of patience.

Gibberd is an interesting choice, and a second surprise. He was not a specialist in Islamic architecture. He was a modernist. He planned the post-war new town of Harlow, and he designed the Metropolitan Cathedral in Liverpool, a circular concrete church with a crown-like tower. So the same architect shaped one of England's best-known Catholic cathedrals and its best-known mosque. Look at the building again with that in mind. You can see a modern mind at work.

The design is clean and simple. Pale walls rise in plain vertical lines, broken by tall arched windows. The arches are a clear reference to traditional Islamic buildings, but they are drawn with modern restraint. There is no heavy ornament on the outside. Instead, the shapes do the work. The dome sits like a smooth golden bowl turned upside down. The minaret stands tall and narrow, a tower from which, traditionally, the call to prayer is made.

Let your eye rest on that dome. On a grey London day, it holds a soft, dull glow. When the sun breaks through, it turns bright gold and shines above the trees. From inside the park, you can sometimes catch it between branches, far across the lawns. It is one of the few golden domes on the London skyline. Walkers along the canal and the park paths often use it as a landmark to find their way.

Now the courtyard. This open space is where the life of the mosque spills outside. Before prayers, people gather here to greet friends and talk quietly. You will hear many languages — Arabic, Urdu, Bengali, Somali, Turkish, English and more. Families arrive together. Older men sit and wait. Students pass through between classes. On a Friday, around the midday prayer, the space fills quickly. It is the most important weekly prayer, and the crowd shows the size of this community.

If you go inside, the mood changes again. Shoes come off at the entrance, and the floor turns soft under your feet. The main prayer hall is covered in carpet, and the space feels wide and open. There are no pews and no chairs. Worshippers stand, bow and kneel together in rows. Above them, a large chandelier hangs from the centre of the dome. Light falls from the windows and the dome above, and the room feels calm and still.

Look toward the front wall of the hall. Here you will find the mihrab, a niche that shows the direction of Mecca. Every mosque in the world has one. Muslims face this direction when they pray, so the whole room is arranged around it. Nearby stands the minbar, a raised pulpit with steps. The imam uses it to deliver the sermon at Friday prayers. These two features tell you how the space is used, even when it is empty.

The building is more than a place of prayer. The Islamic Cultural Centre runs classes, a library, events and community services. People come here for marriages, for funerals, for advice and for learning. Visitors of any faith, or none, are generally welcome outside prayer times. Dress modestly, with arms and legs covered. Women are usually asked to cover their hair, so a scarf in your bag is useful. Speak softly, and ask before taking photographs of people at prayer.

As you leave, turn around once more. See how the golden dome sits above the green of the park and the pale terraces nearby. This is a building that took a long time to arrive. It grew out of a wartime promise, years of fundraising, and a modern architect's sense of line. Walk back toward the park gates slowly. Listen to the traffic return, and to the birds in the trees. And notice how often the dome stays with you, catching light between the branches behind you.
