The guide

The story

Stand for a moment beneath the portico. Six tall columns rise above you, pale and smooth, and the traffic of the Haymarket rolls past just a few steps away. Taxis brake, buses sigh, and footsteps hurry towards Piccadilly Circus. Yet under this deep stone porch, the air feels a little stiller. You are standing at the front door of one of London's oldest working theatres, a building that has watched this street change for two centuries.

First, the name. Haymarket is not poetic. It is practical. For a long time this wide road really was a market for hay and straw, feeding the horses of a growing city. Carts came in from the countryside, and the street was busy with traders and animals. When you look at the shops and theatres today, it is hard to picture. But the width of the road is a clue. It was made wide enough for loaded wagons to stop and turn.

A theatre has stood on this street since 1720. That first playhouse was a modest wooden building, put up by a carpenter named John Potter. It sat a little to the north of where you are now. It opened without a royal licence, which mattered a great deal at the time. In eighteenth-century London, only a few theatres had official permission to stage serious spoken drama. The rest worked in a grey area, and they were often in trouble with the authorities.

Here is the story most visitors never hear. In the 1760s the theatre was run by Samuel Foote, a comic actor known for his sharp tongue. During a visit to a country house, a group of aristocrats, including the Duke of York, dared him to ride a difficult horse. Foote was thrown, and his injured leg had to be amputated. The story goes that the Duke felt responsible. Foote soon received a royal patent, and the small Haymarket theatre gained the right to call itself a Theatre Royal.

The building in front of you is not Foote's theatre. It was designed by John Nash, the architect who shaped much of Regency London, and it opened in 1821. Nash moved the theatre slightly south, to this site, for a very deliberate reason. Turn around and look across the road. The portico lines up with Charles the Second Street, which runs straight down to Saint James's Square. From the square, the columns close the view like a stage set.

That is worth a pause. Nash did not think of buildings one at a time. He thought in streets and sightlines. Here, a theatre became part of the city plan itself. If you walk a short way down Charles the Second Street and look back, you will see the effect clearly. The pale columns fill the end of the street, framed by darker buildings on either side. It is a piece of urban theatre, staged before you even buy a ticket.

Look up at the columns themselves. They are in the Corinthian style, the most decorative of the classical orders from ancient Greece and Rome. You can recognise it by the capitals at the top, carved with curling acanthus leaves. Above them sits a triangular pediment. Behind the portico, the front wall is lined with windows and doors in a calm, even rhythm. Today the building is listed at Grade One, the highest level of heritage protection in England.

Inside, the story is a little different. The auditorium has been rebuilt more than once, most notably in the early twentieth century. So the room an audience sits in today is not exactly the room Nash designed. It is an intimate space by West End standards, with gilded balconies curving close to the stage. Many actors say they can feel the audience breathing. If you are attending a show, take a moment before the lights go down to look up at the ceiling and the boxes.

This stage has premiered work that still fills theatres around the world. Two of Oscar Wilde's comedies opened here in the 1890s: A Woman of No Importance in 1893, and An Ideal Husband in 1895. Imagine the evening crowds of late Victorian London arriving by carriage on this same street. They came in silk and top hats to hear Wilde's sharp lines for the very first time. Some of those lines are now quoted by people who have never seen the plays.

Every old theatre has a ghost, and the Haymarket is no exception. Locals and staff tell tales of John Baldwin Buckstone, an actor-manager who ran the theatre in the nineteenth century. He was said to love the building deeply. According to theatre folklore, his figure has been glimpsed backstage and in the dressing rooms, sometimes by well-known actors. Whether you believe it or not, the legend is part of the building's life. Some performers even say a sighting brings good luck.

Now look across the road. Directly opposite stands another grand playhouse, His Majesty's Theatre. The two buildings face each other like rivals in a long conversation. Between them, this short stretch of street has seen more first nights than many whole cities. Around you, the West End spreads out in every direction, from Leicester Square to Covent Garden. Trafalgar Square is only a few minutes' walk to the south, down past the National Gallery.

If you come back in the evening, the mood changes completely. The portico is lit, and the columns glow warm against the dark sky. Crowds gather on the pavement, checking tickets and finishing conversations. Before a performance, the sound of the street seems to fall away as people step inside. After the final curtain, the doors open and the noise returns all at once. It is one of the small daily rhythms of this part of London.

So take one last look before you walk on. Notice the worn steps beneath the portico, smoothed by two centuries of arriving audiences. Notice how the columns catch the light, and how the street opposite runs straight towards the trees of Saint James's Square. As you leave, glance back once. The theatre will still be framing the view, quietly waiting for tonight's crowd, just as Nash intended.
