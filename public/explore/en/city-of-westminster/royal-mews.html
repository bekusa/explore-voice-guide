The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere beyond the archway, a hoof strikes stone, then another. There is a faint smell of straw, saddle soap and warm horse. This is not a museum frozen behind glass. You are standing at the edge of a working royal stable in the heart of Westminster, a few steps from Buckingham Palace. The coaches here are polished for real journeys. The horses are trained for real processions. Let's walk through it together.

First, that curious word, mews. It has nothing to do with cats. In medieval English, to mew meant to moult, and a mews was the place where royal hawks were kept while they shed their feathers. The old King's Mews stood near Charing Cross, roughly where Trafalgar Square and the National Gallery are today. Over the centuries the hawks gave way to horses, but the name stayed. Today, all over London, quiet lanes called mews were once stable yards.

The buildings around you belong to the age of George the Fourth. When the king turned Buckingham House into a palace in the 1820s, the royal stables moved here too. The architect was John Nash, the same man who shaped Regent Street and much of Regent's Park. Look at the main archway and the clock tower above it. Nash liked calm, classical order, and he gave even the horses an elegant address. The quadrangle behind the arch still follows his plan.

Step into that quadrangle and notice the rhythm of it. Pale walls, regular windows, stable doors at ground level. Above them, staff once lived close to the animals they cared for, and some still do today. This is the strange thing about the Royal Mews. It is a place of ceremony, but it is also a place of daily chores. Hay is delivered. Harness is cleaned. Floors are swept. The routine of a country stable continues behind palace walls.

Now to the horses themselves. Two kinds of horse do most of the royal work here. The Windsor Greys are grey horses, often appearing almost white, chosen to pull the monarch's own carriage. The Cleveland Bays are a traditional British breed, rich brown with dark legs and mane. They pull many of the other carriages on state occasions. Depending on the day, you may see some of them in their stalls. Do not be surprised if a few are away, exercising or on duty.

These horses need unusual training. A procession down The Mall means crowds, cheering, flags, brass bands and sometimes the boom of gun salutes. A normal horse would bolt. So the animals here are slowly taught to stay calm through noise and movement. Here is something many visitors miss. The coachmen and grooms practise processions in the quiet hours, often very early in the morning, when the streets of Westminster are nearly empty. Few Londoners ever see these rehearsals.

And then there is the coach everyone comes to see. The Gold State Coach was built for King George the Third and delivered in 1762. It is covered in gilded carving: sea gods called tritons, palm trees, cherubs and crowns. The painted panels on its sides are the work of the Italian artist Giovanni Battista Cipriani. Since the coronation of William the Fourth in 1831, it has carried the monarch at every coronation. Most recently, it carried King Charles the Third back from Westminster Abbey in 2023.

The coach is not comfortable — not at all. It weighs around four tonnes and hangs on leather braces, so it sways as it moves. It needs eight horses, and it travels only at a walking pace. Several monarchs are said to have complained about the rocking ride. Look closely at the size of the wheels and the depth of the gilding. This is a vehicle built to be seen by thousands of people, not to give its passenger a smooth journey.

Nearby stands a much younger coach with a surprising secret. The Diamond Jubilee State Coach was built in Australia and first used in 2014. From the outside it looks traditional. Inside, it carries small pieces of history from across Britain. Fragments of famous ships, cathedrals and historic buildings are set into its woodwork. It also has modern touches, such as heating and electric windows. In a way, it is a carriage made of other places.

Other carriages here have their own roles. The Glass Coach, with its large windows, has carried royal brides and grooms on their wedding days. Smaller carriages are used to collect foreign ambassadors when they present their credentials to the monarch. That ceremony still happens regularly, so some of these vehicles leave the Mews far more often than you might think. Look at the coachwork, the brass lamps and the tidy harness hanging nearby. Everything is kept ready for use.

The Mews also looks after the motor cars. Alongside the carriages, the royal household keeps its official cars here, maintained by its own staff. This is a reminder that the Royal Mews is a department of a working household, not only a collection. Horses, carriages and cars are all managed from this one yard. The person in charge has the old title of Crown Equerry, a name that comes from the Latin word for horse. Tradition and modern logistics sit side by side.

Take a moment to notice the details around you. Harness here is a craft in itself. Leather is cut, stitched and polished by hand, and brass fittings are rubbed until they shine. Saddlers and other skilled staff keep these traditional trades alive. If you look at a full set of state harness, think of the hours of work behind it. Every buckle has to hold during a long procession, in front of the world's cameras.

As you leave, listen again. The traffic of Buckingham Palace Road is just outside, but inside the yard the sounds are older: hooves, a bucket, a quiet voice calming a horse. When you next see a royal procession on television, you will know where it began — here, in this stable yard. Look back once at Nash's archway and the clock above it. Then step out into Westminster, where the horses' route begins.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check before visiting, since the Mews closes during royal events.
Arrive early to see horses before they leave for exercise.
Combine with a walk along The Mall afterwards.
Ask staff which horses are in the stalls today.
Book ahead on summer weekends when palace visitors peak.
