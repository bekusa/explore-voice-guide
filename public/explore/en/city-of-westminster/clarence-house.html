The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on The Mall and listen. Taxis hum past on the red road surface. Pigeons scatter from the plane trees. Behind a long brick wall, a cream-coloured house sits quietly among the trees. It does not shout for attention. It almost hides. That modest appearance is part of the story, because this is one of the most closely watched private homes in Britain. Welcome to Clarence House.

The house takes its name from its first owner. In the 1820s, Prince William, Duke of Clarence, wanted a home of his own. He was a younger son of King George the Third, and nobody expected him to become king. He lived next door in Saint James's Palace, in rooms he found cramped and old-fashioned. So he asked for something new, something lighter and more comfortable, right beside the old Tudor palace.

The architect was John Nash. Londoners know his work well — the white terraces around Regent's Park, the sweep of Regent Street, and the early rebuilding of Buckingham Palace. Nash liked smooth stucco, a kind of plaster render painted to look like stone. Clarence House was finished in the late 1820s, and it carried that same clean, pale finish you can still see today. It was a gentleman's town house, not a fortress.

Then history took a turn. In 1830, the Duke's older brother, King George the Fourth, died without a legitimate heir. The Duke of Clarence became King William the Fourth. Here is a surprise many visitors miss. The new king did not move into a grand palace. He liked this house so much that he kept using it. Buckingham Palace was still unfinished, and William never actually lived there. A king, in other words, chose the smaller home next door.

After William's death, the house passed through other members of the royal family. Over the nineteenth century it was altered again and again. Rooms were added, a storey went up, and the original Nash design became harder to see. Today, much of what you look at is the result of later changes. Historians often say that little of Nash's original interior survives. The outline is his, but many hands have shaped the details.

During the Second World War, the house took on a very different role. It became an office for the British Red Cross and the Order of Saint John. Instead of royal dinners, its rooms held workers who tracked prisoners of war and sent parcels to families' loved ones abroad. The building was damaged during the bombing of London. When peace came, it needed serious repair before anyone could call it a home again.

That repair was made for a young couple. In 1947, Princess Elizabeth married Philip Mountbatten. Soon after, Clarence House was restored for them, and they moved in around 1949. Their daughter, Princess Anne, was born here in 1950. For a short, happy period, this was a family home with young children. Then, in 1952, King George the Sixth died, and Elizabeth became Queen. The family moved to Buckingham Palace, just a short walk down The Mall.

The next resident stayed for half a century. Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother, widow of George the Sixth, moved here in 1953. She lived in Clarence House until her death in 2002. For many Londoners, the house became her house. Each year on her birthday, in early August, crowds gathered at the gates. She would come out to greet them, often with members of her family, and well-wishers brought flowers and cards.

It is worth picturing that scene where you stand. Ordinary people pressed against the railings. Children were lifted onto shoulders. Bands sometimes played. It was one of the few moments when this private house felt open to everyone. Those birthday gatherings went on even when she was over one hundred years old. For a generation of Londoners, the gates of Clarence House are tied to that memory.

After her death, the house passed to her grandson, Prince Charles. He moved in during 2003, after a period of redecoration. He chose to keep much of the feel his grandmother had created, including paintings and furniture she had gathered. When he became King Charles the Third in 2022, Clarence House remained closely linked with him as his London residence. So once again, as with William the Fourth, a king lives in the smaller house beside the palace.

Now look at the setting itself. Clarence House stands at the western edge of the Saint James's Palace complex. The old red-brick palace, with its Tudor gatehouse, lies just to the east. Green Park and Saint James's Park stretch nearby. The house connects to the palace buildings, so it is part of a larger royal quarter rather than a lone mansion. Streets here are quiet, even though central London surrounds you on every side.

The house is mostly a working royal home, so you usually see it from outside. In some years, it has opened for guided tours of a few ground-floor rooms during limited periods, often in summer. Those arrangements change, so check the Royal Collection before you plan around them. If you do go inside, you are likely to see portraits, porcelain, and furniture connected with the Queen Mother's long life here.

Before you move on, take one more look. Notice the pale walls behind the trees, the tall windows, the flagpole above the roofline. When a flag flies there, it can show whether royal activity is underway. Listen again to the traffic on The Mall, and the leaves moving in the breeze. This is a house where a sailor prince became king, a princess began her family, and a widow greeted the crowds for fifty years. As you walk on toward the palace or the park, it is still there — quiet, private, and very much lived in.
