The guide

The story

Stand for a moment in the small courtyard off the Strand. Black cabs slide past you in an unusual direction. Polished steel letters spell out the word Savoy above your head. The air smells of exhaust and warm stone, and from inside the hotel doors comes a faint breath of perfume and coffee. You are standing at one of the most unusual front doors in London's theatreland.

This short lane is called Savoy Court. Look at the traffic again. Cars here drive on the right, not the left. It is often described as the only road in Britain where this is the rule. The usual explanation is practical. Drivers dropping off guests could pull in, and passengers stepped out right at the entrance without crossing the flow of cars. It is a small detail, but it sets the tone. Everything about this place was designed to make arrival easy.

The name itself is far older than the theatre. In the Middle Ages, this stretch of riverbank belonged to the Savoy Palace. It was one of the grandest houses in medieval London, and it was later held by John of Gaunt, a powerful royal duke. During the Peasants' Revolt of 1381, rebels burned the palace to the ground. The name survived the fire. Today it lives on in the street, the hotel, a small chapel nearby, and the theatre in front of you.

The theatre was the dream of one man — Richard D'Oyly Carte. He was a producer and impresario, a person who organises and funds performances. Carte had brought together a writer, W. S. Gilbert, and a composer, Arthur Sullivan. Their comic operas were already a sensation. Carte wanted a permanent home built just for them. The Savoy Theatre opened its doors in 1881, and its first production was the Gilbert and Sullivan opera Patience, which had transferred from another stage.

Here is the detail that most visitors miss. The Savoy was the first theatre in the world to be lit entirely by electricity. That is also how it is often described as the first public building of its kind. Before this, theatres burned gas. The flames gave off heat and fumes, and they turned the air thick on a crowded night. Fire was a constant danger. Electric light was clean, cool, and steady. For Victorian audiences, it was close to magic.

Carte knew people were nervous about this new technology. So he did something theatrical. According to well-known accounts, he stepped onto the stage holding a glowing light bulb. He wrapped it in fabric and broke it in front of the audience. The cloth did not catch fire. The point was simple — electric light was safe. It was a demonstration and a piece of showmanship at the same time, which is very much the spirit of this building.

The partnership of Gilbert and Sullivan became so tied to this stage that their works are still called the Savoy operas. Several premiered here, including Iolanthe and The Mikado. These shows mixed sharp satire with catchy, singable tunes. They poked fun at politicians, judges, the navy, and the manners of polite society. The fans who followed them devotedly earned their own nickname. People called them Savoyards, and the word is still used for Gilbert and Sullivan enthusiasts today.

The operas made a lot of money. Carte put much of that success into a new project right next door. The Savoy Hotel opened in 1889. It was built with modern comforts that were rare at the time, including electric light and many private bathrooms. Look around you now and you can see how the two buildings share this courtyard. The theatre and the hotel grew up together. The stage helped pay for the luxury rooms.

The theatre you will enter today is not the Victorian original, at least not on the inside. In 1929, the interior was rebuilt in the Art Deco style. Art Deco was the sleek, geometric fashion of the 1920s and 1930s, full of straight lines, metallic finishes, and stylised patterns. The new design kept the place feeling modern. It was a fitting choice. A theatre famous for being first with electric light now looked like the future again.

Then came disaster. In 1990, a fire broke out and gutted much of the auditorium. For a building once celebrated for making theatres safer from fire, this was a hard irony. The restoration took several years. Craftspeople worked from records and surviving details to recreate the Art Deco interior. The theatre reopened in 1993. What you see inside is a careful rebirth, faithful to the look of the late 1920s.

When you go inside, pay attention to the surfaces. The auditorium shines with metallic leaf, warm colours, and smooth, curving balcony fronts. There are no heavy Victorian cherubs here. Instead, the decoration is flatter and more patterned, almost like the inside of a jewellery box. Under the house lights, the walls glow softly. When the lights go down, that glow fades, and the stage becomes the brightest point in the room — just as Carte intended.

The theatre today hosts a wide range of productions, from musicals to plays, so the stage is far from a museum. On a show night, this courtyard fills with people in coats and scarves, checking tickets on their phones. Doormen from the hotel greet arriving cars. Taxis turn in, drop off, and swing back out to the Strand. Outside the courtyard, the busy road carries buses toward Trafalgar Square in one direction and toward the old City of London in the other.

Before you move on, look up once more at the steel letters above the entrance. Notice the polished surfaces, the neat lines, and the way the light catches them. Listen to the hum of engines on the wrong side of the road. This small courtyard holds a medieval name, a Victorian experiment with light, and a 1920s style restored after fire. As you walk back toward the Strand, think of that glowing bulb... and of the man who broke it to prove a point.
