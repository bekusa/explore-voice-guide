The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wheels of a suitcase rattle over cobblestones somewhere behind you. A swallow cuts past a tower roof. The stone under your hand is warm, pale gold, and rough enough to catch your fingertips. Above you, the walls climb in layers, one ring behind another, with pointed grey roofs rising like a row of witches' hats. This is the Cité of Carcassonne, a fortified hilltop town above the river Aude. People have lived on this hill for well over two thousand years.

Before the towers and the knights, this was a strategic rise of ground. It sat close to the old route between the Atlantic and the Mediterranean, and close to the road towards Spain. The Romans recognised its value. A Gallo-Roman town grew here, and parts of its early defensive walls still survive. As you walk, look low in the inner wall. In some stretches, the masonry changes. Small stones sit in neat rows, separated by bands of red brick. That pattern is one of the oldest layers you can see.

The people who held this hill changed many times. After Rome weakened, the Visigoths controlled the area. Later, Islamic armies from the south held it for a period, and then the Franks took it. Each group adapted the defences rather than starting again. This is why the walls look like a patchwork. They are not one building. They are centuries of building, stacked and repaired by people who often did not share a language or a faith.

Here is a story every child in Carcassonne learns. Legend has it that Charlemagne besieged the town for years. Inside, a widow named Dame Carcas ruled the defenders. When food almost ran out, she fed the last grain to the last pig, then threw it over the wall. The besiegers saw a well-fed animal and decided the town could never be starved. They left. As they did, bells rang out, and locals say the town's name came from that moment. It is a lovely tale. Historians agree it is folklore.

The real history of the high Middle Ages is darker. By the twelfth century, the Trencavel family ruled here as viscounts. Their territory included many followers of the Cathars, a Christian movement that the Church in Rome condemned as heresy. In 1209, a crusade arrived from the north. The young viscount, Raymond-Roger Trencavel, went to negotiate and was taken prisoner. The town surrendered, and he died in captivity soon after. The Cité then passed to Simon de Montfort, the crusade's military leader, and eventually to the French crown.

Under the French kings, the Cité became a royal fortress on the frontier with the Kingdom of Aragon. Much of what you see now dates from this period. The builders added the outer ring of walls and strengthened the inner one. Between the two rings lies a long open space called the lices. Walk into it. You are standing in a killing zone. Any attacker who crossed the first wall would be trapped here, under fire from the higher towers above.

Now a surprise. In 1659, the Treaty of the Pyrenees moved the border between France and Spain far to the south. Overnight, Carcassonne lost its military purpose. The fortress slowly fell into neglect. People took stones for other buildings. Houses filled the lices. By the nineteenth century, the authorities even considered demolishing the whole thing. The Cité you see today exists partly because people fought to save a ruin.

One of those people was a local historian, Jean-Pierre Cros-Mayrevieille, who campaigned for the walls. The writer Prosper Mérimée, then an inspector of historic monuments, supported the cause. The restoration went to the architect Eugène Viollet-le-Duc, who began work in the 1850s. The project continued for decades, even after his death. His team rebuilt towers, battlements and wooden walkways. They cleared away many of the houses that had grown against the stone.

Look up at those pointed roofs again. They are the most debated detail of the whole site. Viollet-le-Duc covered many towers with steep slate cones. Critics said this style belonged to northern France, not to the sunny south, where roofs were usually low and covered with clay tiles. Later restorers replaced some roofs with flatter terracotta ones. So as you walk, compare them. Grey slate here, orange tile there. You are looking at an argument about history, written in roofing.

That argument matters because this place is part reconstruction. It is a medieval fortress seen through nineteenth-century eyes. Some visitors feel disappointed when they learn this. Others find it more interesting. Without that work, there might be nothing here to argue about. In 1997, UNESCO added the Cité to its World Heritage list, and it recognised the restoration itself as an important chapter in the history of conservation.

At the heart of the Cité stands the Château Comtal, the count's castle. It is a fortress inside a fortress, with its own moat and its own walls. Even if the town fell, the lord could retreat here. Nearby, the Basilica of Saint-Nazaire mixes two styles. The heavier, rounded part is Romanesque, an older style with thick walls and round arches. The lighter part, full of tall windows, is Gothic. Step inside on a sunny day and watch coloured light fall across the floor.

The streets inside the walls are narrow and twisting. In summer they fill with visitors, shop signs, and the smell of cassoulet, the slow-cooked dish of beans, sausage and duck that this region claims as its own. Early in the morning or late in the evening, the mood changes. The crowds thin. You can hear your own footsteps again, and the stone holds the heat of the day long after sunset.

When you leave, take a moment at one of the gates and look down the hill. Below lies the lower town, the Bastide Saint-Louis, founded in the thirteenth century on a grid of straight streets. Between the two runs the Aude, crossed by the old stone bridge. For centuries, people walked between the hill and the river just as you are about to. One town looked back to defence. The other looked forward to trade.

So before you go, touch the wall once more. Feel the difference between the small Roman stones, the large medieval blocks, and the cleaner nineteenth-century repairs. Every layer is still here, side by side. Listen again for the wheels on cobblestones, the swallows, the voices in a dozen languages. The walls were built to keep people out. Today, they bring people in.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early morning to walk the streets before crowds.
Walk the lices between the two wall rings.
Wear sturdy shoes for steep, uneven cobblestones.
Visit the Château Comtal for access to the ramparts.
Cross the old bridge for a full view of the walls.
