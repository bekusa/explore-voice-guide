The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. Up here it comes straight off the ridge, dry and steady, and it carries the faint smell of sun-warmed stone and wild thyme. Under your feet the ground is rough limestone, cut and worn by many centuries of boots. Below the walls, the land drops away sharply, and far down you can see the green line of a river. This is Beaufort Castle, and the cliff it stands on is the reason it exists at all.

The river below is the Litani, one of the most important rivers in Lebanon. Here it bends through a deep gorge before it turns west toward the sea. Whoever held this rock could watch the roads between the coast, the inland valleys, and the hills to the south. Look east on a clear day and you may see the long pale shape of Mount Hermon. The view tells you more about this fortress than any plaque could.

Locals rarely call it Beaufort. In Arabic it is known as Qalaat al-Shaqif, and the word shaqif refers to a high, steep rock. The castle sits close to the village of Arnoun, a short drive from the town of Nabatieh. The name Beaufort came later, from French-speaking Crusader knights. It simply means beautiful fort, or strong fort. So two languages describe the same thing: a fortress built on a cliff.

The Crusaders were European armies who came to the eastern Mediterranean from the late eleventh century onward. They founded several kingdoms, including the Kingdom of Jerusalem. Historians generally agree that the Crusaders took control of this site in the first half of the twelfth century. They strengthened what they found and made it a major stronghold of the Lordship of Sidon, a feudal territory based on the coast. Much of the stonework you see today comes from Crusader building and from later rebuilding.

Now for the most famous story connected with these walls. In the late 1180s, the Muslim leader Saladin was taking back castle after castle across the region. He came to Beaufort and found it held by Reginald, the Lord of Sidon. Reginald did not simply fight. He negotiated, he asked for time, and he delayed for months. Saladin eventually lost patience. The siege continued, and the castle finally surrendered around 1190. One clever lord, one rock, and a long season of waiting.

The fortress did not stay in one set of hands for long. Through treaties and changing alliances, it returned to Frankish control in the thirteenth century. Later it passed to the Knights Templar, a religious military order famous for building and holding castles. Their time here was short. In 1268, the Mamluk Sultan Baybars captured Beaufort. The Mamluks were a powerful dynasty based in Egypt and Syria, and Baybars spent years removing the last Crusader strongholds from the region.

As you walk, look at the stone itself. You will notice that not every wall matches. Some blocks are large and carefully squared. Others are smaller and set with more mortar. This mix shows the long life of the building. Crusader masons, Mamluk builders, and later local rulers all repaired and changed it. In places, the walls seem to grow directly from the bedrock. The builders used the natural cliff as part of the defence wherever they could.

On the side facing the gentler slope, the castle needed more help. Here the builders cut deep into the rock to make a ditch, a moat without water, to keep attackers and siege machines away. Standing near it, you get a sense of the labour involved. This was cut by hand, with iron tools, block by block. Many visitors walk straight past it toward the view. It is worth pausing and looking down.

In the seventeenth century, the castle appears again in Lebanese history. Fakhr al-Din the Second, a powerful local emir who controlled much of Mount Lebanon under the Ottoman Empire, used it as one of his strongholds. The Ottomans were the Turkish empire that ruled this region for about four hundred years. After that period, the castle slowly lost its military role. Earthquakes, weather, and time damaged its towers. For generations it was more a ruin on a ridge than a working fortress.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Beaufort's military story did not end in the Middle Ages. In the twentieth century, its height made it valuable again. In the 1970s, Palestinian armed groups used the site. In 1982, Israeli forces captured it during their invasion of Lebanon, and they held it for eighteen years. When Israel withdrew from south Lebanon in 2000, the castle was handed back. For many Lebanese families, that moment still carries strong emotion.

That modern history left its own marks. Some damage you see may be medieval, but some came from shelling and military use in recent decades. In the years after 2000, a restoration project cleared debris, stabilised walls, and added paths for visitors. So when you look at a repaired arch or a fresh-looking section of masonry, you are looking at the newest layer of the castle's story. It is a medieval fortress, but also a modern memorial.

Take a moment to listen. Apart from the wind, it is often quiet up here. You may hear birds over the gorge, a car on the road toward Arnoun, or voices from a nearby village. The light changes quickly on this ridge. In the morning it is clear and cool. Late in the afternoon the stone turns golden, and the valleys fill with soft blue shadow.

Before you leave, walk back to the edge and look down at the Litani once more. Think of everyone who stood near this spot: Crusader lookouts, Saladin's soldiers waiting below, Mamluk guards, Ottoman-era emirs, and soldiers from modern wars. Each of them watched the same river and the same hills. Now it is your turn. Notice the rock under your hand, the wind on your face... and the long, quiet view to the south.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current security conditions in south Lebanon before travelling.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven limestone paths and steps.
Bring water and sun protection; the ridge has little shade.
Come in late afternoon for warm light over the gorge.
Hire a driver from Nabatieh if you lack a car.
