The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Underfoot, gravel crunches softly. Somewhere beyond the trees, the river Spree slides past, and a tour boat sounds its horn. But here, between tall plane trees and clipped hedges, the noise of Berlin seems to fall away. Ahead of you, the park opens into a long, formal axis of stone and lawn. At the far end, on top of a green mound, a giant figure stands against the sky. This is the Soviet War Memorial in Treptower Park, and it is built to be approached slowly.

First, a little context. In the spring of 1945, the Red Army fought its way into Berlin. The battle for the city was one of the last and bloodiest of the Second World War in Europe. Tens of thousands of Soviet soldiers died in the final weeks of fighting. After the war, the Soviet authorities chose several sites in Berlin to bury their dead and to honour them. Treptower Park became the largest. Thousands of soldiers lie buried beneath the lawns you are about to walk past.

The memorial was designed in the late 1940s. The architect was Yakov Belopolsky, and the main sculptor was Yevgeny Vuchetich, who later created some of the largest monuments in the Soviet Union. Work took several years, and the site opened in May 1949, just before the anniversary of victory. So, what you see is not a quiet cemetery. It is a full piece of state theatre, planned from start to finish. Every step, every statue, and every view line has a role.

Notice how the journey begins. Near the entrance sits a stone woman, seated and grieving. She is often called the Mourning Mother, and she represents the Soviet homeland weeping for her sons. Her head is bowed. Her hand rests on her chest. Walk past her, and the path guides you along an avenue of trees toward a gateway. This is the first emotional beat — grief — before anything about victory. The designers wanted you to feel loss before you feel triumph.

Now you reach the gateway itself. Two huge, stylised banners in dark red granite lean toward each other, like flags lowered in salute. At their feet, two bronze soldiers kneel, helmets in hand, heads bowed. Here is a detail many visitors hear about. It is often said that the stone for this memorial came from Hitler's ruined Reich Chancellery. Historians treat that claim with caution, and the story may be more legend than fact. Still, you will hear it told again and again on this spot.

Step through, and the whole space opens in front of you. This is the moment the designers planned most carefully. A long, sunken lawn runs straight ahead, divided into five large grassy squares. These are the mass graves. Thousands of soldiers lie under that quiet grass. Pause here for a breath. The scale is meant to feel overwhelming, and it does. The open sky, the symmetry, the silence — it all pushes your eye toward the figure on the hill.

Along both sides of the lawn, you will see a row of large white stone blocks. There are sixteen of them, one for each republic of the Soviet Union at that time. Each block carries carved scenes from the war — soldiers at the front, workers in factories, mothers saying goodbye, partisans in the forest. Look closely at the inscriptions. They are quotations from Joseph Stalin, written in Russian on one side and German on the other. After the Soviet Union collapsed, these texts stayed exactly where they were.

That brings us to a surprising fact. After German reunification in 1990, a treaty between Germany and the wartime Allies included a promise. Germany agreed to protect and maintain the Soviet war graves on its territory. That means this memorial, with its Stalin quotations and Soviet symbols, is cared for by the German state today. In the early 2000s, the site went through a major restoration. The great bronze soldier was even taken down and carried away for repair, then returned to his hill.

Now walk to the end of the lawn and climb the steps of the mound. Sculptors and architects call this kind of hill a kurgan, an ancient burial mound from the steppes of Eastern Europe. On top stands the statue that most people come to see. A Soviet soldier, roughly twelve metres tall, holds a small child against his chest. In his other hand, he holds a lowered sword. Under his boots lies a broken swastika. The message is direct: the soldier has destroyed fascism, and he protects the innocent.

There is a story behind the child. Soviet accounts tell of a sergeant named Nikolai Masalov. During the fighting in central Berlin, he is said to have rescued a German girl under heavy fire. That story became a symbol, and it shaped the idea for this statue. Accounts of the details vary, so treat it as part fact and part legend. A different soldier is usually named as the model who posed for the sculptor. Either way, the figure is a protector, not a conqueror.

Look at the base of the statue. Inside the pedestal is a small, round memorial hall. When it is open, you can glimpse a mosaic inside. It shows people from the different Soviet peoples laying wreaths, under a bright, golden light. This room is easy to miss, because most visitors look up at the soldier and forget to look in. Take a moment to check whether the door is open. The mosaic is one of the most colourful parts of the whole site.

The memorial is still a living place, not only a museum piece. Every year, around the eighth and ninth of May, people gather here to mark the end of the war. Flowers, wreaths, and candles appear on the steps. You may hear Russian, Ukrainian, German, and many other languages. Since the war in Ukraine began, these days have also become tense, and police often watch the gatherings. History here is not finished. People still argue about what this place means, and who it belongs to.

Before you leave, turn around at the top of the mound. Look back down the long green lawn, past the white stone blocks, toward the red granite gateway and the trees beyond. From up here, the whole design makes sense at once. Grief at the entrance, silence over the graves, and this single figure at the end. Listen again for the gravel, the birds, and the river somewhere behind the trees. As you walk back down, you are walking over the resting place of thousands of young men, far from home.
