The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere near the low ground, frogs are calling from the reeds. Water sits in shallow pools among fallen marble, green and still, and turtles slide off the warm stones as you approach. Around you, white column drums lie in the grass like toppled loaves of bread. Beyond the fence, the plastic roofs of greenhouses shine in the sun. This is the Letoon, one of the most sacred places of ancient Lycia, and it is quieter than almost anywhere you will visit on this coast.

Lycia was the old name for this rugged corner of southwest Anatolia, the land between the mountains and the Mediterranean. Its people had their own language, their own tombs cut into cliffs, and their own way of governing. The Letoon was not a city. It was a sanctuary, a holy precinct where people came to worship rather than to live. It sat a short distance from Xanthos, the leading Lycian city, and the two sites are listed together today as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The sanctuary belongs to Leto, a goddess from Greek myth, and to her twin children, Apollo and Artemis. Their story is tied to this valley. According to the myth, Leto was pursued by the jealous goddess Hera and could find no place to rest. Wolves guided her to the river here so she could drink and bathe her newborn twins. Some ancient writers linked the name Lycia to the Greek word for wolf. That link is folk etymology, a story told about the name, not proven history.

The frogs you hear have their own legend. In one version, retold by the Roman poet Ovid, Leto arrived thirsty at a pool. Local shepherds refused to let her drink. They stirred the mud and shouted insults. In her anger, the goddess turned them into frogs, condemned to croak in the water forever. So when the chorus rises from the flooded ruins, locals like to say you are hearing those rude shepherds still. It is only a tale... but it suits the place very well.

Now look at the centre of the site. Three temples stand side by side, facing the same way, like a family photograph in stone. The largest belongs to Leto herself. The smallest, in the middle, is usually given to Artemis. The third belongs to Apollo. Very few sanctuaries anywhere set three temples in such a tidy row. Standing between them, you can feel how the whole precinct was planned around this one sacred family.

The Temple of Leto is the one that catches the eye first. Archaeologists have put part of it back together, lifting original blocks into place and adding new stone only where pieces were missing. This method is called anastylosis, which simply means rebuilding a ruin with its own fragments. Look closely and you can tell old marble from new. The ancient stone is weathered and pitted, while the replacement pieces look smoother and paler.

Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. The Temple of Apollo once had a mosaic floor. Its design showed symbols of the twins rather than human figures — a lyre for Apollo, the god of music, and a bow and arrows for Artemis, the hunter. A small picture with no faces at all, yet every worshipper would have known exactly who was meant. If the floor is covered or fenced when you visit, that is to protect it.

The most famous discovery from the Letoon is not on display here. In 1973, excavators found a stone slab carved with the same decree in three languages: Lycian, Greek, and Aramaic, the official language used by the Persian Empire. Because scholars could already read the Greek, the slab helped them understand far more of Lycian, a language that had been difficult to decipher. Today this trilingual stele is kept in the museum in Fethiye, the nearby coastal town.

That mix of languages tells you something about Lycia itself. This region stood between larger powers. Persians ruled here for a time, then the Greek-speaking world of Alexander and his successors, and later Rome. The Lycians kept their own identity through all of it. Their cities formed a league, an early form of shared government, and the Letoon served as its religious heart. Delegates and pilgrims from across the region came to this valley.

Turn toward the slope and you will find the theatre. It is built into the hillside, in the Greek style, with stone seats rising in a curve. Its vaulted entrance passage is worth a look, and carved theatre masks have been found decorating parts of the structure. Climb a few rows and sit down. From here you can see the whole sanctuary laid out below, the three temples, the pools, and the flat green plain beyond.

Now look again at the water. The ground here sits very low, and groundwater rises up into the ruins for much of the year. One structure, a nymphaeum, which is a monumental fountain dedicated to water spirits, is often half flooded. This is a real headache for archaeologists. It is also why the site feels so alive. Reeds grow between columns, dragonflies hover, and the turtles treat ancient marble as their private sunbathing deck.

In later centuries, after the old gods lost their worshippers, Christians built a church within the sanctuary, reusing some of the earlier stone. Over time the whole area was abandoned and buried by river silt. Modern excavation has been led by French archaeologists since the mid-twentieth century, and their work continues. So what you see is not a finished picture. It is a site still being uncovered, piece by patient piece, season after season.

Before you leave, stand still once more near the pools. Notice the contrast around you. Inside the fence, marble, reeds, and frog song. Outside, the working farms of the plain, the hum of a tractor, the glint of greenhouse roofs. People have been growing food in this valley for thousands of years, and they still do. As you walk back, listen behind you. The shepherds of the old story are still calling from the water.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with nearby Xanthos the same day.
Visit Fethiye Museum to see the trilingual stele itself.
Bring water and a hat; shade is scarce here.
Wear shoes that can handle mud near the pools.
Climb the theatre seats for an overview of the site.
