The guide

The story

Listen first. Small waves slap against the rocks below. Cicadas buzz in the pines, a dry and steady sound that rises with the heat. Underfoot, the path is loose stone and dust, with low walls of rough grey masonry on either side. The water here shifts from deep blue to a pale, clear green near the shore. You are standing on Gemiler Island, a small strip of rock and scrub off the southwest coast of Turkey. The ruins around you belong to a Christian community that lived here many centuries ago.

Start with the name. In Turkish, gemi means ship, and Gemiler means, quite simply, ships. That fits. For a very long time, this island has been a place where boats stop. Today they are wooden excursion boats from Ölüdeniz and Fethiye, anchored in the channel between the island and the mainland. In the Byzantine era, the boats carried pilgrims. The island is also widely known by another name: Saint Nicholas Island.

That second name points to the island's most intriguing story. Saint Nicholas was a fourth-century bishop of Myra, a city further east along this same coast. Over many centuries, his legend grew into the figure that much of the world now calls Santa Claus. His remains were later taken from Myra to the Italian city of Bari. A long-debated idea suggests that this island may have been linked to his original burial or to an early shrine in his honour. Researchers have proposed this connection, but it remains a theory, not settled fact.

What is certain is the scale of the building here. For an island this size, the density of ruins is remarkable. Archaeologists have recorded several churches, along with houses, cisterns for collecting rainwater, and many tombs. Much of this construction is generally dated to the late Roman and early Byzantine centuries. That was the period when the eastern Roman Empire, ruled from Constantinople, had become Christian. Japanese archaeologists carried out long-term research here, and their work helped map the island's layout.

Now look along the ridge for one of the most unusual features. A long covered walkway once ran across the island, linking churches at different ends. Sections of its walls and supports still stand, and you can follow the line of it as you climb. Imagine pilgrims moving along it in shade, out of the sun and wind, from one sacred space to the next. It is a rare survival. Few sites anywhere preserve a structure quite like this processional route.

As you walk, notice the building materials. Most walls are made of local stone, roughly cut and set in mortar. In places, you will see thin courses of brick or tile layered between the stones. That is a common Byzantine technique. Some arches still hold their curve. Others have collapsed into heaps of rubble, now held together by thorny shrubs and wild herbs. On a warm day, you may smell thyme and sage as your feet brush past.

Keep an eye on the church floors and walls. In some places, faint traces of decoration survive. Patches of plaster may still carry a little colour, and fragments of floor mosaic have been recorded on the island. Much of this is fragile and weathered, so treat every surface gently. Here is something many visitors miss: the island's water came almost entirely from the sky. The large cisterns you see were essential. Without them, a settlement of this size could not have survived a long, dry Aegean and Mediterranean summer.

The tombs are another quiet surprise. They are scattered across the slopes, some cut into rock, some built from stone. Their number suggests that people did not only live here. Many were brought here to be buried. That fits the idea of a holy island, a place where believers wanted to rest close to something sacred. Whether that sacred focus was Saint Nicholas himself, or another saint, scholars still discuss.

So why was the island abandoned? The full answer is not clear. Many coastal communities in this region declined in the seventh century. It was a time of conflict, raids by sea, and wider upheaval across the eastern Mediterranean. An exposed island like this would have been hard to defend. It is likely that people moved away gradually. After that, the stones were left to the goats, the pines, and the sea.

When you reach higher ground, turn and look back toward the mainland. The hills across the channel are part of the coast near Kayaköy, an abandoned Greek village inland that many visitors also explore. The long-distance walking route called the Lycian Way passes through these hills too. Lycia was the ancient name for this whole stretch of coast. From up here, you can see how the island and the shore face each other like two halves of one harbour.

The view also shows why this channel has always been popular with sailors. The water between island and mainland is sheltered. Boats can anchor in calm conditions while the open sea moves beyond. In summer, you will hear engines idling, swimmers laughing, and music drifting faintly from the decks. It is a busy, cheerful scene today. Yet a few steps uphill, the ruins are almost silent, and the past feels very close.

Take a moment at the highest point you can comfortably reach. Feel the wind, which often picks up in the afternoon. Watch the light on the water, bright and broken into small flashes. Count the church outlines below you if you can pick them out. Each one was raised by hand, stone by stone, on a rock with no river and no forest of building timber. The effort behind it says a great deal about how much this place mattered.

As you head back down, slow your pace. Look at the edges of the path, where carved blocks sometimes sit among plain stones. Listen again for the cicadas and the small waves. Soon you will be back on a boat, salt drying on your skin. But for now, you are walking where pilgrims walked, on an island whose name still carries both ships and a saint.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for loose, uneven stone paths.
Bring plenty of water; there is little shade.
Visit early morning before excursion boats arrive.
Climb gently toward the ridge for channel views.
Combine the trip with nearby Kayaköy village.
