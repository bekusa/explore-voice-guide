The guide

The story

Listen to the sand under your feet. It does not crunch like ordinary beach sand. It feels softer, rounder, almost like tiny pearls rolling against your skin. Around you, the water of the Gulf of Gökova is a clear, pale green, and pine trees cover the low hills behind the shore. This is Sedir Island, a small piece of land off the coast of Muğla Province in southwestern Türkiye. It is famous for its beach, but it is also the site of an ancient city.

In antiquity, this island and its town were known as Kedrai, or Cedrae in Latin. The name connects to the word for cedar. The modern Turkish name, Sedir, also means cedar. So for more than two thousand years, people have named this place after trees. The town belonged to the wider region of Caria, an ancient land in what is now southwestern Anatolia. Its people lived from the sea, from olives, and from trade along this sheltered gulf.

Ancient writers did record a dramatic moment here. The Greek historian Xenophon describes how the Spartan commander Lysander attacked Kedrai near the end of the Peloponnesian War, the long conflict between Athens and Sparta. According to his account, the town was taken by force and its people were enslaved. Xenophon also notes that the population was mixed, part Greek and part non-Greek. That small detail matters. It shows that this was a frontier place, where Greek and Carian cultures lived side by side.

In later centuries, the area came under the influence of Rhodes, the powerful island state to the southwest. Rhodes controlled a stretch of the mainland coast, and Kedrai became part of that network. Later still, the region passed into the Roman world and then into the Byzantine era. Each period left something behind. As you walk inland from the beach, you will move through layers of stone from very different centuries, often only a few steps apart.

Now, the story most visitors come for — the sand. Legend has it that the Roman general Mark Antony shipped this sand from Egypt as a gift for Cleopatra, so that the queen could bathe on a beach like the beaches of her homeland. Locals call this shore Cleopatra Beach for that reason. It is a romantic tale. But it is a tale, and no ancient source confirms it.

What science can say is more interesting. The grains here are oolitic. That means each grain is a small, round sphere, built up in thin layers, like an onion. Ooids usually form in warm, shallow, chemically rich seas. Researchers have noted that this sand is very different from the sand on the surrounding coastline. Scholars still discuss how it formed here. Whatever the answer, you are standing on something unusual for this part of the Mediterranean.

Because the sand is so rare, it is strictly protected. Visitors once took handfuls home as souvenirs, and the beach began to shrink. Today the authorities control access closely. You may be asked to leave your shoes behind, rinse off before stepping on the beach, and avoid lying directly on the sand with towels. Taking even a little sand off the island is forbidden. These rules can feel strict, but they are the reason the beach still exists.

Leave the beach behind and follow the paths into the ruins. The ancient city walls run along the island, built from large, carefully fitted blocks of stone. In places they still stand well above head height. Look at the joints between the stones. The ancient builders shaped each block to fit its neighbour, without mortar. Lizards often sun themselves on these blocks, and cicadas fill the pines with a steady, dry buzzing in summer.

One of the most rewarding sights is the small theatre. Its stone seats are cut into the slope of the hill and face the water. Sit on one of the upper rows for a moment. From here you can understand why the builders chose this spot. The audience watched performances with the gulf and the mainland mountains behind the stage. The theatre is modest in size, which suits a town of this scale.

Nearby, you can find the remains of a temple and of the public spaces where the citizens met, traded, and argued. Scholars have linked the temple to the god Apollo, although the ruins are fragmentary. Later, in the Christian period, the people of the island built a church, and its foundations are still visible. Pagan temple and Christian church, on one small island — the change of faith in this region is visible here in stone.

Notice the vegetation too. Olive trees grow among the ruins, and some look very old, with twisted, split trunks. The pines give shade and a resinous smell on hot days. The whole gulf is part of a special environmental protection area. Seagrass meadows in the shallow water shelter fish, and the coast here is a haven for marine life. Swimming areas are marked, so please keep inside them.

Getting here is part of the experience. Most visitors arrive by boat, often from small harbours on the nearby mainland coast, a short crossing across calm water. Others come on longer day trips from the resort towns of the region. As the boat approaches, watch the island grow from a low green shape into a line of pale walls and sand. On busy summer days, many boats arrive at once, so mornings are usually quieter.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment, wherever you are. Feel the round grains of sand, or the warm stone of the old walls. Listen to the water against the jetty and the cicadas in the pines. People have stood on this island for more than two thousand years, under these same trees and above this same gulf. As your boat pulls away, look back at the walls along the shore. They are the last thing to fade from view.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive on an early boat to avoid midday crowds.
Rinse off before stepping onto the protected beach.
Never take any sand away from the island.
Wear sturdy shoes for the rocky paths to ruins.
Bring water and sun protection for little shade.
