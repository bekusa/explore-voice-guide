The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the dry grass on the hillside. Somewhere below, a goat bell rings, then stops. Under your shoes the ground is loose and pale, mixed with small pieces of limestone and broken pottery. Look up, and the rock face above you is cut with doorways. They are square, dark and silent. They were made more than two thousand years ago for the dead of Tlos.

This is one of the oldest cities of ancient Lycia. Lycia was the mountainous land along Turkey's southwest coast, between the sea and the high Taurus ranges. Today you are in Muğla Province, a short drive inland from Fethiye. Tlos sits on a steep outcrop above the wide valley of the Eşen River. In ancient times that river was called the Xanthos. From up here the farmland spreads out below like a green and gold carpet.

The position was chosen with care. A city on this rock could watch the whole valley. It could see travellers, armies and trade caravans long before they arrived. Many scholars connect Tlos with a place called Dalawa, which appears in Hittite records from the Bronze Age. The Lycians themselves called it Tlawa. If that link is right, people have lived on this hill for well over three thousand years. That makes Tlos far older than most of the ruins you can see today.

Tlos was not a small town. In the Hellenistic and Roman periods it was one of the leading members of the Lycian League. This was an alliance of cities that worked together and sent representatives to a shared assembly. The largest cities in the League held the most votes, and Tlos was among them. Some writers have pointed to the League as an early example of representative government. It is worth remembering when you stand among these broken walls.

Now turn your attention back to the cliff. The rock-cut tombs are the first thing most visitors notice, and they are the heart of Tlos. Lycians carved many of their tombs to look like the front of a house or a temple. You can see false columns, beams and door panels, all shaped directly from the living rock. The dead were given homes that looked like the buildings of the living. The whole hillside becomes a city above a city.

One tomb draws more attention than the others. It is often called the Tomb of Bellerophon. Its facade looks like a small temple, and inside the porch there is a carved relief of a rider on a winged horse. This is Bellerophon on Pegasus. In Greek myth, Bellerophon was a hero sent to Lycia to fight the Chimera, a fire-breathing monster. Legend has it that he settled in this land afterwards. Whoever was buried here wanted to share in that story.

The climb to the tombs is short but uneven. The path has loose stones and some steep steps cut into the rock. Take your time, and use your hands where you need to. When you reach the tomb entrances, look inside. The chambers are plain and quiet, with stone benches where the bodies once lay. The tombs were robbed long ago. What remains is the shape of the space, cool and dim, and the light from the valley outside.

Above the tombs, on the summit, you will see walls of a very different kind. These are not ancient. They belong to a fortress from the Ottoman period. Locals connect it with Kanlı Ali Ağa, a powerful regional lord of the nineteenth century. His name means Bloody Ali Agha, and stories about his hard rule still circulate in the area. The fortress walls stand on top of much older Lycian and Roman foundations. In one view you can see many centuries placed one above another.

Now walk down toward the lower city. Here the Roman period is easiest to see. There is a long stadium, laid out on the flat ground below the acropolis. Rows of stone seats line one side. Beside it is the open space of the agora, the city's market and meeting place. Imagine it full of traders, officials and farmers from the valley. Today it is grass, scattered column drums and the sound of insects in the heat.

Further on are the Roman baths. Local people often call them Yedi Kapı, which means Seven Doors. The name comes from the row of large arched windows along one wall. These openings face the valley. Bathers once looked out over the fields and mountains while they rested in the warm rooms. It is a small detail, but it shows how much the people of Tlos valued this view. They built their comfort around it.

The theatre lies on the slope a little away from the centre. It is a Roman theatre, built into the hillside in the usual way. Earthquakes and time have damaged it, and many seat blocks have fallen. Still, you can sit in the curved rows and look down at the stage area. Carved stones lie around it, some with decoration still sharp at the edges. Archaeological work continues at Tlos, so parts of the site may look different from one visit to the next.

It is easy to forget how long Tlos remained forgotten by the outside world. For centuries, farmers grazed animals among the ruins and reused the stones in their houses. European travellers began writing about the site in the nineteenth century. They were drawn by the tombs and by the link with Bellerophon. Systematic excavation came much later. Many of the buildings you see today were cleared and studied only in recent decades.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot near the edge of the hill. Look east across the valley toward the high mountains, often pale with snow in the cooler months. Look down at the road, the fields and the modern village. Then look back at the dark doorways in the cliff. The people who carved them saw this same valley every day. As you walk back down, listen again for the wind in the grass and the goat bells below.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the loose, steep path to the tombs.
Visit early morning to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Carry water; shade on the hillside is limited.
Combine your visit with the nearby Saklıkent Gorge.
Bring a torch to look inside the dim tomb chambers.
