The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wooden shingles creak in the breeze above you, and somewhere behind the walls a bell rings for the nuns. Then look up at the church. Almost every surface is painted, and the colour that fills your eye is green. It is a deep, cool green, like the wooded hills that ring this valley. You are standing in the Bukovina region of northeastern Romania, at Sucevița Monastery. It is one of the last and fullest of the painted churches of Moldavia.

First, a little orientation. For centuries, this corner of Europe belonged to the Principality of Moldavia, a small Orthodox state between larger powers. In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, its princes and nobles built churches here and covered them with frescoes, inside and out. Painting the outside walls was unusual. A fresco is paint laid into wet plaster, so the colour binds with the wall. Here, those paintings faced snow, rain and summer sun. Many have survived for over four hundred years.

Sucevița came late in that tradition, and you can feel it. It was founded by the Movilă family, a powerful noble clan. Three brothers stand behind it. Gheorghe was a senior churchman. Ieremia became Prince of Moldavia. Simion would also rule for a short time. The church was raised in the 1580s, and the painting followed around the turn of the seventeenth century. By then, the masters of this art knew exactly what they were doing.

Notice the walls before the church. This is not a soft rural cloister. It is a square fortress, with thick stone walls, corner towers and a heavy gateway. Moldavia was a frontier land. Armies crossed it, and raiders came through these valleys. A monastery had to protect its monks, its treasures and the people who ran to it in danger. So the outer walls feel military, and the church inside them feels like a jewel box.

Now look at the shape of the church itself. Historians often describe the Moldavian style as a meeting of two worlds. The plan and the painting come from the Byzantine East, the Greek Orthodox tradition. Some details, like the pointed window frames and the stone buttresses, come from the Gothic West. Above it all sits a huge, steep roof with wide eaves. Those eaves are not only decoration. They throw rain away from the frescoes, and they are one reason the paint is still here.

Walk to the north side. This wall usually suffered most from cold winds, and painters often left it weaker. Here it holds the most famous image at Sucevița: the Ladder of Virtues. It is based on a book by Saint John Climacus, a monk from Mount Sinai. A long diagonal ladder rises from earth to heaven. Monks climb it, step by step. On one side, angels in neat rows wait for them. On the other side, dark demons pull at their robes.

Take your time with this scene. The order on the angels' side is calm and regular. The demons' side is chaos. Small black figures grab ankles and drag climbers down into a waiting mouth below. Each rung of the ladder stands for a virtue a monk must master. Most visitors see a dramatic picture. The surprise is how practical it is. It was a lesson for the people who lived here, painted where they walked every day.

Now move round to the south wall, the side that catches the sun. Here you will find the Tree of Jesse, a family tree of Christ. It grows from the reclining figure of Jesse and branches out across the wall. Look carefully among the figures. In several Moldavian churches, the painters placed ancient Greek philosophers near the prophets. They were shown as wise pagans who pointed toward the truth. It is a small, generous idea, hidden in plain sight.

Here is a second surprise. Walk to the west wall, at the back of the church. It is almost bare. On a building where nearly every surface is covered, that empty face is striking. Nobody can say for certain why it stayed unpainted. Locals tell a story that a painter fell from the scaffolding here and died. After that, the legend says, no one would finish the work. Treat it as a tale, but it fits the silence of that wall.

There is another legend, too. According to local tradition, a woman spent many years carrying stones to the site with her ox cart, to help build the monastery. Some visitors look for a carved stone head of a woman set in the walls and connect it to her. Historians cannot confirm the story. Still, it reflects something true. Places like this depended on the labour of ordinary people, not only on the princes who paid for them.

Step inside if you can. The interior is darker, lit by small windows and candles. The paintings continue across walls and vaults, and the smell of wax and old wood fills the rooms. In one chamber lie members of the founding family, including Prince Ieremia Movilă. The monastery also keeps a museum of embroidery, manuscripts and religious objects. Moldavian needlework of this period was extremely fine. Some pieces include gold and silver thread.

Remember that this is still a living convent. Nuns live and pray here, and the daily services are real worship, not a show. You may see sisters tending the garden or sweeping the paths. Dress modestly, and keep your voice low inside the church. In 2010, Sucevița joined the group of painted churches listed by UNESCO, the United Nations cultural agency. Even so, the rhythm of the place belongs to the people who live here.

Before you leave, walk back toward the gate and turn around once more. From a distance, the green walls seem to sit inside the hills, as if the forest had climbed onto the church. The angels are still lined up on their ladder. The western wall is still waiting for its painter. Listen again for the shingles and the bell. Then carry that quiet with you as you step back out into the valley.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cover shoulders and knees before entering the church.
Walk all four walls clockwise to follow the paintings.
Visit early morning for softer light on the frescoes.
Ask whether interior photography is allowed before shooting.
Allow time for the embroidery museum inside the walls.
