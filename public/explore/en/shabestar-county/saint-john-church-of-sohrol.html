The guide

The story

Listen first. A village lane, a dog barking somewhere beyond a mud-brick wall, wind moving through poplar trees. Underfoot, the ground is dry and uneven, packed earth and loose stone. Then the church comes into view: a compact stone body, a drum, and a pointed roof rising above the low houses. This is the Church of Saint John in the village of Sohrol, in Shabestar County, in the north-west of Iran.

Before we talk about history, take in the setting. Shabestar County lies north-west of the city of Tabriz, close to the northern shore of Lake Urmia. This is farming country, with orchards, fields, and villages scattered across rolling hills. The light here is often sharp and clear, and in the afternoon it turns the stone warm and golden. The church does not compete with the landscape. It sits inside it, quietly, like a building that has watched many generations come and go.

This is an Armenian church. Armenians have lived in this corner of Iran for many centuries. Around Lake Urmia, and in the valleys towards Salmas and Khoy, there were once many Armenian villages, each with its own church. Some of those communities shrank or moved away over the last two centuries, especially during the upheavals of the early twentieth century. The buildings often stayed behind. The church at Sohrol is one of these survivors, a reminder of a community that shaped this land.

The church is dedicated to Saint John. In Armenian he is called Surp Hovhannes. The name was popular for churches across the Armenian world, and it usually honours John the Baptist, a figure of deep importance in the Armenian tradition. When you hear locals call this place the Church of Sohrol, or simply the Armenian church, they mean this same building. Names here travel easily between Armenian, Azerbaijani Turkish, and Persian.

Now look at the shape. Armenian church builders developed a very recognisable style over more than a thousand years. The typical form is a solid stone hall or cross-shaped plan, with a round or many-sided drum on top, and a pointed, cone-like roof above the drum. That roof is not a decoration. It protects the dome beneath it from rain and snow. If you can see that drum and cone from where you stand, you are looking at an idea that travelled here from the Armenian highlands.

Here is something many visitors miss. From outside, Armenian churches often look plain, almost severe. The walls are thick, the windows are narrow, and the doorways are small. This was practical. Thick stone walls keep the interior cool in summer and hold some warmth in the cold winters of this region. Narrow windows let in thin beams of light rather than a flood. Inside, that light falls in lines across the floor and walls, and the effect can be very quiet and very focused.

The exact dating of this church is not something we can state with confidence. Like many rural churches in the region, it has probably been repaired and rebuilt more than once. Earthquakes are common in north-west Iran, and stone buildings here often carry layers of repair from different periods. So rather than fixing a single year to it, it is more honest to think of this building as a long project, shaped by many hands over a long time.

Take a moment to study the stonework. Look at how the blocks are cut and laid. In some places the joints may be tight and regular. In other places you may notice rougher patches, different stone, or newer mortar. These differences tell you where work happened at different times. Weathering also leaves its own marks. Wind, frost, and sun soften the edges of carved surfaces, and the side facing the prevailing weather usually looks older than the sheltered side.

Armenian builders often carved crosses into the walls of their churches, and into separate standing stones called khachkars, which means cross-stones. A khachkar is a flat stone slab with a cross carved on its face, often surrounded by patterns of leaves and interlaced lines. Not every church has them, and some have been lost or moved. But it is worth walking slowly around the outside of the building and looking near the doorways, corners, and the surrounding ground for any carved crosses or old inscriptions.

The church is also part of a living village. Sohrol is not a museum site with a gift shop. People live here, farm here, and walk past this building every day. Most of the village population today is not Armenian, yet the church has remained part of the local landscape. Iran has listed many Armenian churches as national heritage, and state restoration work has helped many of them survive. Treat the place with the respect you would give any sacred building, even when it is quiet and empty.

Think about what this building would have meant to the people who built it. For a small rural community, the church was the centre of life. Baptisms, weddings, funerals, and feast days all happened here. The bell, the chanting of the Armenian liturgy, and the smell of incense would have filled this space. The Armenian Church has its own ancient language of worship, called Grabar, or classical Armenian, which is still used in services today.

There is another quiet surprise here. Iran is home to some of the oldest Armenian Christian sites in the world. Further north-west, in West Azerbaijan province, there are famous monasteries that are recognised by UNESCO. Those draw most of the foreign visitors. Small village churches like this one receive far fewer travellers. That means you may have this place almost to yourself, with time to look closely, to listen, and to notice details that crowded sites never allow.

Before you leave, step back and look at the church as a whole. See how its height compares with the houses around it. Notice the colour of the stone against the sky and against the earth-coloured walls of the village. Look at the hills behind it, and imagine the same view in winter, under snow. A building like this was meant to be seen from a distance, as a marker for travellers and for people coming home from the fields.

And now, simply stand for a moment. Feel the ground under your feet and the air on your face. Listen again to the village: the wind, the voices, perhaps a tractor on a distant road. The church has heard these sounds for a very long time. As you walk away, turn back once more and look at the roof against the sky. It is a small building, but it carries the memory of a whole community in its stone.

Practical tips

Visit in late afternoon when the stone turns warm gold.
Ask in the village if someone holds a key to the interior.
Dress modestly; shoulders and knees covered for this sacred site.
Combine the trip with a visit to nearby Tabriz.
Bring water and sturdy shoes for uneven village lanes.
