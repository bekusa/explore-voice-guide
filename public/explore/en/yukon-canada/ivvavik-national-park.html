The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. There is almost nothing to hear. Perhaps the wind moving across the tundra, the low rush of a river somewhere below you, the buzz of an insect near your ear. Underfoot, the ground is soft and springy, a thick mat of moss, lichen and dwarf shrubs. In summer the light does not fade at midnight. It simply circles the sky. Welcome to Ivvavik National Park, in the far northwest corner of Canada's Yukon.

Let's start with the name, because it explains almost everything. Ivvavik comes from Inuvialuktun, the language of the Inuvialuit, the Inuit people of the western Canadian Arctic. It is usually translated as a place for giving birth, or a nursery. The name does not refer to people. It refers to caribou. Every spring, the coastal plain and foothills here become a calving ground for the Porcupine caribou herd, one of the great migrating herds of North America.

The Porcupine herd takes its name from the Porcupine River, far to the south. Each year these animals travel across a huge range that crosses the border between Yukon and Alaska. In late spring the pregnant females move north toward the Arctic coast. Here, the wind off the sea keeps the insects down, and fresh plants appear as the snow melts. The calves arrive into this landscape. Within days, they can walk and run with their mothers.

Now, here is something many visitors do not realise. Ivvavik was not created in the usual way, by a government drawing a line on a map. It was established in 1984 through the Inuvialuit Final Agreement, a land claim settlement between the Inuvialuit and the Government of Canada. It was the first national park in Canada created as a direct result of an Indigenous land claim agreement. That origin still shapes the park today.

Ivvavik is managed cooperatively. Parks Canada works together with the Inuvialuit through joint management bodies. In practice, this means that traditional knowledge sits beside scientific study. Inuvialuit people continue to hunt, fish and travel on these lands, as their families have done for generations. So when you walk here, you are not in an empty wilderness. You are in a homeland, a place with names, stories and seasonal routines that are much older than the park.

Look at the shape of the land around you. To the south rise the British Mountains, rounded and rugged at the same time. To the north, the land slopes down toward the Beaufort Sea, part of the Arctic Ocean. To the west, across the border, lies the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge in Alaska. Together, these protected areas form one long, connected stretch of Arctic country. Animals do not see the border at all.

Here is the second surprise. Much of this landscape was never buried under the great ice sheets of the last ice age. While glaciers covered most of Canada, parts of northern Yukon stayed free of ice. This region was part of Beringia, a wide unglaciated zone that once linked Asia and North America. Because the ice did not scrape the land flat, you can still see older landforms here. The valleys, ridges and tors have a different, more weathered character than glaciated country further east.

The Firth River runs through the heart of the park, north toward the sea. It is one of the oldest river valleys in Canada, cut deep into rock over a very long time. In places, the canyon walls close in and the water turns to white rapids. For many visitors, the Firth is the reason they come. Rafting trips descend the river over many days, passing cliffs, gravel bars and side valleys where wildlife comes down to drink.

The river has also carried people for thousands of years. Archaeologists have found sites along the Firth that show long human use of this valley, from ancient hunters to more recent Inuvialuit families. Stone tools, old campsites and caribou fences tell of people who followed the animals and the seasons. If you come across stones arranged in lines or circles, leave them exactly as they are. They may be part of that long human story.

Now think about the animals that share this place. Beyond the caribou, the park is home to grizzly bears, wolves, wolverines and Arctic foxes. Dall's sheep, white wild sheep, move along the steep slopes of the mountains. Muskoxen, with their long, shaggy coats, sometimes graze in the valleys. Overhead, you may see golden eagles, gyrfalcons, or flocks of migrating birds that come north to nest in the brief summer. Every sighting here is earned. The animals are wild, and the distances are large.

Getting here is part of the experience. There are no roads into Ivvavik. Most visitors fly in by small charter aircraft, usually from Inuvik in the Northwest Territories. The plane lands on gravel strips or open tundra. Parks Canada has operated a base camp in the park, at Imniarvik in the Sheep Creek area, where small groups can stay and explore on guided hikes. Because of this, only a small number of people visit each year. The solitude is real.

Life here moves with the season. Summer is short and intense. Wildflowers appear on the tundra, purple, white and yellow, close to the ground. The permafrost beneath the surface stays frozen, so water collects in pools and wet patches. The sun stays up through the night, and the light at midnight turns gold and soft. Then, quite quickly, autumn arrives. The tundra turns red and orange. The caribou move south again, and the long dark season returns.

Visiting a place like this asks something of you. The land is fragile. A footprint in the moss can last for years. Weather can change fast, and help is very far away. Bears are a real presence, so groups travel with care and keep food stored properly. Most people come with guides or after careful planning with Parks Canada. In return, the land gives you something rare: a sense of scale, and of how small and temporary our own visit is.

So, before you move on, stand still for a moment. Look at the ground at your feet, the tiny leaves and lichens that grow so slowly here. Look up at the ridgelines, and imagine the caribou coming over them in spring, as they have for countless generations. Listen again to that quiet. When you leave Ivvavik, you may carry that silence with you. It is one of the things this nursery of the North gives to everyone who passes through.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan your charter flight from Inuvik well in advance.
Contact Parks Canada before travel for registration and orientation.
Pack waterproof layers for sudden Arctic weather changes.
Carry bear spray and store food in bear-proof containers.
Bring an eye mask for sleeping under the midnight sun.
