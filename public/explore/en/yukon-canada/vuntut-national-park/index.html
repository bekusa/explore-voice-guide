The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. There is no road noise here. No engines, no distant traffic, only wind moving across low tundra and the thin call of a bird somewhere over the water. The ground underfoot is soft and springy, a carpet of moss, lichen and dwarf shrubs. In summer the light does not leave. It circles the sky, low and golden, long after the hour when you expect night. This is Vuntut National Park, in the far north of the Yukon, well above the Arctic Circle.

The name comes from the Gwich'in language, spoken by the Vuntut Gwitchin people who have lived in this country for thousands of years. Vuntut is usually translated as "among the lakes." Once you see the land from the air, the name makes perfect sense. Below you, water spreads in every direction. Thousands of shallow lakes and ponds sit in the flat ground, round and oval, some joined by narrow channels, others standing alone. They catch the sky like scattered pieces of a broken mirror.

That great maze of water is the Old Crow Flats. It is one of the largest wetland complexes in northern Canada, and it is protected as a wetland of international importance. Part of the Flats lies inside the park, and the rest is managed as a special area by the Vuntut Gwitchin. Each summer, very large numbers of waterbirds arrive here. Ducks, geese, loons and swans come to nest and to moult. For a few weeks, while they replace their flight feathers, many of them cannot fly at all.

Here is something most visitors never realise. You cannot drive to this park. There is no road at all. The nearest community is Old Crow, a small village on the Porcupine River, south of the park. Old Crow is the only community in the Yukon that has no year-round road connection. People and supplies arrive mostly by plane. So if you are standing here now, you came by air, by boat, or on foot. Very few people in the world can say that.

The park was created in 1995. It came out of the land claim agreement between the Vuntut Gwitchin First Nation and the governments of Canada and the Yukon. This matters. The park was not drawn on a map far away and imposed on the people who live here. The Vuntut Gwitchin helped to shape it, and they share in its management with Parks Canada. Their knowledge of the land, the animals and the seasons guides how the park is cared for today.

Now for a second surprise. During the last ice age, enormous ice sheets covered most of Canada. But this corner of the Yukon stayed largely free of ice. It belonged to a vast region scientists call Beringia — a dry, cold grassland that once linked Asia and North America where the Bering Sea lies today. Mammoths, steppe bison and ancient horses moved across it. Along the rivers of this region, the eroding banks still give up fossil bones from that distant world.

Because of this ice-free history, the Old Crow area is important to archaeologists and to people who study how humans first came to the Americas. Researchers have worked in this region for decades, looking at bones, tools and ancient sediments. Some of the questions are still debated, and the dates are argued over in scientific papers. But the main idea is simple. People may have lived in this northern country very early, while much of the continent was still buried under ice.

The animal that defines this land is the caribou. The Porcupine caribou herd is one of the great migrating herds of North America. Every year it travels across the Yukon and Alaska, between winter forests and the calving grounds near the Arctic coast. Parts of its route cross this park. For the Vuntut Gwitchin, the caribou are far more than wildlife. They are food, clothing, story and identity. The life of the community has followed the movements of the herd for generations.

The herd does not come on a timetable. Some years the caribou pass close to Old Crow, and some years they take another path. If you see them, you may see a few animals at the edge of a ridge, or a long line moving steadily over the hills. Watch how they walk. They rarely seem to hurry, and yet they cover huge distances. Listen too. Caribou make a quiet clicking sound as they walk, from tendons in their feet.

Look around you at the landscape itself. To the north and in parts of the park, the ground rises into rolling hills and low mountains. Between them lie wide valleys of tundra, the colour of olive and rust in summer, then red and gold in late August. Small spruce trees grow in sheltered places. Underneath everything is permafrost, ground that stays frozen all year. Only a thin top layer thaws each summer. That is why the earth feels soft and why water lies everywhere.

This park is not alone. To the north it meets Ivvavik National Park, which runs toward the Arctic coast. To the west, across the border, lies the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge in Alaska. Together these protected lands form one of the largest connected wild areas on the continent. Animals do not see the border. The caribou, the bears, the wolves and the birds move freely across lines that exist only on human maps.

There are no visitor centres out here, no marked trails, no shelters and no signs pointing the way. Anyone who travels in this park must be completely self-reliant. Weather changes fast. Grizzly bears live here. Help is far away. That is why the park sees very few visitors each year, which makes it one of the least visited national parks in Canada. For those who do come, that emptiness is the whole point. The silence is real, and so is the responsibility.

In Old Crow, the John Tizya Centre and the community itself are the best places to learn more. People there can share what this land means to them, far better than any guide. If you have the chance, listen to local stories about the caribou, the muskrat camps on the Flats, and the long spring days. Ask before you take photographs of people. Respect the land as a home, not only as scenery.

Before you go, stand still one more time. Notice the light lying low across the water. Notice the cool air, the smell of wet moss, the wide sky with nothing in it but birds and cloud. These lakes have reflected that same sky for thousands of years. As you turn away, take one last look at the shining water among the hills — and remember that you were one of the few people who stood among the lakes.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan access by charter flight or from Old Crow.
Travel fully self-reliant with emergency communication gear.
Carry bear spray and know grizzly safety procedures.
Visit the John Tizya Centre in Old Crow first.
Ask permission before photographing community members.
