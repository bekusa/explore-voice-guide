The guide

The story

The air changes as you climb. Down on the plain it is warm and close, but up here on the plateau a steady wind comes off the cliffs. It carries dry grass, wood smoke from a distant village, and the faint mineral smell of sun-warmed sandstone. Underfoot the stone is worn smooth in places, rough and broken in others. And all around you... silence, except for birds and the wind in the scrub.

You are standing at Rohtasgarh, the fort of Rohtas, in the Rohtas district of Bihar. It sits high on the Kaimur plateau, close to the valley of the Son River. One thing is worth saying clearly at the start. This is not the better-known Rohtas Fort in Pakistan, built by the Afghan ruler Sher Shah Suri. The two share a name and a connection to that ruler, but they are entirely different places.

The setting explains almost everything about this fort. Nature built most of the defences. Sheer cliffs drop away on several sides, and the top of the plateau is broad enough to hold palaces, temples, wells and gardens. An army that held this ground could watch the plains for a long distance. An army outside it could do very little except wait. For centuries, rulers understood that whoever held Rohtas held a key to the region.

The name itself comes from a legend. Local tradition links the hill to Rohitashva, the son of King Harishchandra, a figure from Hindu storytelling who was famous for never breaking his word. According to the tale, Rohitashva lived or took refuge here, and the place carries his name. This is folklore rather than recorded history. But you will still hear it told with real affection in the villages below.

The documented history becomes clearer in the sixteenth century. Around 1539, the fort passed from a Hindu chieftain to Sher Shah Suri, the Afghan ruler who briefly pushed the Mughals out of northern India. How he took it is the subject of a popular story. Legend has it that his soldiers entered hidden inside covered palanquins, the curtained litters used to carry women. Whether that happened exactly as told, nobody can say for certain.

Here is a connection many visitors miss. Sher Shah Suri came from this part of the world. His great tomb stands in Sasaram, the nearby town, rising from the middle of a large artificial lake. So when you look out from these cliffs across the plain, you are looking toward the home ground of the man who once held this fort. Rohtas was not a distant conquest for him. It was close to his roots.

After the Mughals returned to power, the fort took on a new life. Under the emperor Akbar, Raja Man Singh, one of his most trusted generals and a governor in the east, used Rohtas as an important base in the late sixteenth century. Much of what you see today comes from this period. Man Singh built a palace complex here. It is the largest and most complete group of buildings on the plateau.

Walk through those palace rooms slowly. You will see a mix of styles that tells you a lot about Mughal India. There are arched halls and domed pavilions, but also carved brackets and balconies that come from Rajput building traditions. Rajput refers to the warrior clans of western India, to which Man Singh belonged. The stone is local sandstone, and in many places traces of plaster and painted decoration still cling to the walls.

Keep an eye out for the great gateways too. The best known is the Hathiya Pol, the Elephant Gate. It takes its name from the elephant figures carved into its stonework. Gates like this were built tall enough for elephants carrying riders to pass through. Stand beneath it for a moment and look up. The scale was designed to impress anyone arriving, whether they came as guest, rival or tax collector.

The fort was never only a military place. Scattered across the plateau are temples and a mosque, sometimes within sight of each other. There is a temple dedicated to Ganesh, the elephant-headed god of beginnings, and older shrines linked to the hill's sacred past. There is also a mosque from the Mughal era. Together they show that this hilltop served many communities over many centuries, each leaving its own layer.

As Mughal power faded in the eighteenth century, Rohtas slowly lost its importance. Armies moved differently, new trade routes grew, and power shifted toward the coast. The palaces emptied. Trees and grass took over the courtyards. That long period of quiet is part of why the fort feels the way it does today. There are no crowds pressing through, no souvenir stalls lining every corridor. Mostly, there is space.

Getting here is part of the experience. Many visitors climb up from the village at the foot of the plateau on a long stone stairway, and it is a real effort in the heat. During the monsoon season, streams fill and waterfalls pour off the edges of the plateau. The rocks turn green, mist settles in the valleys, and the whole landscape looks completely different from the dry months.

Before you leave, find a spot near the edge where you can see the plain below. Look at the fields, the thin lines of roads, perhaps a river glinting in the distance. This is the same view that sentries, governors and pilgrims looked at for hundreds of years. Listen to the wind for a moment. Then, as you walk back across the plateau, notice how the old walls fade quietly into the trees.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start the climb early to avoid the midday heat.
Carry plenty of water; facilities on the plateau are limited.
Wear sturdy shoes for the long stone stairway.
Visit during monsoon to see waterfalls off the plateau.
Combine with a visit to Sher Shah's tomb in Sasaram.
