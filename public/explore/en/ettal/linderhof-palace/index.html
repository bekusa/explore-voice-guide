The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your feet, and somewhere ahead water falls onto stone. The air smells of cut grass and pine. All around you, steep wooded slopes close in like the walls of a green room. Then, in the middle of this quiet Alpine valley, sits a small white palace covered in gold. It looks as if it was lifted out of France and set down by mistake among the mountains.

This is Linderhof Palace, in the Graswang valley, a short drive from the village of Ettal in southern Bavaria. It was built by King Ludwig the Second of Bavaria in the second half of the nineteenth century. Many visitors know him from Neuschwanstein, the castle on the hill. Here is the first surprise. Of Ludwig's three great palaces, Linderhof is the smallest, and it is the only one he saw finished in his lifetime. This was the home he actually lived in.

Before the palace, there was a simpler building here. Ludwig's father, King Maximilian the Second, used this valley for hunting and kept a modest lodge on the land. Young Ludwig knew these woods well. When he became king, he chose this remote spot for a private retreat. The name Linderhof comes from the linden trees, also called lime trees, that grew by the old farmstead here. Look for a large old linden near the palace. It carries the name of the whole estate.

Now look at the facade. It is pale stone, with columns, carved figures, and a crown of gilded decoration. This style is French Rococo, a light and playful form of decoration from eighteenth-century France. Ludwig admired the French kings, especially Louis the Fourteenth, the Sun King, who built Versailles. Linderhof is his tribute to that world. But it is not a copy. It is a dream of Versailles, made small enough for one man.

And one man is the key to this place. Ludwig was shy and disliked public life. He preferred the night to the day, and he often travelled through the mountains after dark in a gilded sleigh. Here is the second surprise. In planning his French-style palaces, Ludwig used a secret code name, Meicost Ettal. It is an anagram of a famous phrase linked to Louis the Fourteenth: "L'état, c'est moi," which means "I am the state." A small private joke, hidden in plain sight.

Inside, the rooms are small but very dense with decoration. Gold leaf covers the walls and ceilings. Mirrors, crystal chandeliers, silk, and porcelain fill every corner. The largest room is the king's bedroom, with a deep blue bed under a heavy canopy. Another room, the Hall of Mirrors, uses facing mirrors to repeat the candlelight again and again, until the small space seems to run on forever.

The dining room holds one of the best stories of the palace. Ludwig did not like servants watching him eat. So his table was built to sink through the floor to the kitchen below. Staff set the food on it, and the table rose back up, fully laid. The idea comes from an old German fairy tale about a magic table that sets itself. Locals say the king often ate alone here, but set places for imagined guests from the court of France.

Step back outside and look at the gardens. They were designed under the court garden director Carl von Effner, and they mix two traditions. Close to the palace, everything is formal and French: clipped hedges, terraces, and flower beds in neat patterns. Further out, the paths soften into the English style, where the landscape looks wild and natural, even though it is carefully planned. The mountains become part of the garden.

In front of the palace is a large pool with a gilded statue group in the centre. On the hour, when the fountain is running, a single jet of water shoots high into the air, far above the palace roof. Behind the palace, a cascade of water steps down the hillside, with a fountain figure of Neptune, the Roman god of the sea, at its foot. On a quiet day you can hear the water from almost every path.

But Linderhof's strangest treasures are hidden in the park. The first is the Venus Grotto, an artificial cave built inside the hillside. Ludwig loved the operas of Richard Wagner. The grotto recreates a scene from Wagner's opera Tannhäuser, about a knight held in the underground realm of Venus. It has an underground lake, a boat shaped like a shell, and painted scenery. Remarkably for its time, it was lit with coloured electric light, powered by an early generator.

Other buildings in the park take you even further from Bavaria. The Moorish Kiosk is a small pavilion with a gilded dome and a glittering interior. Ludwig bought it after it had been shown at a world exhibition in Paris in the 1860s. Nearby is the Moroccan House, another exotic pavilion. And deep in the woods stands Hunding's Hut, a rough timber hall modelled on a scene from Wagner's opera The Valkyrie. Here, the king could step inside a legend.

Ludwig died in 1886, under circumstances that are still debated today. Soon after his death, his palaces were opened to the public. For a king who hid from people, this is a quiet irony. The rooms he built for solitude are now full of visitors from around the world. Restoration work continues across the estate, so some buildings may be closed or covered when you arrive.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the water parterre and turn slowly in a full circle. You will see clipped green hedges, white stone, flashes of gold, and then the dark forest climbing up the slopes. Notice how quiet it is between the falls of water. This is the stillness Ludwig came here to find. As you walk back along the gravel, listen for the fountain one last time... and imagine the valley at night, with only the king awake.
