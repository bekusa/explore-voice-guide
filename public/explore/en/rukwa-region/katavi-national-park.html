The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves through tall yellow grass. Somewhere far off, something heavy grunts... and then another voice answers, deeper and closer. The air smells of dust, warm earth and, faintly, mud. Above you, the sky over western Tanzania is wide and pale. You are in Katavi National Park, one of the most remote protected areas in the whole of East Africa. Very few travellers make it this far. That is a large part of why it feels the way it does.

Let us start with where you are. Katavi lies in the far west of Tanzania, in the country's southwestern highlands. Lake Tanganyika is not far beyond the hills to the west. For many years this land sat inside Rukwa Region. More recently, a new region named Katavi was created from part of Rukwa, and the park took its place there. The nearest town of any size is Mpanda. Closer still is the small village of Sitalike, near the park's main entrance.

The park is large. It is usually counted among the biggest national parks in Tanzania, covering several thousand square kilometres. Yet it receives only a small fraction of the visitors who crowd the northern parks, such as the Serengeti. On many game drives here, you may not see another vehicle for hours. That silence is real. There are no long lines of safari jeeps around a single lion. There is mostly you, your guide, and the animals going about their day.

The park was first protected in the 1970s and later expanded to its present size. Its name comes from a local spirit. According to a tradition of the people who live in this area, a hunter spirit called Katabi lives in a tamarind tree near the shore of Lake Katavi. Locals say people still leave small offerings at the tree, asking for blessings. Treat this as living belief, not a museum story. If your guide points out the tree, a quiet moment of respect is welcome.

Now look at the land around you. Much of Katavi is covered by miombo woodland. Miombo is a type of open forest found across southern and central Africa. Its trees have light, airy crowns, and in the dry season many of them turn copper, red and gold. Between the woodlands lie huge open floodplains. In the rains they become marshes and shallow lakes. In the dry months they bake into flat, cracked grassland that stretches toward the horizon.

The heart of the park is the Katuma River. It runs across the floodplains and links the main seasonal lakes, including Lake Katavi in the north and Lake Chada further south. In the wet season, roughly from November to April, the river spreads wide. Roads flood, and many camps close. But as the dry season goes on, the water shrinks. By the later months of the year, the Katuma may be little more than a chain of muddy pools. And everything that needs water comes to those pools.

This is the first surprise of Katavi — the hippos. As the river dries, hundreds of them can be packed into a single shrinking pool. They lie pressed together, flank against flank, in water barely deep enough to cover them. Space becomes precious. Males yawn to show their long teeth. Fights break out, with loud splashing and roaring. The smell can be strong. It is not pretty, but it is one of the most remarkable wildlife sights in Africa, and few people ever witness it.

The second surprise is hidden in the riverbanks. When the water gets very low, crocodiles here do something unusual. Many of them retreat into caves and burrows dug into the soft banks of the river. There they wait out the hottest, driest weeks. Ask your guide to show you these openings. Sometimes you can see a snout or a pale belly in the shadows. It is a reminder that every animal here has its own strategy for surviving the long dry season.

Katavi is also known for its great herds of buffalo. Groups of hundreds, sometimes far more, move across the open plains together. You will hear them before you see them clearly — a low rumble of hooves and a cloud of dust. Where buffalo gather, lions follow. The lions here hunt across the floodplains, and large prides are often reported. Elephants, giraffes, zebras and many kinds of antelope share the same ground, including topi with their glossy, dark-patched coats.

Look up as well as across. The wetlands and riverbanks attract many birds. Fish eagles perch on dead trees and throw back their heads to call. Storks and herons walk slowly through the shallows. In the woodlands, small brightly coloured birds dart between branches. Even if you are not a birdwatcher, keep your ears open. The calls, the buzz of insects and the grunting of hippos together make the soundtrack of this place.

Getting here takes effort, and that shapes the experience. Many visitors arrive by small aircraft onto bush airstrips. Others make a long road journey through western Tanzania. Accommodation is limited to a handful of camps and simpler options near the park entrance. The pace is slow. Days begin early, before the heat, and drives often pause during the middle of the day. Evenings fill with the sound of frogs, insects and, quite often, a distant lion.

A few practical thoughts. The dry season, roughly from June to October, is the usual time to come, when animals gather around the remaining water. Bring neutral clothing, a hat, sunscreen and good binoculars. Tsetse flies can be present in wooded areas, so long sleeves help. And listen to your guide. Buffalo and hippos are dangerous, and the rules about staying in the vehicle exist for good reasons.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Look at the colour of the grass, the shape of the trees on the horizon, the dust hanging in the light. Notice what you can hear when the engine is off. Katavi does not perform for visitors. It simply continues, season after season, as the river rises and falls. As you leave the floodplain, look back once. Whatever you saw here today, very few people have ever seen it in this quiet way.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in the dry season for the largest animal gatherings.
Pack binoculars to scan the wide open floodplains.
Wear long neutral sleeves to deter tsetse flies.
Start game drives early before the midday heat builds.
Book flights or road transfers well ahead given limited options.
