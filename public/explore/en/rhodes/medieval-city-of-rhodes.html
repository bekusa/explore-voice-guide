The guide

The story

Listen to your footsteps for a moment. Under your shoes, thousands of small sea pebbles are set on edge, black and white, pressed tight into the ground. Locals call this pebble paving chochlaki. It is uneven, a little slippery when wet, and it clicks softly underfoot. Above you, honey-coloured stone walls rise on every side. Swallows cut across the narrow strip of sky. Somewhere nearby, a café radio plays, and a moped hums down a lane too narrow for cars. You are standing inside one of the oldest inhabited medieval towns in Europe.

This is the Medieval City of Rhodes, often simply called the Old Town. It is not a museum built for tourists. People live here. They hang laundry from wooden balconies, argue over football, and sell bread from small shop windows. That mix of daily life and deep history is the reason UNESCO added the town to its World Heritage list in 1988. Most of what you see around you took shape under one unusual group of rulers... a religious order of knights.

They were the Knights Hospitaller, also known as the Knights of Saint John. The order began in Jerusalem, caring for sick and poor pilgrims. Over time it became a military force as well. After the crusader states in the Holy Land fell, the knights needed a new home. In the early fourteenth century, they took Rhodes. For a little over two hundred years, this island became their fortress, their hospital, and their capital.

The knights came from across Christian Europe, and they organised themselves by language. Each group was called a tongue. There was a tongue of France, of Provence, of Auvergne, of Italy, of Spain, of England, and of Germany. Each tongue had its own inn, a kind of headquarters and dining hall. If you walk up the Street of the Knights, you can still see several of these inns, standing shoulder to shoulder along the slope.

That street is worth a slow walk. It runs straight and steep, paved in grey stone, and it feels very different from the twisting lanes elsewhere. The façades are severe and plain. Look up and you will see carved coats of arms set into the walls — shields, crosses, lions, lilies. Each one marks a knight or a commander who paid for the building. Here is a detail many visitors miss: the street looks more complete today than it did a century ago. Much of it was carefully restored in the twentieth century.

That restoration leads to the first big surprise. At the top of the street stands the Palace of the Grand Master, the leader of the order. It looks like a perfect medieval castle, with round towers and heavy walls. But in the mid nineteenth century, a huge explosion of stored gunpowder destroyed much of it. What you see now is largely a reconstruction. It was built in the 1930s, when Italy ruled the island. The Italian authorities rebuilt it with grand halls, and they filled the floors with ancient mosaics brought from the nearby island of Kos.

So when you step inside the palace, you are looking at three layers at once. There is the medieval plan of the knights. There is the Italian vision of what a knight's palace should look like. And there are Roman-era mosaics, much older than either. It is a little like a stage set built on real foundations. Knowing this does not make the building less interesting. It makes it more honest about how cities remember their past.

Now think about the walls that wrap around you. The knights spent enormous effort on them. Over the years, they made them thicker and lower, with wide moats and angled bastions. This was a direct response to a new weapon: gunpowder artillery. High, thin medieval walls could not survive cannon fire. So the knights built a wall system designed for a new age of war. Walking the dry moat today, you may still notice old stone cannonballs lying in the grass.

Those defences were tested twice by the Ottoman Empire. In 1480, an Ottoman force besieged the city and failed to take it. Then, in 1522, Sultan Suleiman, known in Europe as Suleiman the Magnificent, arrived with a much larger army. The siege lasted for months. Finally the knights agreed to surrender, and Suleiman allowed them to leave with honour. Here is the second surprise: their story did not end here. The order later settled on the island of Malta, where they became known as the Knights of Malta.

After 1522, the town changed character. For almost four hundred years, it was an Ottoman city. Churches were turned into mosques. Minarets rose above the rooftops. Wooden balconies, small fountains, and a Turkish bath appeared in the lanes. Look for the Mosque of Suleiman, painted a soft pink, near the top of Sokratous Street. Its slender minaret is one of the clearest signs of this long chapter, which many visitors pass by without a second glance.

Walk east, toward the lower part of town, and you reach the old Jewish quarter. Jewish families lived in Rhodes for many centuries. Their community ended in tragedy during the Second World War. In 1944, most of the island's Jews were deported to Auschwitz, and very few came back. Today, the Kahal Shalom synagogue still stands in a quiet lane, and a small memorial square nearby remembers those who were lost. It is a place to slow down and lower your voice.

The modern life of the town gathers around Sokratous Street and Hippocrates Square. Here you will find shop owners calling out about sponges, leather sandals, and ceramics. There are fountains, café tables, and the smell of grilled meat and strong coffee in the evening. Then, just one turn away, a side lane goes completely silent. Stone arches span the alley above your head. These arches are not decoration. They help brace the buildings against earthquakes, a real risk on this island.

Before you move on, stop and look again at what is directly in front of you. Notice the colour of the stone, how it turns gold in late light and grey in the shade. Notice the pebbles under your feet, set by hand, one by one. Knights, sultans, Italian architects, and ordinary Rhodian families have all walked this ground. As you leave, listen for the next sound around the corner... a door closing, a bell, a voice calling from a balcony. The town is still being lived in, one quiet afternoon at a time.

Practical tips
