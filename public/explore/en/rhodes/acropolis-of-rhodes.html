The guide

The story

Listen first. Cicadas fill the pine trees, wind moves over the dry grass, and somewhere below, a scooter climbs the road. The ground under your feet is pale limestone, warm, dusty, and polished smooth in places by centuries of walking. The light here is bright and white. Ahead of you, a few honey-coloured columns stand against the sky. This is the Acropolis of Rhodes, on the hill that locals call Monte Smith. It does not look like the fortress you may expect.

That is the first surprise. Most ancient Greek acropolises were citadels, high rocks with walls, built for the last stand of a city under attack. The one at Lindos, down the coast, is exactly that. This hill is different. It was never a true fortress. It was laid out as a broad, open landscape of terraces, temples, gardens, and places for sport. Think of it less as a castle and more as a monumental public park.

To understand why, go back to the late fifth century before Christ. Three older cities on the island — Ialysos, Kamiros, and Lindos — agreed to join together and found one shared capital at the northern tip. Ancient sources date this union to 408 BCE. The new city of Rhodes was planned from the start, not grown by accident. It had a regular grid of streets, a style of planning often linked to the Greek architect Hippodamus of Miletus, although historians debate his personal role here.

The new city grew fast. Its harbours sat on the sea route between Greece, Egypt, and the eastern Mediterranean, so trade brought wealth, and wealth brought ambition. Over the next two centuries, in the period historians call Hellenistic — the age after Alexander the Great — Rhodes became one of the great naval and commercial powers of the Greek world. This hill was where the city displayed its confidence, in stone, on terraces that looked down over streets and harbours.

Now look at those columns. They belong to the Temple of Pythian Apollo, the god of light, music, and prophecy. What you see is partly ancient and partly modern. In the early twentieth century, Italy controlled the Dodecanese islands, and Italian archaeologists excavated this hill and reconstructed parts of the temple. Look closely and you can often tell the difference. Some blocks are weathered and grey. Others are cleaner, flatter, and clearly newer.

This mix matters. The Italian restorations follow the methods of their time, and modern archaeologists might choose differently today. Some purists find them too heavy. Others are simply grateful. Without them, a visitor would see little more than foundations in the grass. As you stand here, it helps to hold both thoughts at once. You are looking at the ancient past, and you are also looking at how a later generation imagined it.

Below the temple, follow the slope down with your eyes. A long, narrow track stretches out, lined with rows of stone seats. This is the stadium, where athletes raced on foot. Greek stadiums were built to a standard length of roughly two hundred metres, and this one follows that pattern. Much of the seating you see was rebuilt by the Italians. Still, the shape is ancient. The ground has held this long, flat line for more than two thousand years.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. At one end of the stadium, look for the remains of the starting area. Greek runners began from a line of stone slabs, often cut with grooves for their feet. It is a small, very human thing. Imagine bare feet pressed into those grooves, a crowd on the slopes, and a signal to begin. Then the dust, the noise, and a short, hard sprint toward the far end.

Beside the stadium sits a small theatre. It is compact, white, and almost perfectly semicircular. Much of it is also a modern reconstruction. Scholars are not certain whether it was used for drama, music, or perhaps teaching and public speeches. Rhodes was famous in the ancient world for its schools of rhetoric — the art of persuasive speaking. Students came from far away to train here, and some later Roman politicians studied on the island.

Nearby, the foundations of a large gymnasium spread across the flat ground. In a Greek city, the gymnasium was much more than a place for exercise. Young men trained their bodies there, but they also studied, argued, and listened to teachers. Sport, learning, and civic life sat side by side. That is why the stadium, the theatre, and the gymnasium cluster together on this hill. They were parts of one idea about how a citizen should grow.

Now for the second surprise, which needs a short walk. Higher up the hill, look for openings cut into the rock. These are the remains of nymphaea — artificial grottoes dedicated to the nymphs, the nature spirits of springs and trees. They were shaded, cool, and planted with greenery. In the heat of a Rhodian summer, they must have felt like a different world. Few visitors find them, so take your time if you do.

Finally, a word about the name. Monte Smith comes from a British admiral, Sir Sidney Smith, who is said to have used this hill as a lookout during the wars against Napoleon at the turn of the nineteenth century. From up here you can see why. The ground drops away to the sea, and the view runs far along the coast. The temples of Athena and Zeus once stood nearby too, though little of them survives today.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the temple steps. Feel the warm stone, and listen again to the cicadas and the wind in the pines. Look down toward the modern town, where the ancient street grid still lies under the roads. When you walk back down, notice the pale dust on your shoes. It is the same limestone the city's builders cut, carried, and shaped... all those centuries ago.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in the early morning to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Bring water and a hat; shade is limited on the hill.
Wear shoes with good grip on the smooth limestone paths.
Stay for sunset views over the western coastline.
Combine the visit with a walk down into the medieval Old Town.
