The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you walk any further. The air changes here. It turns cool and damp, and it carries the smell of wet limestone and moss. Somewhere ahead, water drips onto stone in a slow, uneven rhythm. Then you see the entrance, a vast mouth cut into the cliff. It is so large that the trees outside look small against it. This is Devetashka Cave, just above the village of Devetaki in north-central Bulgaria.

The cave sits in karst country. Karst is a landscape of soluble rock, usually limestone, that water slowly dissolves over very long periods of time. Rain seeps into cracks, widens them, and carves out channels and chambers underground. The Osam River runs through this valley, and the whole region is full of caves, springs and gorges. Devetashka is one of the largest. Its scale comes from water patiently removing rock, one drop at a time, for an almost unimaginable stretch of years.

As you step inside, let your eyes adjust. Most caves grow darker the deeper you go. This one does something different. High above the main hall, the ceiling is open in several places. These are natural holes in the rock, and daylight falls through them in wide columns. On a sunny day you can watch the beams move slowly across the floor. Dust and moisture in the air make the light almost solid... like something you could touch.

Below those openings, look at the ground. Where the light reaches, plants grow. Moss, ferns and small green shoots cover rocks that would otherwise stay bare and grey. It is a small garden inside a cave. Step a few metres away from the light, and the green disappears. The difference is sharp and easy to see. It is a simple lesson in how much life depends on sunlight, written directly onto the cave floor.

Now for the part that surprises most visitors. People have lived here for an extraordinarily long time. Archaeological excavations in the mid-twentieth century found traces of human presence reaching back to the Palaeolithic, the Old Stone Age. Stone tools from that era suggest that early hunters used this shelter tens of thousands of years ago. Later layers show settlement in the Neolithic and the Copper Age, when farming communities lived here. Few places let you stand where so many generations stood before you.

It is not hard to understand why. Think about what this cave offered to people without buildings. The huge entrance gave protection from rain and snow. The openings in the roof let in light and let smoke escape from fires. Fresh water ran close by. The valley outside held game, fish and fertile ground. For a community thousands of years ago, this was close to an ideal home — a roof, a hearth and a pantry, all provided by the landscape itself.

Today the cave has other residents. Devetashka is an important home for bats. Several species shelter here, and in some seasons their numbers reach into the thousands. They hang in the higher, darker parts of the cave, far from the light. At dusk, they leave to hunt insects over the valley. If you visit near sunset, stay quiet and watch the entrance. You may see them pour out into the evening air.

That is why the cave is a protected natural site. Bats are sensitive to noise, light and disturbance, especially when they breed or hibernate. Their presence here also made headlines. In the early 2010s, scenes for the Hollywood action film The Expendables 2 were shot inside the cave. Conservation groups in Bulgaria protested strongly. They argued that the filming disturbed the bat colonies. The debate became a national conversation about how to balance tourism, film work and the protection of wild places.

Walk deeper, and the sound of the space becomes part of the experience. Your footsteps echo. Voices travel further than you expect. The dripping water is louder in some corners and almost silent in others. Underfoot, the ground can be uneven, muddy or slippery, so move slowly. Look at the walls closely. The limestone has layers, streaks of colour and shapes formed by flowing water. In places you can find stalagmites rising from the floor, built drop by drop from minerals.

The cave is not only a single room. Beyond the main hall, passages and smaller chambers lead further into the hill. Water moves through parts of this system, and in wetter seasons it can be more visible. Most of these deeper sections are not open to casual visitors, and that is a good thing. They protect the bats and the delicate formations. What you see from the main hall is the public face of a much larger and quieter world underground.

Outside, the surrounding landscape deserves your time too. The village of Devetaki is small and rural, with fields, orchards and quiet roads. The nearby town of Lovech is the regional centre. A short drive away, the Krushuna Falls run over limestone terraces into clear blue-green pools, formed by the same karst processes that shaped this cave. Many visitors combine the two places in one day. Together, they show both sides of this landscape: what water builds and what water removes.

Before you leave the main hall, choose a spot near one of the light columns and simply stand still. Look up at the opening, then down at the green patch it feeds. Notice how the temperature feels on your skin. Notice the scale of the ceiling above you. It is easy to rush through a place like this, taking photographs. It is better to give it a few minutes of silence. The cave rewards patience more than speed.

As you walk back towards the entrance, the daylight grows stronger and the air slowly warms. Turn around once, just before you step outside. Look back into the dark arch and the faint beams of light inside it. People sheltered here when the world looked very different. Bats will fly out of it again tonight. And the water will keep dripping, slowly widening the stone, long after today's visitors have gone home.
