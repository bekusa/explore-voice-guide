The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere above you, water drips onto stone, and each drop lands with a small, clear note. The air is cool on your skin, even when the day outside is warm. Under your feet the ground is uneven: packed earth, loose gravel, the odd smooth rock. Now lift your head. High in the ceiling, two pale openings look straight back at you. They are almost perfectly paired, like a face watching from the dark.

This is Prohodna, one of the most photographed caves in Bulgaria. The name comes from a Bulgarian word for passing through, and that is exactly what the cave is. It is a tunnel open at both ends, so you can walk in one side and out the other. Before we go further, here is one honest note on place. Prohodna sits in the Iskar Gorge, near the village of Karlukovo in northern Bulgaria. That is a long way from Bosnek and the slopes of Vitosha, which have caves of their own.

So what made this tunnel? The answer is water and time. The rock around you is limestone, a soft stone that slowly dissolves when rainwater seeps through it. Over a very long span of time, water found cracks in the hillside and widened them. Small channels became passages, and passages became chambers. The result is what geologists call karst, a landscape of caves, sinkholes and hollow ground. The country around Karlukovo is full of it, with many caves scattered across the hills.

Now, the two openings above you. Locals call them the Eyes of God. They are holes in the cave roof where the rock has given way, and they let daylight fall in two separate beams. On a bright day you can watch those beams move slowly across the floor as the sun crosses the sky. The shape is natural, but the likeness to a face is strong. Most visitors stop here, go quiet, and simply stare upward for a while.

Here is a detail many people miss. When it rains, water runs down through the eyes and drips to the floor below. From the right spot, the falling water looks like tears running from the face in the ceiling. Locals have a soft way of speaking about it. They say the cave is weeping. It is a poetic image, and a reminder that the sky is never far away in here. If the weather turns, you may see it for yourself.

The cave has two mouths, and they are very different. One entrance is huge, a tall arch that you can see from far down the slope. The other is smaller and lower. Walk the full length and you feel the space change around you. It opens and narrows, brightens and dims. The passage is not deep enough to be fully dark, so you never lose the daylight completely. That half-light is a large part of the mood.

Look closely at the walls and floor. The limestone carries layers, lines that show how the rock was laid down long before the cave existed. In places the surface is smooth and rounded, polished by water. In other places it is broken and sharp where blocks have fallen. Lichens and moss grow near the openings, where light and moisture meet. Further from the light, the stone turns grey and bare. Each zone has its own colour and texture.

People have been coming to this gorge for a very long time. Caves in the Karlukovo area have attracted archaeologists, because shelters like these were useful to people in prehistoric times. I will not give you dates or claims about exact finds here, because the details deserve a proper source. But it is easy to imagine the appeal. A dry roof, running water nearby, and a clear view down the valley. Those things mattered to people long before anyone came for photographs.

Today the cave draws a very different crowd. It sits inside a protected area known as the Iskar-Panega Geopark, which covers this stretch of karst landscape. Climbers come for the rock. Photographers come for the eyes. And the cave has become known for adventure sports, including bungee jumps from the high ceiling. On a busy weekend you may hear voices echo from the far end, or the clink of climbing gear. On a quiet weekday you may have it almost to yourself.

Sound behaves strangely in here. Speak at normal volume and your voice comes back to you, softened and stretched. That is why the cave has hosted concerts and performances over the years. The stone walls and high roof act like a natural hall. Try it gently. Hum a single note and listen to how long it lingers. Then stop and listen to what remains: dripping water, wind at the entrance, perhaps a bird calling outside.

Step back toward one of the openings and look out. The Iskar River has cut its way through these hills, and its gorge is one of the great natural corridors of Bulgaria. A railway follows the valley, and sometimes you can hear a train far below. Around you, the slopes are dry and rocky, with low trees and pale stone showing through. It is a landscape shaped by water from above and below, and the cave is simply its most dramatic room.

A word on footing. The floor is natural, so it can be slippery after rain, and loose stones roll easily. Take your time, especially on the slopes near each entrance. Children love this place, but keep them close near the steeper sections. And remember that this is a living cave in a protected area. Bats and other small creatures may use the quieter corners. Leave the stone as you found it, so the next visitor sees what you saw.

Before you leave, find one last spot to stand. Choose a place where both eyes are in view. Notice where the light falls right now, and how it will be somewhere else within the hour. Listen once more for the drip of water. Feel the cool air against your face, and the warmer air waiting at the entrance. Then walk out slowly, and turn back once. From outside, the arch looks like a doorway into the hill, and in a way, that is exactly what it is.
