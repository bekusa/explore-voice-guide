The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the wide stone square. The heat rises off the paving, and somewhere nearby a bicycle bell rings. In front of you is a long, pale building with tall shuttered windows and a small domed tower on the roof. The shutters are painted a deep green. Behind you, voices drift across the open space, laughter and the scrape of wheels. This is the Jakarta History Museum, also known as the Fatahillah Museum. It is the old heart of a city that once had another name entirely.

That name was Batavia. In the early seventeenth century, the Dutch East India Company, often called the VOC, took control of this stretch of coast. In 1619, forces under Jan Pieterszoon Coen destroyed the port town of Jayakarta and began building a fortified trading city in its place. Batavia became the Asian headquarters of a company that traded in spices, textiles and people. Much of what happened in that network passed through the building in front of you.

This building was the Stadhuis, the city hall of Batavia. The version you see now was completed around 1710, replacing earlier, simpler halls on the same site. It was far more than an office. The city council met here. Courts sat here. Marriages were registered here. And beneath your feet, prisoners waited in the dark. For about two centuries, power in Batavia looked out from these windows onto the square.

Look at the shape of the building. It is symmetrical, with a central section and two wings stretching left and right. The small domed tower sits exactly in the middle of the roof. Many visitors say it echoes the old town hall on Dam Square in Amsterdam, and the resemblance is easy to see in the proportions. The builders brought a European civic style to a tropical coast. Notice how the thick walls and high ceilings were a practical answer to the heat.

The square has its own story, and it is not a gentle one. In Batavia's time, this open space was where the city gathered for announcements, markets and public punishment. Executions took place here, in full view of the city hall. Today children chase pigeons across the same ground. It is worth holding both pictures in your mind at once. The square was a stage for authority, and the audience had little choice but to watch.

Now, the name Fatahillah. It honours a sixteenth-century commander who captured the port of Sunda Kelapa from the Portuguese in 1527. Tradition holds that he renamed it Jayakarta, often translated as complete victory. That name later shaped the word Jakarta. So the square carries the name of a local hero, while the building in its centre was built by a colonial power. That contrast is part of the point.

After Indonesian independence, the building served various government uses. In 1974 it opened as a museum dedicated to the history of Jakarta. Inside, the rooms move through the city's long past. There are maps of Batavia's canals, heavy carved furniture from the colonial era, ceramics, weapons and paintings. Some rooms focus on the earliest settlements along this coast, long before any European ships arrived. Take your time with the old maps. They show a city laid out in neat grids, crossed by canals.

Those canals are a surprise for many visitors. The Dutch designed Batavia to look like a city back home, with waterways running between the streets. In the humid lowlands, still water became a serious problem. Disease spread, and Batavia gained a grim reputation as one of the unhealthiest posts in the company's empire. Over time, wealthier residents moved south to higher ground. The old town slowly lost its place as the centre of power.

Here is the part most people remember. Below the building are the old prison cells. They are low, cramped and damp, and in the past they could flood. Prisoners were held here in crowded conditions while awaiting trial or punishment. The Javanese prince Diponegoro, who led a long war against Dutch rule, is said to have been held here in 1830 before his exile. If the cells are open during your visit, stoop and look inside. The air alone tells you a great deal.

When you step back outside, look for the old bronze cannon known as Si Jagur. It is a favourite with locals. At one end, the cannon is shaped like a clenched fist, with the thumb tucked between two fingers. Locals say the cannon once carried a fertility legend, and couples came to it hoping for children. Treat that as folklore, not history. But it shows how ordinary people gave their own meanings to an object made for war.

Around the square, the old town of Kota Tua stretches in every direction. Other colonial buildings face the open space, several of them now museums or cafés. Visitors rent brightly painted bicycles, sometimes with wide-brimmed hats to match, and ride slow loops across the paving. Street performers stand frozen in costume, waiting for a coin and a photograph. The atmosphere is relaxed, a little playful. It is very different from the strict, ordered city that once ran its business here.

The building has been restored more than once, and you can see the care in its details. The shutters, the wooden doors and the tiled roof all follow the old style. Yet the walls also carry traces of age and repair, especially low down where damp has worked into the plaster. Jakarta today is a city of towers and highways. This one low, pale building holds a memory of how the city began, and of who paid the price.

Before you leave, stand once more in the middle of the square and face the museum. Look up at the small tower on the roof, and then at the green shutters below. Listen to the bicycles, the vendors, the pigeons lifting off the stones. Once, this was a place of orders and verdicts. Now it is a place where families sit in the shade and talk. As you walk away, notice how the old town quietly gives way to the modern city around you.
