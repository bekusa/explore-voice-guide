The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beneath the traffic of South Jakarta, there is another sound here. Birds call from high in the canopy. Leaves move in the warm air. The ground under your feet is shaded and slightly damp, and the light comes down green through old trees. You are standing inside one of the largest green spaces in Indonesia's capital. This is Ragunan Zoo, known locally as Taman Margasatwa Ragunan. For many Jakartans, it is less a zoo and more a weekend forest.

The story of this place begins somewhere else entirely. The zoo first opened in the nineteenth century, in the central district of Cikini. Back then it was called the Planten en Dierentuin, a Dutch name meaning plant and animal garden. Indonesia was a Dutch colony at the time, and Jakarta was known as Batavia. The garden was a place for the colonial elite to stroll, to look at exotic plants, and to see animals from across the archipelago.

Here is a detail most visitors never hear. The land in Cikini was donated by Raden Saleh, a Javanese painter. He is often called the father of modern Indonesian painting. He spent many years in Europe, painted for royal courts, and came home with grand ideas. His gift of land helped create the first zoo in the city. So the roots of Ragunan go back to an artist, not a scientist.

As Jakarta grew after independence, the old garden in Cikini became too small. The city decided to move the animals south, to the quiet area of Ragunan, in the district of Pasar Minggu. The new zoo opened here in the 1960s. And the old site did not disappear. It became Taman Ismail Marzuki, one of Jakarta's main arts centres, with theatres, galleries, and a planetarium. Raden Saleh's land still serves culture today... just in a different form.

Now look around you. The zoo covers well over a hundred hectares, and much of that space is trees. Wide paths run between enclosures, lakes, and open lawns. On a weekday morning it can feel almost empty. On a public holiday it feels like half the city has arrived. Families spread mats on the grass. Children run between food stalls. Groups of friends share rice and fried snacks under the shade. This picnic culture is a big part of what Ragunan means to local people.

The animals here come mostly from Indonesia itself, and that is the real strength of the collection. Indonesia is an archipelago of thousands of islands, and many species live nowhere else on Earth. Here you can see animals from Sumatra, Borneo, Sulawesi, Java, and beyond. Look for the Sumatran tiger, the smallest of the surviving tiger subspecies, with darker stripes set close together. It is critically endangered in the wild, so every one you see here carries real weight.

Then there is the Komodo dragon, the largest living lizard in the world. In the wild it lives only on a few islands in eastern Indonesia. Watch how it moves. It is slow and heavy most of the time, its tongue flicking out to taste the air. But this animal can move quickly when it wants to. Standing in front of it, you begin to understand why sailors once told stories about dragons in these islands.

If you have time, search for the anoa. It is a small wild buffalo from the island of Sulawesi. Many visitors walk right past it, because it looks like a modest, compact cow. But it is a rare animal, and it is shy by nature. There are also tapirs, with their long, flexible snouts, and a wide range of tropical birds. Some of the birds have colours so bright they look painted.

One of the most important parts of the zoo is the Schmutzer Primate Center. It sits inside the grounds but works almost like its own park. It was created with support from a private legacy, and it focuses on apes and monkeys. Inside, you can see orangutans, gibbons, and gorillas in large, planted spaces. Walkways and tunnels let you watch them at different heights. The design tries to show the animals in a setting closer to the forest than to a cage.

The orangutans deserve a long pause. Their name comes from the Malay words for person of the forest. Watch their hands. They hold food with care, test branches before trusting their weight, and sometimes seem to study the visitors as closely as the visitors study them. Wild orangutans live only on Sumatra and Borneo, and their forests continue to shrink. Here, the conversation about conservation stops being abstract.

Ragunan is also a living part of the city's ecosystem. The tall trees give shade and clean air to a dense part of Jakarta. Wild birds and squirrels move freely through the branches, and they are not part of any exhibit. Long-tailed macaques sometimes appear too. Keep your snacks closed and your bags zipped, because these monkeys are quick, curious, and very good at taking things.

Like many older zoos around the world, Ragunan has faced criticism over the years. Some enclosures are old, and animal welfare groups have pushed for improvements. The zoo has slowly modernised parts of the grounds, and the primate centre shows the newer approach. As you walk, you may notice the contrast between older concrete spaces and greener, more open areas. That contrast is part of the honest story of the place.

Before you leave, find a quiet bench under the trees. Listen again. The birds are still calling. A family nearby is probably laughing over lunch. A child is pointing at something in the branches. Somewhere behind the leaves, a gibbon may start its long, rising song. As you walk back toward the gates, notice how the air changes... warmer, louder, more city. And remember that this patch of forest began, long ago, with a painter's gift of land.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come on a weekday morning to avoid crowds.
Avoid public holidays when local families fill the grounds.
Bring a mat and enjoy a picnic under trees.
Keep food sealed around the free-roaming macaques.
Check whether the primate centre needs a separate ticket.
