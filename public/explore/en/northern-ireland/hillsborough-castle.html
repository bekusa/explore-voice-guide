The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel shifts under your shoes, rooks call from the old trees, and somewhere beyond the hedges a lawn mower hums. The air in County Down often carries the smell of wet grass and cut leaves. In front of you stands a long, pale Georgian house. It is calm and orderly, and it looks more like a country home than a fortress. Welcome to Hillsborough Castle, a house that has hosted royalty, governors, and some of the most important conversations in modern Irish history.

First, the name. Despite the word castle, there are no battlements or arrow slits here. The house is a grand Georgian mansion, built in the eighteenth century and reshaped in the decades that followed. Georgian simply means the style of the era of the four King Georges. It favours symmetry, tall sash windows, and classical columns. The word castle is a polite title, an echo of the old fort that once guarded this hilltop village.

That fort still exists, a short walk away in the village. In the seventeenth century, the Hill family came to this part of Ulster and fortified the site. The village took their name, Hillsborough, and so did the house. Over the generations, the Hills rose in rank and influence. Their wealth came from land, and this estate became the centre of their world. The streets you walked to get here grew up around the family's ambitions.

One member of the family deserves a mention. Wills Hill, who became the first Marquess of Downshire, served the British government in the eighteenth century. He held responsibility for the American colonies in the years leading up to the American Revolution. Historians generally judge his policies as harsh, and they did little to calm the colonists. So here is a surprising link. A man from this quiet Ulster estate played a part in the story that ended with American independence.

In the twentieth century, the house began a new life. After the partition of Ireland in the early 1920s, the new government of Northern Ireland needed a residence for its Governor, who represented the Crown. The Hill family sold the estate, and from the mid 1920s the Governors lived here. The office of Governor ended in 1973. Since then, the house has served as the official residence of the Secretary of State for Northern Ireland, a senior minister in the British government.

It is also a royal residence. When members of the Royal Family visit Northern Ireland, this is where they stay and where they hold receptions. Queen Elizabeth the Second came here many times during her long reign. Today the house and gardens are cared for by Historic Royal Palaces, the independent charity that also looks after the Tower of London. That charity took over in 2014 and opened much more of the estate to visitors.

Now, here is the part that most visitors do not expect. This elegant house has been a quiet stage for peace talks. In 1985, the British and Irish governments signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement here. It gave the Irish government a formal advisory role in Northern Ireland's affairs. The agreement was controversial at the time, and many unionists protested against it. But it marked a turning point in how London and Dublin worked together.

The house returned to the headlines again and again. During the long peace process of the 1990s and 2000s, politicians met in these rooms late into the night. In 2003, President George W. Bush and Prime Minister Tony Blair held talks here. And in 2010, local leaders reached the Hillsborough Agreement, which transferred control of policing and justice to the Northern Ireland Assembly. Think of that as you pass through the doors. Ordinary-looking rooms carried very heavy decisions.

Inside, the State Rooms show the formal side of the house. Expect high ceilings, polished floors, large portraits, and heavy curtains framing the tall windows. The Throne Room is used for official ceremonies and receptions. The State Drawing Room feels lighter, with soft colours and comfortable furniture. Guides often point out that these are working rooms, not frozen displays. Dinners, award ceremonies, and official meetings still take place here, so the layout can change from visit to visit.

Step outside again, and the gardens open up around you. They cover a large area of lawns, woodland, walled garden, and water. Paths lead you under tall old trees and along quiet walks. The Yew Tree Walk is a dark green corridor of clipped and ancient yews. The planting changes with the seasons, from spring bulbs to deep autumn colour. Here, the house feels less like a seat of government and more like a family home.

Look for Lady Alice's Temple, a small classical building beside the water. It is named after a daughter of the Hill family. It is a romantic garden folly, which means a decorative building made for pleasure and views rather than practical use. The walled garden is a different mood entirely. Here, gardeners grow fruit, vegetables, and flowers, and much of the produce supplies the house. Notice the warm brick walls, which hold the heat of the sun for the plants.

When you leave the estate, take time for the village of Hillsborough itself. It is one of the most handsome villages in Northern Ireland, with Georgian terraces climbing the hill. The parish church, with its tall spire, stands close by. The old fort sits in its own parkland near the lake. Small cafes and pubs line the main street. The village and the house grew together, and walking between them helps you understand the whole place.

Before you go, pause one last time and look back at the house. Notice how its long front faces the lawns with a kind of quiet confidence. It was built for a powerful family, then it served a new government, and then it became a meeting place for rivals. Listen to the rooks again and feel the gravel under your feet. Many visitors walk past this facade without knowing how much it has witnessed. Now, you know.

Practical tips

Book your State Rooms tour ahead, as numbers are limited.
Wear sturdy shoes for the gravel paths and garden walks.
Allow extra time to walk through Hillsborough village afterwards.
Check for closures, since official events still happen here.
Bring a light rain jacket, as County Down weather changes quickly.
