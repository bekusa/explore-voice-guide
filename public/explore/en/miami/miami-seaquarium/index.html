The guide

The story

Listen first. Salt wind comes off Biscayne Bay, and palm fronds rattle in it like dry paper. Somewhere a gull complains. The air smells of warm concrete, sunscreen, and the faint mineral tang of sea water pumped through tanks. You are on Virginia Key, a low barrier island between the mainland and Key Biscayne. Across the water, the towers of downtown Miami rise in a pale line. This is the Miami Seaquarium, one of the oldest marine parks in the United States, and one of the most argued about.

The Seaquarium opened in 1955. Florida was changing fast in those years. Air conditioning, new highways, and cheap flights were turning the state into a winter playground for the rest of the country. Roadside attractions competed for every family car heading south. A large marine park, with live dolphins and sharks you could watch up close, was a bold idea for its time. For many visitors, it was the first time they had seen a dolphin anywhere except a book.

The island itself carries an older story. Before the causeway was built, Virginia Key was reached mostly by boat. During segregation, a beach on this island was set aside for Black residents of Miami, because they were barred from most other beaches in the area. That beach still exists today as a historic park, just a short distance from here. So the ground under your feet belongs to a longer story about who was allowed to enjoy the sea, and who was not.

The road you probably arrived on is the Rickenbacker Causeway. It links the mainland to Virginia Key and Key Biscayne. Driving across it, you rise over open water, and the bay spreads out on both sides. On a clear day, the water turns from deep blue to a milky green over the shallows. That drive was part of the original appeal. Families left the city behind in a few minutes and arrived somewhere that felt like an island holiday.

Now for a surprise many visitors miss. In the 1960s, this park became a television set. The film Flipper, and the television series that followed, used the Seaquarium for filming. Flipper was a bottlenose dolphin who helped a park ranger's family solve problems along the Florida coast. Several different dolphins played the role. The show made dolphins into household favourites across America. It also helped create the huge public appetite for dolphin shows that marine parks would serve for decades.

Look around at the structures. Much of the park was built in a mid-century style. You will see curved concrete, open-air stadiums, and wide walkways designed for crowds and sunshine. The large dome near the entrance area, often called the Golden Dome, hosted sea lion performances. These buildings were made for a Florida before smartphones, when a day out meant sitting on a bench in the heat and watching animals do tricks. Salt, sun, and hurricanes have been hard on them over the years.

At the heart of the Seaquarium's story is one animal. Her name was Lolita to the park, and Tokitae to many of her supporters. The Lummi Nation of the Pacific Northwest also gave her a name of their own. She was a killer whale, also called an orca, captured as a young calf in 1970 in the waters of Puget Sound, near Seattle. She was brought here, thousands of kilometres from her family, and she lived in this park for more than five decades.

For years she shared her tank with a male orca named Hugo. After he died, she lived without another orca. Her pool, in the Whale Stadium, became the centre of a long protest. Critics said it was far too small for an animal of her size. They noted she could barely swim more than a few body lengths in any direction. Supporters of the park pointed to her long life and her care. The argument became one of the best known animal welfare campaigns in the world.

Here is the second surprise. In 2023, after a change of ownership, the park and a group of partners announced a plan to move her back to the Pacific Northwest. The idea was a protected sea pen, in the waters where her family still swims. Many people believed she would finally go home. But in August 2023, before the move could happen, she died here in Miami. She was in her late fifties, which is old for an orca in any setting.

Her death changed the mood around this place. Questions about the park's care, its buildings, and its future grew louder. Miami-Dade County, which owns the land, moved to end the lease with the operator. Legal disputes followed, and the park's status has been uncertain since. Depending on when you are standing here, the gates may be open, closed, or in the middle of a new chapter. It is worth knowing that this story is still being written.

There is another side to the Seaquarium that deserves mention. For decades, the park took part in rescuing injured manatees, sea turtles, and other animals from Florida waters. Manatees are slow, gentle mammals sometimes called sea cows. In Florida they are often hit by boat propellers or harmed by cold water and polluted bays. Some rescued animals were treated here and later released. That work is part of the record too, alongside the shows and the controversy.

Whatever you think about keeping large marine animals in tanks, this place shows how public attitudes have shifted. In 1955, a dolphin jumping through a hoop felt like pure wonder. Today, many visitors stand at the same rail and ask harder questions. Was the animal happy? Did it have enough space? Where did it come from? The concrete has not changed much. The people looking at it have changed a great deal.

Before you leave, turn toward the bay. Watch the water beyond the fences. On a calm day, you may see a pelican fold its wings and drop into the sea. You might even spot a wild dolphin or a manatee rolling near the surface. They move freely here, with the whole of Biscayne Bay around them. As you walk back toward the causeway, let that open water be the last thing you look at... and carry the question this place leaves behind.
