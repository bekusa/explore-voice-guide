The guide

The story

Listen to your footsteps first. Inside the great hall, the floor does not click or echo the way polished stone does. It gives a soft, dry hush. That is because the wooden floorboards are covered with a thin layer of fine sand. Light falls in from tall windows on every side, and above you hang brass chandeliers, still fitted for candles. This is the Portuguese Synagogue of Amsterdam, and much of what you see has stayed close to its original form for more than three centuries.

The building was inaugurated in 1675. It was the spiritual home of the Sephardic Jews of Amsterdam. Sephardic means Jews whose families came from Spain and Portugal. Many of them, or their parents and grandparents, had left the Iberian Peninsula to escape the Inquisition. Some had lived for generations as outward Christians and kept their faith in secret. In the Dutch Republic, they found a place where they could return openly to Judaism.

Amsterdam in the seventeenth century was not a perfect haven, but it was unusual. Trade mattered more to the city than religious conformity. The Portuguese Jewish merchants brought networks that reached Lisbon, Brazil, the Mediterranean and beyond. As the community grew wealthy and confident, it wanted a house of prayer to match. The result was one of the largest synagogues in the world at the time, built in full view of the city rather than hidden in a back courtyard.

The architect was Elias Bouman, a Dutch builder. Look at the exterior and you will see a calm, rectangular block of brick, with long rows of large windows and a strong, simple outline. It is Dutch classical architecture, restrained and orderly. According to tradition, the design also drew on descriptions of the Temple of Solomon in Jerusalem. That idea shaped the proportions and the sense of a sacred enclosure, set apart from the street.

That enclosure is easy to miss at first. The main hall stands inside a square of lower buildings that wrap around it like a protective wall. These hold offices, meeting rooms, a winter synagogue and a famous library. Walk the courtyard and you feel the noise of traffic and trams drop away. The low ring of buildings works almost like a cloister, the covered walkway around a monastery garden, giving the great hall room to stand alone.

Now step back inside and look at the sand again. Nobody can point to a single documented reason for it. The practical explanation is that sand absorbs dust, moisture and dirt from shoes, and softens the sound of many feet. A more poetic explanation is often told by visitors and guides. The sand recalls the forty years the Israelites wandered in the desert. Some also connect it to the secret prayers of Jews in Iberia, who muffled sound in hidden rooms. Treat those last ideas as tradition, not proven fact.

The light is the second thing people remember. The main sanctuary was designed for daylight from its many windows, and for candlelight after dark. Even today, there is no electric lighting in the great hall. On special evenings, hundreds of candles burn in the brass chandeliers and along the benches. If you are here by day, imagine the room after sunset, with only flame and reflection on the wood and brass.

That wood deserves a close look. At the far end stands the Hechal, the large cabinet that holds the Torah scrolls. In the centre stands the Tevah, the raised platform where the scripture is read. Both are made of dark, richly grained jacaranda wood, a tropical timber brought from Brazil. It is a quiet reminder of how far the trade routes of this community reached. Notice how the reading platform faces the ark across the open floor, so the congregation sits between them.

Raise your eyes to the galleries. These upper levels were for women, following Orthodox custom, which separates men and women during prayer. The wooden benches below still carry the sense of a working room rather than a museum. And that is the next surprise. This is still an active synagogue. The Portuguese Jewish community of Amsterdam continues to hold services here, especially on major holidays, often in the same candlelit way.

One of the most remarkable rooms is not in the great hall at all. In the surrounding buildings is the Ets Haim library. Its roots go back to the early seventeenth century, before this synagogue was even built. It is considered one of the oldest Jewish libraries in the world that is still in use. Its shelves hold manuscripts and printed books that trace the learning, worries and debates of a community rebuilding its identity in a new country.

The twentieth century brought the darkest chapter. During the Nazi occupation of the Netherlands, most of Amsterdam's Jews were deported and murdered. The Portuguese community was devastated. Yet the building itself survived the war largely intact, which was rare. Just across the road, at the square called Jonas Daniël Meijerplein, stands the statue of a dockworker. It honours the February Strike of 1941, when Amsterdam workers walked out in protest against the persecution of their Jewish neighbours.

The synagogue is now part of the Jewish Cultural Quarter, together with the Jewish Museum and other sites nearby. In the basement, treasure chambers display ritual silver, textiles and objects used in worship over the centuries. The streets around here were once the heart of Jewish Amsterdam. The painter Rembrandt lived a short walk away and drew many of his Jewish neighbours. Painters of the seventeenth century, including Emanuel de Witte, also recorded this very interior, with its light falling across the floor.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in the main hall. Look up at the unlit candles, down at the sand, and across to the dark wood of the ark. Then walk back out through the courtyard, and notice the moment the city sound returns. Outside are wide roads, trams and modern buildings. Behind you is a room that has kept the same light, the same floor and the same purpose for more than three hundred years.
