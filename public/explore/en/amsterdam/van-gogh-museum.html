The story

A wide open square spreads out under a gray Dutch sky. People cross it on foot and on bicycles. Along one side stands a building of glass and pale stone. Inside hang the colors of a man who saw the world burning bright. Yellow wheat. Deep blue night. Pink blossom against a clear sky.

This is the Van Gogh Museum, on the Museumplein in Amsterdam. It holds the largest collection of Vincent van Gogh's work found anywhere. More than two hundred paintings. Hundreds of drawings. And many letters written in his own hand. Under a single roof, you can follow almost his entire life as an artist.

Vincent van Gogh was born in the Netherlands in 1853. He came to painting late. He tried other paths first. He worked as an art dealer, a teacher, and a preacher before he took up a brush in earnest. In the end, he painted for only about ten years. In that short time, he made roughly two thousand works.

His younger brother Theo stood at the center of his life. Theo worked as an art dealer in Paris. He sent Vincent money, paint, and steady encouragement. The two wrote to each other constantly. Many of those letters survive, and some are kept here. They show a man thinking hard about color, about light, and about what art is really for.

Vincent sold very little while he lived. He struggled with his health and with his mind. He died in 1890, at the age of thirty-seven. Theo died only months after him. It was Theo's widow, Johanna, who held the paintings together. She worked for years to show them and to publish the brothers' letters. Without her, this collection might have been scattered and lost.

In time, the works passed to a foundation set up by the family. A museum was built to hold them, and it opened in 1973. The main building was designed by the Dutch architect Gerrit Rietveld. He was known for clean lines and simple, honest forms. The calm of the building lets the paintings do the talking.

A second wing was added later. The Japanese architect Kisho Kurokawa designed it, and it opened in 1999. It curves where the older building stands square. Together, the two parts give the collection room to breathe, and space for large temporary exhibitions. The newer wing also brings a wide staircase and a hall full of daylight.

Stand close to one of the canvases, then step back. Up near the surface, the paint stands up in ridges. You can see the path of the brush, even the marks left by the bristles. Step away, and those ridges settle into a field, a face, a sky. Van Gogh built his pictures from texture as much as from color.

The paintings are arranged so you can watch him change. His early work is dark and heavy. One canvas, The Potato Eaters, shows a peasant family at a dim table. The colors are brown and earthy, the faces rough. He spent long days among farmers and weavers, learning to draw the weight of their work. This is Van Gogh before the light came in.

Then he moved to Paris, and later to the south of France. His palette caught fire. Here you find the yellows and blues of his middle and late years. Sunflowers crowded in a vase. A simple bedroom with a wooden bed and bright walls. Fields of grain under restless skies. Each brushstroke sits where you can see it, thick and sure.

One small painting often draws a quiet crowd. It is called Almond Blossom. White flowers open across a pale blue sky. Van Gogh made it for Theo's newborn son, his nephew, who was named after him. It is a gift in paint, full of hope for a new life. Knowing its story changes the way the branches look.

Do not rush past the letters. Many are kept here, often with small sketches drawn in the margins. He used them to plan his paintings and to explain his ideas to Theo. Read even a few lines, and you hear a clear, searching voice. He wrote much the way he painted. Directly, and with deep feeling.

You leave by the same bright square where you came in. The colors stay with you for a while afterward. A man who sold almost nothing in his own life now fills a building with light. Look up at the wide sky over the square. You may find you are seeing it a little more like he did.

Key facts

Vincent van Gogh lived from 1853 to 1890
Museum opened in 1973 on the Museumplein
Holds over two hundred Van Gogh paintings
Main building designed by architect Gerrit Rietveld
Curved exhibition wing opened in 1999

What to look for
