The guide

The story

Listen first. A tram bell rings somewhere behind you. Bicycle tyres hiss over the cobbles. Water laps against the stone edge of the Keizersgracht canal. Now look down at your feet. Set into the paving of this square are large slabs of pink granite, cut into sharp triangles. They are easy to miss. Many people walk right across them without a second glance, and that is part of the story.

You are standing at the Homomonument, on the Westermarkt in central Amsterdam. It is a memorial to gay men and lesbian women who have been persecuted because of who they love. It was unveiled in 1987, and it is widely described as one of the first monuments of its kind anywhere in the world. Most memorials rise up toward the sky. This one lies almost flat... and it asks you to search for it.

The shape matters. During the Nazi era, men accused of homosexuality were sent to concentration camps. There, they were forced to wear a pink triangle sewn onto their clothing. It marked them as targets — often for harsher treatment than other prisoners. After the war, many survivors received little recognition. Some still faced laws that criminalised their lives. Decades later, gay rights activists took that badge of shame and claimed it as a symbol of pride and resistance.

The memorial was designed by the Dutch artist Karin Daan. Her idea was simple and clever. She took three pink granite triangles and placed them so that, together, they form the corners of one much larger triangle. You cannot see the whole shape from any single point. You have to walk around it. You have to connect the pieces in your own mind. The full triangle only exists when you move through the square.

Each of the three triangles is set at a different height. Each one stands for a different moment in time. One represents the past. One represents the present. And one represents the future. Take a slow walk around the square now and try to find all three. Notice how the paving stones run between them, so that ordinary life — bikes, feet, market stalls — flows straight through the middle of the memorial.

Start with the triangle at the canal edge. Here, granite steps lead down to the water of the Keizersgracht. This is the past. It points across the old city toward the National Monument on Dam Square, where the Netherlands remembers its war dead. The connection is deliberate. It places the people who wore the pink triangle within the wider story of the Second World War. They are not a footnote. They belong in that remembrance too.

Next, find the triangle that is raised a little above the ground, like a low platform. This one points toward the Anne Frank House, just a short walk along the canal. Turn and look in that direction for a moment. Two stories of persecution sit within sight of each other here. Anne Frank hid with her family a few steps from where you stand. The memorial does not compare these histories. It simply places them side by side.

The third triangle sits level with the pavement. It represents the present, and it points toward the office of the COC, one of the oldest gay and lesbian rights organisations in the world. The COC was founded in Amsterdam soon after the Second World War. Its work helped make the Netherlands one of the first countries to open civil marriage to same-sex couples. So this flat triangle looks toward daily, ongoing activism — not toward a finished story.

Now look for words. Cut into the granite is a single line of Dutch poetry: "Naar vriendschap zulk een mateloos verlangen." In English, it means roughly "such an endless longing for friendship." The line comes from Jacob Israël de Haan, a Dutch poet and writer of the early twentieth century. He wrote openly about love between men at a time when that was a brave act. Many visitors stand over the words without realising a poem lies beneath their feet.

Here is something many visitors miss. The steps at the water's edge are not only symbolic. People use them. On many days you will find flowers laid on the granite — single roses, small bunches, sometimes candles or handwritten notes. Locals come here to mark personal losses. The memorial is not a museum piece behind glass. It is touched, sat on, and cared for by the people who use it. That everyday contact is part of its design.

The square comes alive on certain days of the year. On the fourth of May, when the Netherlands holds its national Remembrance of the Dead, people gather here to lay wreaths. During Pride in summer, the steps and triangles fill with flowers, flags, and crowds. And in recent years the memorial has also become a place for vigils after attacks on gay communities elsewhere in the world. When news breaks, Amsterdam often comes here first.

Look up now, toward the tall church tower beside you. That is the Westerkerk, a Protestant church from the seventeenth century. Its tower rises high above the canal houses, and its bells ring across the neighbourhood. The painter Rembrandt was buried in this church, though the exact location of his grave is no longer known. Anne Frank wrote in her diary about hearing these bells from her hiding place. So the sounds around this memorial carry their own layers of history.

Think about what this place asks of you. It does not tower over you or demand silence. You could cross the square on a bicycle and never notice it at all. That quietness is honest. For much of history, the lives it remembers were hidden too. The memorial waits, flat and open, for the moment you choose to see it. And once you have seen the three triangles, it is hard to stop seeing them.

Before you leave, stand for a moment at the top of the canal steps. Watch the light move on the water of the Keizersgracht. Listen to the bells, the bicycles, the voices of people passing by. Then look once more at the pink stone under your feet. As you walk away along the canal, you may notice the triangles in your memory, joining up into one shape — past, present, and future, all in the same square.
