The guide

The story

Put your hand flat against the wall. The brick is cool and slightly rough, and it runs in long, even courses down the length of the Damrak. Trams ring past behind you. Bicycles tick over the cobbles. And in front of you stands a building that looks, at first glance, almost plain: a long brown block, a square tower, and very little decoration at all.

This is the Beurs van Berlage, the old commodity exchange of Amsterdam. It was designed by the architect Hendrik Petrus Berlage and completed in the early years of the twentieth century. Today, it hosts conferences, exhibitions, concerts and events. But for many decades it was a place of trade. Merchants came here to buy and sell grain, coffee and other goods.

That plainness is the first surprise. Around 1900, grand public buildings in Europe were often covered in columns, domes and carved stone. Berlage chose a different path. He wanted a building that was honest about how it was made. Brick should look like brick. A load-bearing wall should look like it carries weight. Nothing should pretend to be something else.

Look along the facade and you will see what that idea means in practice. The windows sit in deep, clear openings. Arches spring directly from the brickwork. Stone appears only where it does real work: at corners, at the bases of supports, at points of stress. The building feels calm. It does not shout for your attention. Instead, it rewards a slower look.

Now turn your eyes to the tower. It rises above the corner of the building and carries a clock. Near the top runs a short Dutch motto, Beidt uw tijd. It means, roughly, bide your time, or wait for your moment. It is a gentle line to place above a trading floor, where people made money by acting fast. Perhaps that was the point — a quiet reminder that patience has its own value.

Here is something most people walking past never think about. Amsterdam sits on soft, wet ground. Almost every old building in the city stands on wooden piles, driven deep into the soil to reach firmer layers below. The exchange is no exception. Thousands of piles support this long brick mass. So the solid building in front of you is, in a sense, standing on a hidden forest under the street.

The site itself has a watery past. The Damrak was once an open stretch of the river Amstel, where boats came right into the heart of the city. Over time, much of it was filled in to make land. An earlier exchange building stood nearby before this one, and the city decided it needed something larger and more modern. Berlage's design was the answer.

If you can step inside, the second surprise is waiting. The great halls are wide and open, with walls of exposed brick and roofs of iron and glass. Daylight falls from above onto the old trading floor. The iron trusses are left visible, so you can read the structure with your eyes. In places, glazed bricks add soft colour — and it is a warmer, lighter space than the outside suggests.

Berlage did not work alone. He believed that architecture, art and craft should come together in one building. The sculptor Lambertus Zijl made carved figures and details for the exchange. The painter Jan Toorop created tile panels. The poet Albert Verwey wrote verses that appear in the building. Each piece is set into the architecture instead of being hung on it like a picture.

This idea of a shared, thoughtful public building made a deep impression. Many architects see the Beurs as a turning point in Dutch design. It looks back to medieval brick traditions, yet it also points forward to modern architecture, where structure and honesty matter more than ornament. A younger generation of Amsterdam architects took brick in bolder, more expressive directions in the decades that followed. That style is known as the Amsterdam School, and you can see it across the city.

The building has also had moments in national life. In 2002, the civil wedding of the future King Willem-Alexander and Máxima took place here. Over the years, the trading moved elsewhere, and the halls found new uses. Music, meetings and exhibitions now fill the spaces where merchants once called out their prices. The building has changed its purpose but kept its character.

As you stand here, notice how the exchange sits in its setting. To one side, the Damrak runs toward Centraal Station, with its crowds arriving and leaving. On the other side, the streets lead toward Dam Square and the Royal Palace. The Beurs stands between the station and the old centre, a long brick edge between the city's arrivals and its oldest heart.

Take a moment before you move on. Look again at the brick. You may now notice small things you missed at first: a carved head above a doorway, a change in the colour of the stone, the exact line where an arch meets a wall. The building was designed for people who took their time. The motto on the tower still asks the same thing of you. So walk slowly along the facade as you leave. Listen to the trams and the bicycle bells. Feel the cobbles under your feet, with the wooden piles somewhere far beneath them. Above you, the clock keeps turning — and the old exchange keeps standing, quietly, on its hidden forest.
