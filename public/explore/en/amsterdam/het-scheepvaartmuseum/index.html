The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water laps against stone. Gulls call overhead, and somewhere a rope creaks against a wooden hull. Before you stands a broad, square building of pale sandstone, rising straight out of the harbour as if it grew there. The air smells faintly of salt, wet timber, and diesel from passing boats. This is Het Scheepvaartmuseum, the National Maritime Museum of the Netherlands. To understand Amsterdam, you have to understand the sea. And this building is one of the best places in the city to begin.

Look at its shape first. It is a near-perfect square, with four wings set around a central courtyard. The front is calm, symmetrical, and almost severe. This is Dutch classicism, a seventeenth-century style that borrowed the order and balance of ancient Rome and Greece. Columns, a triangular pediment, rows of identical windows. Nothing is flashy. The design says something about the people who built it: serious, practical, and very confident about their place in the world.

The building was not made as a museum. It was finished in 1656 as 's Lands Zeemagazijn, which means the State's Naval Storehouse. The architect was Daniël Stalpaert, who also worked on other major projects for the city in that era. This was the supply depot of the Amsterdam Admiralty, one of the bodies that ran the Dutch navy. Inside, workers stored sails, ropes, cannons, flags, and everything a warship needed before it left port. Think of it as a giant warehouse for a naval power at its peak.

Here is something many visitors walk past without noticing. The building stands on water, not on solid ground. Like much of Amsterdam, it rests on wooden piles driven deep into the soft, wet soil below. The site was chosen for a simple reason — ships could pull right up beside it. Supplies moved straight from the storehouse onto the decks. The water around you was once a busy working harbour, full of masts, shouting crews, and heavy loads swinging on ropes.

The mid-seventeenth century was the period the Dutch call their Golden Age. Amsterdam was one of the richest trading cities on Earth. Its ships carried spices, textiles, grain, and timber between Asia, Africa, the Americas, and the Baltic. That wealth had a dark side too. Dutch trade included the enslavement and transport of people, and colonial violence across the globe. Today the museum does not hide this. Several displays inside address the human cost of maritime power alongside its achievements.

The navy kept using this building for a very long time — right up until the 1970s. Then it became the home of the maritime collection. Over the following decades, the old storehouse grew tired. In the early 2010s it reopened after a major renovation. That renovation gave the museum its most surprising feature, and you will want to see it as soon as you go inside.

Step into the central courtyard and look up. Above you, a vast roof of glass and steel covers the open square. The steel frame is not a simple grid. Its lines spread out in a web of crossing curves and straight rays. The pattern is based on the compass roses and rhumb lines found on old nautical charts. Rhumb lines are the straight lines sailors once used to plot a steady course across the sea. So when you stand here, you are standing beneath a map.

Notice how the light changes in the courtyard. On a bright day, the steel frame casts a shifting net of shadows across the stone floor. On a grey Amsterdam afternoon, the light turns soft and even. The courtyard feels like an outdoor square and an indoor hall at the same time. Children often stop in the middle and spin around, tracing the lines above with their fingers. It is a good place to pause before you choose which wing to explore first.

The collection inside is large and varied. You will find globes, atlases, and sea charts from the era when Dutch mapmakers were among the best in Europe. There are ship models of remarkable detail, navigational instruments in brass and wood, and paintings of sea battles and harbours. Some galleries focus on the art of the Van de Velde family, father and son, who painted the sea with extraordinary precision. Other rooms are interactive, built for younger visitors who want to touch, steer, and play.

One object often surprises people: a royal barge, long and gilded, once rowed by a team of oarsmen on ceremonial occasions. It sits indoors, gleaming with carved decoration in gold and deep colours. Up close, the craftsmanship is extraordinary. Every curl of wood was shaped by hand. It is a reminder that boats in this country were not only tools for war and trade. They were also symbols of power, celebration, and national pride.

Now walk back outside and look along the quay. A tall wooden ship sits moored beside the museum. This is a replica of the Amsterdam, an East India Company ship. The original sailed in the eighteenth century and ran aground near Hastings, on the south coast of England, during its first voyage. Its wreck still lies there in the sand. The replica was built in Amsterdam in the late twentieth century, largely by volunteers and trainees. You can usually go on board and explore its decks.

On the ship, pay attention to the space. The decks are low. The beams are close above your head. Hundreds of sailors, soldiers, and passengers once lived on ships like this for months at a time. Imagine the smell of tar, sweat, and old food. Imagine the noise of storms and the cramped hammocks swinging in the dark. Many people who set out on these voyages never returned. Standing below deck makes those history books feel suddenly physical and close.

From the quay you can also see the wider harbour around you. Across the water rises a large green building shaped a little like the bow of a ship. That is NEMO, the city's science museum. Behind you lies the Kattenburg area, once full of naval workers and shipyards. Bicycles rattle over the bridges. Tour boats glide past slowly. The harbour no longer serves warships, but it is still very much alive with movement and water traffic.

Before you leave, take one more look at the storehouse itself. Find the pale stone, the even windows, the solid square shape sitting calmly on the water. For more than three centuries, this building has watched ships come and go. It has held cannons, sails, and now stories. As you walk away, listen again to the water against the quay. It is the same sound that sailors heard here long ago... the sound of a city that was built by the sea.

Key facts

Before you go

Practical tips

Start in the central courtyard to get your bearings.
Allow time to explore the replica ship on the quay.
Wear flat shoes for steep ladders aboard the ship.
Visit on a sunny day for roof shadow patterns.
Combine your visit with a walk past NEMO nearby.
