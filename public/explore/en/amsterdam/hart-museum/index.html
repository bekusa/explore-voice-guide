The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you look. Water slaps softly against the stone quay of the Amstel. Bicycle bells ring on the bridges, and a canal boat hums somewhere behind you. Then turn toward the building itself. It is long, calm, and almost severe. Row after row of tall windows stretch along the river. No towers. No gilded domes. Just a steady wall of brick and glass that seems to say: patience. This is the home of H'ART Museum, and the building is older and stranger than its new name suggests.

The building is called the Amstelhof. It went up in the late seventeenth century, during the years many Dutch people still call their Golden Age. That was the era of Rembrandt, the Dutch East India Company, and the great ring of canals you have probably already walked along. But the Amstelhof was not built for merchants or princes. The Protestant church community of the city built it as a home for elderly people who could no longer care for themselves. That purpose shaped everything you see.

Think about that plain facade again. In a city where rich families competed with carved gables and decorated doorways, this building is modest. That was deliberate. A charitable home was meant to look dignified and orderly, not proud. The long, regular rhythm of the windows let light into many small rooms. The scale tells you how many people lived here. And remarkably, the Amstelhof kept that role for more than three centuries. It was still a care home into the early 2000s.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. When you walk through these galleries, you are walking through rooms where generations of older Amsterdammers ate, slept, prayed, and grew old together. The walls have been opened up and modernised. The floors have been redone. But the bones of the place are still a home. Some visitors say the building feels unusually gentle for a museum. Perhaps that is why.

The second life of the Amstelhof began in the early 2000s. The State Hermitage Museum in Saint Petersburg, one of the largest art museums in the world, was looking for a partner abroad. Amsterdam and Saint Petersburg already had an old connection. In the late seventeenth century, Tsar Peter the Great came to the Netherlands to study shipbuilding. He later borrowed Dutch ideas for his new capital on the Neva river. So a Hermitage branch on the Amstel felt like a story coming full circle.

The museum first opened in a smaller wing of the complex in 2004. After the last residents moved out and a major renovation was finished, the full Amstelhof opened as the Hermitage Amsterdam in 2009. For years, it showed treasures lent from Russia. There were exhibitions on the Romanov court, on Dutch masters collected by the tsars, and on French painting. Crowds came to see objects that rarely left Saint Petersburg.

Then history changed the museum's direction. After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, the Amsterdam museum ended its partnership with the Hermitage in Saint Petersburg. It was a sudden and serious decision for an institution built around that link. The building did not change. But its identity had to. In 2023, the museum took a new name: H'ART. The name combines the word heart with the word art, so the idea is a place with art at its centre.

This is the second surprise. H'ART does not own a large permanent collection of its own. Instead, it works with major international partners. These include the British Museum in London, the Centre Pompidou in Paris, and the Smithsonian American Art Museum in Washington. Each partner lends works for temporary exhibitions. So what you see inside depends on when you come. One season might bring ancient objects, another modern painting from France or America.

That makes the museum an interesting kind of space. It is less like a treasure house and more like a stage. The exhibitions travel in, they stay for a few months, and then they leave. Because of this, it is worth checking what is showing before you visit. It also means that a return trip a year later can feel like visiting a completely different museum, inside the very same walls.

If you go inside, pay attention to the courtyard. The Amstelhof is built around a large enclosed garden. In the old care home, this was where residents could sit outside, safe from the noise and traffic of the city. Today it is still a quiet green space, framed on all sides by the long brick wings. On a calm day, it is one of the most peaceful spots in this part of central Amsterdam. The city feels very far away, even though it is only a wall's thickness off.

Now look outward again, along the river. The Amstel is the river that gave Amsterdam its name. The city began as a settlement beside a dam on this water. Just along the bank from here is the Magere Brug, a white wooden drawbridge that many visitors photograph at night when its lights come on. Behind the museum, the Nieuwe Herengracht canal runs past. You are standing at a quiet seam between the busy centre and the calmer eastern neighbourhoods.

This stretch of riverbank rewards slow walking. The light here changes quickly. In the morning, the facade can look cool and grey. In the late afternoon, the low sun turns the brick warmer and the water throws moving reflections across the windows. Houseboats line parts of the canals nearby. Small cafes sit on the corners. It is a good place to stop, sit for a while, and simply watch the river traffic pass.

So before you walk on, take one more look at that long, patient wall. For more than three hundred years, it sheltered people at the end of their lives. Then it carried a bridge between two cities, and when that bridge broke, it found a new purpose. The building has never been grand. It has been useful, and it has adapted. As you leave, listen again to the water against the quay. It was making that same sound when the first residents arrived here, centuries ago.
