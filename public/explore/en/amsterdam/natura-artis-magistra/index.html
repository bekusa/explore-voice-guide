The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the old trees, gravel crunches beneath your feet. Somewhere a bird calls, sharp and foreign, and from beyond the railings comes the faint ring of a tram bell. This is one of the few places in central Amsterdam where the city's noise falls away. It is replaced by the sounds of a garden that has been growing for nearly two centuries.

You are standing in Natura Artis Magistra, which most people in Amsterdam simply call Artis. The full name is Latin, and it means "Nature is the teacher of art." It sounds grand, and that is deliberate. The founders did not want a menagerie for cheap thrills. They wanted a society devoted to studying nature, and to sharing that knowledge with the educated public of their time.

Artis was founded in 1838, which makes it the oldest zoo in the Netherlands and one of the oldest in mainland Europe. The driving figure was Gerardus Frederik Westerman, a bookseller with a passion for natural history. He and two partners started a society. Animals, books, and specimens followed. For much of the nineteenth century, entry was largely reserved for paying members. Artis was a social club as much as a zoo, a place where Amsterdam's middle class came to walk, be seen, and learn.

Now look at the neighbourhood around you. This district is called the Plantage, which means "the plantation." In the seventeenth century, the city laid it out as green space beyond the canal ring, with gardens and pleasure grounds. Later it filled with elegant houses. The zoo fits that original idea well. Just a short walk away is the Hortus Botanicus, one of the oldest botanical gardens in the world. Together they keep this corner of Amsterdam green and leafy.

Here is something many visitors miss. Artis is not only a zoo. It is also a historic park, and many of its buildings and trees are protected monuments. Some of the trees are older than most of the houses on the surrounding streets. As you walk, look at the trunks, the iron fences, and the brick pavilions with their painted details. Much of this layout still follows the nineteenth-century plan. The paths curve on purpose, to make a small site feel larger.

One of the most impressive buildings is the Aquarium, which opened in 1882. From the outside, it looks more like a classical museum than a fish tank, with a stately facade and heavy proportions. Inside, the light changes. It becomes dim, blue, and cool. Large tanks show Dutch waters as well as distant seas. There is even a display of an Amsterdam canal, seen from below the surface. Look for the bicycles and the debris on the bottom. It is a quiet reminder of what lies under the water you walked past today.

Another surprise is Micropia, which opened in 2014. It is often described as the world's first museum devoted entirely to microbes. These are the invisible life forms on your skin, in your food, and in the soil under these trees. It may sound like an odd addition to a zoo, but it follows the founders' idea closely. Nature is the teacher, and most of nature is too small to see. Micropia simply gives that small world a stage.

Then there is the old society building, the original heart of Natura Artis Magistra. For generations it held the society's library, lectures, and collections of specimens. Today it has been opened to the public again as the Groote Museum, which explores the connections between people and the rest of nature. The building itself is worth a look. Its large hall was built for an age that believed science and beauty belonged in the same room.

Artis also carries a darker chapter. During the German occupation in the Second World War, Amsterdam's Jewish community suffered terribly, and the Plantage stood at the centre of that story. The Hollandsche Schouwburg, a former theatre a few minutes from here, was used as a deportation centre. According to accounts gathered after the war, some people found hiding places inside the zoo grounds, helped by staff. The details of individual stories vary, but the memory remains part of the zoo's identity.

Now come back to the present. Walk slowly and notice how the animals are placed among the trees. The enclosures are mixed with lawns, ponds, and flower beds, so the experience feels more like strolling through a park than following a list of exhibits. Families stop at the railings. Students sketch. Older Amsterdammers sit on benches they have known since childhood. That mix of people is part of what makes Artis feel local, not only touristic.

The zoo also has a planetarium, where the dome above your head turns into a night sky. It may seem out of place among lions and flamingos. But again, it fits the original mission. The founders imagined a place to study all of nature, from the smallest creature to the stars. Few zoos in the world try to cover that full range in one walk, and that ambition is one of the quiet surprises of this place.

Over the years, Artis has changed its focus. In the nineteenth century, the goal was to display as many species as possible. Today the emphasis is on conservation, education, and how humans live alongside other species. You can see that shift in the newer exhibits, which ask questions as much as they show animals. Yet the old setting remains. Modern ideas live inside Victorian-era walls, under trees planted by people who could never have imagined them.

Before you leave, take one more look around you. Notice the light coming through the branches, and the way it falls on the brick and the water. Listen again for that mix of birdsong, children's voices, and the distant city. Then think about the motto above the society's name once more. Nature is the teacher of art. As you step back into the streets of the Plantage, you might see the canals, the trees, and even the pigeons a little differently.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early on weekdays for quieter paths and active animals.
Plan extra time for Micropia, the Aquarium, and the planetarium.
Check whether Micropia requires a separate ticket before visiting.
Combine your visit with the nearby Hortus Botanicus garden.
Wear comfortable shoes; gravel paths cover much of the park.
