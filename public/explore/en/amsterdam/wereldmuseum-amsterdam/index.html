The guide

The story

Stand for a moment at the foot of the building and look up. Red brick rises in heavy layers, broken by pale stone, small towers and carved figures. Behind you, trams rattle along the Mauritskade, and the canal water moves slowly under the bridges. On the other side of the building, the trees of the Oosterpark move in the wind. This is the Wereldmuseum Amsterdam, a museum about people, cultures and the ties that connect them. It sits inside one of the most complicated buildings in the city.

The building did not begin as a museum of world cultures. It was built for the Colonial Institute, an organisation founded in the early twentieth century. Its task was to study and promote the Dutch colonies, above all the territories that are now Indonesia and Suriname. Construction started in the 1910s, but the First World War slowed the work badly. The building only opened in 1926, when Queen Wilhelmina came to inaugurate it. So these walls were designed to celebrate an empire. Today, the people inside use them to question that empire.

The architects were members of the Van Nieukerken family, a father working with his sons. They chose an eclectic style, a mix of older European forms with no single model. You can see touches of Dutch Renaissance in the gables and towers, and something close to a castle in the heavy brick. Walk slowly along the façade and look at the carvings. Many of them show colonial themes — plants, animals and figures from the tropics, and scenes of trade. They were made to impress visitors in the 1920s. Now they serve as evidence of how a nation once saw itself.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The building was never only a museum. It was built as a research and teaching centre, with laboratories, offices and lecture rooms. The Royal Tropical Institute, which grew out of the old Colonial Institute, still uses part of the building today. So when you walk through the corridors, you share the building with a working institute. The museum is only one tenant in a much larger house.

The name has changed several times, and each change marks a shift in thinking. For decades after the Second World War, the museum was known as the Tropenmuseum, the Tropical Museum. Many Amsterdammers still call it that. In 2014, it joined the national museums of world cultures in Leiden and Berg en Dal, and later the museum in Rotterdam. In 2023, all of them took the shared name Wereldmuseum. The word means simply “World Museum”. The new name moves the focus away from the tropics and the colonies, and towards the whole world.

Now step inside. The first thing most people notice is the great central hall. It rises through several floors, with galleries running around it on every level. Light falls from high above through a glass roof, and voices carry upward and soften. Wood, marble and decorated columns give the space a formal, almost ceremonial feeling. Find a spot near the railing on an upper gallery and look down. From there you can see how the whole building turns around this one open heart.

The collections are enormous, and only a small part is on display at any time. The museum holds objects from Asia, Africa, Oceania and the Americas. You will find textiles, masks, musical instruments, jewellery, religious objects and everyday tools. There are also many photographs and films. Some objects were bought or commissioned. Others came through colonial expeditions, military campaigns and missionary work. The museum now says openly that the way an object arrived is part of its story. The labels often tell you where a piece came from, and also how it ended up here in Amsterdam.

This honesty is the second surprise of the visit. A large exhibition looks directly at the Dutch colonial past and its effects today. It does not present the past as a neat chapter that is finished. It speaks about slavery, violence, resistance and the long shadow these left behind. Families in Amsterdam with roots in Suriname, Indonesia and the Caribbean are part of this story. Many visitors leave this section quieter than they entered it. Take your time here. The objects are not only beautiful. They carry the weight of real lives.

The question of ownership also goes beyond the galleries. In recent years, the Netherlands has started to return some objects taken during the colonial era to their countries of origin. The Wereldmuseum takes part in this research and in these conversations. So the collection is not fixed. Pieces may travel back to the communities they came from. Others may stay, with a new understanding of who has the right to tell their story. A museum like this one is always changing, even if the walls stay the same.

Not everything here is heavy, though. The museum also shows how people celebrate, dress, cook, make music and mark the big moments of life. Exhibitions change regularly, and they often bring in living artists, designers and community groups. For children, there are hands-on spaces where they can touch, listen and play. Families often spend a long time in these rooms. If you travel with young people, it is worth checking which family activities are running during your visit.

When you leave, give yourself a few minutes in the neighbourhood. Directly behind the museum lies the Oosterpark, a large green park with ponds, wide lawns and old trees. Locals jog, walk their dogs and sit in the grass on warm days. A short walk away is the Dappermarkt, a street market where stalls sell fruit, fish, fabric and spices from many parts of the world. In a sense, the market continues the museum’s story. The connections shown inside the building are still alive in the streets around it.

The area itself, Amsterdam East, has changed a great deal over the past century. Working-class families once filled these blocks. Later, many people arrived from former colonies and from countries around the Mediterranean. Today you can hear many languages on a single tram ride. This makes the location of the museum feel fitting. A building made to celebrate distant colonies now stands in a neighbourhood where the descendants of those colonies live, work and shape the city.

Before you go, turn around once more and look at the façade. The towers, the carved stone and the brick still carry the confidence of the 1920s. But the people moving through the doors bring new questions with them. Notice the tram bells, the cyclists, the sound of children from the park. The building is the same one Queen Wilhelmina opened. What has changed is the story we choose to tell inside it... and that story is still being written.
