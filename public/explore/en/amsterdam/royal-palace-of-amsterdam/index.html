The guide

The story

Stand on Dam Square for a moment and listen. Tram bells, pigeons lifting off the cobbles, bicycles ticking past. In front of you rises a long, pale wall of stone, darkened in places by centuries of city air. It does not look like a fairy-tale palace. There are no turrets, no golden gates. Instead it is calm, square and heavy, almost like a bank. That impression is closer to the truth than you might think.

This is the Royal Palace of Amsterdam. Yet it was never built for a king. It was built as a town hall, for the merchants and magistrates who ran the city during the Dutch Golden Age. That was the seventeenth century, when ships from this port traded across the world. Amsterdam was rich and confident, and its leaders wanted a building that said so in stone.

The architect was Jacob van Campen. He drew on classical ideas, the clean lines and columns of ancient Rome, as they were reinterpreted in Italy. Construction began in 1648. That year matters. It was the year of the Peace of Münster, when the Dutch Republic won formal recognition of its independence from Spain. So this building rose at a moment of victory. The new town hall was meant to show a young republic that had arrived.

Now think about the ground under your feet. Amsterdam sits on soft, wet soil. To hold up a building of this weight, the builders drove wooden piles deep into the ground. The figure usually given is more than thirteen thousand of them. Schoolchildren in the Netherlands still learn a version of that number by heart. The whole palace stands on a hidden forest of timber, kept from rotting because it stays below the waterline.

Look at the front of the building. You may notice something odd. There is no grand central doorway. Instead there are seven modest arches. A popular explanation says they stand for the seven provinces of the Dutch Republic. Another story says the small entrances were a practical choice — easier to defend if an angry crowd ever stormed the square. Both are told often. Treat them as interpretations, not as documented fact.

Now raise your eyes to the triangular pediment above. The sculpture there shows the Maiden of Amsterdam, a female figure who represents the city. Sea creatures and figures from distant lands bring her gifts. The message is simple. The world's riches flow to this harbour. Much of the sculpture inside and out came from the workshop of Artus Quellinus, a Flemish sculptor. He and his team worked on the building for years.

Walk around to the back if you can. On top of the rear facade stands Atlas, the giant of Greek myth, carrying the globe on his shoulders. It is a bold image for a town hall. And high above everything sits the cupola, crowned with a weathervane in the shape of a ship. It is a fitting symbol. This city's fortune came from the sea, and its leaders never let anyone forget it.

When the building opened in 1655, it was not yet fully finished. Even so, admirers soon called it the Eighth Wonder of the World. For a time it was among the largest town halls in Europe. It held far more than offices. The city's exchange bank operated here, along with courts and cells for prisoners. Government, money and justice all lived under one roof.

Inside, the great surprise is the Citizens' Hall. It is a vast marble room, open to the people of the city. Look down at the floor. Inlaid in the marble are maps of the world, showing the eastern and western hemispheres, together with a map of the heavens. Imagine a merchant crossing this room in his heavy boots. He was quite literally walking across the globe his ships were trading on.

There is a darker room on the ground floor, called the Tribunal. This is where death sentences were pronounced in public. The space is lined with marble figures of kneeling women, carved with sorrowful faces. The room is beautiful, and that beauty makes it unsettling. Justice here was meant to be seen. The building's art never lets you forget that power carried a cost.

Here is a story most visitors miss. Rembrandt himself painted a huge canvas for this building, showing an ancient revolt by the Batavians, a tribe the Dutch saw as their ancestors. It hung only briefly before it came down. Nobody knows for certain why. Rembrandt cut the painting down, and the surviving fragment is now in Stockholm, in Sweden. One of the greatest Dutch painters was turned away from the town hall of his own city.

The building only became a palace in the nineteenth century. In 1806, Napoleon made his brother Louis king of Holland. Louis moved into the town hall in 1808 and turned it into a royal residence. Much of the Empire-style furniture you may see inside dates from his short reign. After the French left, the building passed to the Dutch royal family. Today the monarch uses it for state visits, receptions and official ceremonies, rather than as a home.

In 2013, Queen Beatrix signed her abdication inside this building. Soon after, she appeared on the balcony with her son, the new King Willem-Alexander, while crowds filled the square below. So take one more look before you go. Notice the tall church just beside the palace, the Nieuwe Kerk, where Dutch monarchs are inaugurated. Notice the stone, the ship on the cupola, and the trams sliding past. A town hall became a palace. Under it all, thousands of hidden wooden piles still hold it up.
