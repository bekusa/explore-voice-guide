The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Above the traffic of bicycles and the slap of canal water against stone, a carillon is playing. The notes fall from high overhead, bright and slightly uneven, and drift out across the Prinsengracht. You are standing beside the Westerkerk, the Western Church, and its tower has been marking time over this neighbourhood for roughly four centuries. Look up, past the red brick and pale stone, to the very top. There, painted in blue, red and gold, sits a crown.

That crown is the first surprise. It is not a Dutch royal crown at all. It represents the imperial crown of Maximilian of Austria, a Habsburg ruler of the Holy Roman Empire. In the late fifteenth century, he granted Amsterdam the right to place his crown in its coat of arms, as thanks for the city's support. Amsterdam kept that honour long after the Habsburgs became enemies of the Dutch Republic. So the highest symbol on this Protestant tower is a gift from a Catholic emperor.

The church itself rose in the early seventeenth century, during the years many people call the Dutch Golden Age. Amsterdam was growing fast. New ring canals were being dug outward from the old centre, and wealthy merchants were building tall houses along them. These new districts needed places of worship. The Westerkerk was built for the Dutch Reformed Church, the Protestant faith that had replaced Catholic worship in the city. It opened in 1631 and still holds Protestant services today.

The design came from Hendrick de Keyser, the leading architect and sculptor of Amsterdam in his time. He did not live to see it finished. He died in 1621, soon after building began, and his son Pieter de Keyser carried the work forward with other builders. The style is often called Dutch Renaissance. That means classical ideas from Italy, such as columns and balanced proportions, mixed with the local habit of red brick trimmed with light sandstone.

Now look at the tower in sections, from the ground upward. The base is solid brick, square and heavy. Above it, the stages grow lighter and more open, with stone, columns and windows. Higher still, the tower changes to wood clad in lead, and then rises to the crown. This is the tallest church tower in Amsterdam. Locals call it simply the Westertoren, the Western Tower, and many still use it as a landmark to find their way home.

Step inside if the doors are open, and the mood shifts completely. After the noise outside, the interior is calm and full of light. The walls are white, the windows are large and mostly clear, and the wooden roof forms a long curved vault above you. Reformed worship focused on the spoken word, not on images. So there are no crowded altars and no golden saints. The pulpit, where the sermon is preached, holds the place of honour.

The great exception to this plainness is the organ. Its case is grand and dark, and its large painted shutters can open and close over the pipes. These panels are attributed to the painter Gerard de Lairesse, and they show musical scenes in rich colours. In a building that avoided decoration, music was allowed to shine. Organ concerts are still held here, and if you hear one, notice how the sound fills the high, bare space.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a quiet one. Rembrandt van Rijn, the most famous painter of the Dutch Golden Age, is buried somewhere in this church. He died in 1669, poor and largely out of fashion. He was laid in a rented grave without a marker. Over the years, such graves were cleared and reused, so nobody knows exactly where he lies. A memorial on the wall remembers him instead. His son Titus, who died the year before, was buried here too.

Walk back outside and turn toward the canal houses just along the Prinsengracht. A few doors away is the Anne Frank House. During the Second World War, Anne Frank and her family hid from the Nazis in a secret annex at the back of that building. In her diary, she wrote about hearing the bells of the Westertoren. For a girl who could not go outside, the chimes were a link to the city she could no longer walk through.

Near the church, look for a small bronze statue of Anne Frank. It is modest in size, and people often pass it without noticing. Visitors sometimes leave flowers or small notes at its feet. Standing here, you can look up at the same tower whose bells she listened to. The sound you hear today travels across those same rooftops and courtyards. It is one of the few things in this story that has not changed.

The square in front of the church is called the Westermarkt. Close to the water's edge, you will find three large triangles of pink granite set into the ground and steps. This is the Homomonument, created to remember gay men and lesbian women who were persecuted for who they were. One triangle points toward the Anne Frank House. The pink triangle shape refers to the badge that gay prisoners were forced to wear in Nazi camps. Today the monument is a place for both mourning and celebration.

The Westerkerk has also seen joyful moments. In 1966, the future Queen Beatrix married Prince Claus here, an event watched by the whole country. The church is still part of everyday life. Neighbours attend services, choirs rehearse, and concerts bring in audiences on winter evenings. In warmer months, guided climbs take visitors up the tower. From the top, the canal rings spread out below like the lines of a fan, with gabled roofs in every direction.

Before you go, stand for a moment by the canal and look back at the whole building. See how the brick turns warmer when the sun falls on it, and how the crown catches the light at the top. Listen again for the bells marking the hour. Rembrandt, Anne Frank and generations of Amsterdammers heard that same sound. As you walk away along the water, let the chimes follow you for a few streets... and notice how long you can still hear them.
