The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the water and look up. A great green wall leans toward you, like the bow of a ship rising out of the harbour. The surface is copper, weathered to a soft sea-green, and it catches the grey Amsterdam light in long, dull stripes. Gulls call overhead. Somewhere below your feet, cars hum through a tunnel. This is NEMO, the city's science centre, and it does not look like any other building on this waterfront.

The shape is not an accident. The Italian architect Renzo Piano designed this building, and he drew it as a hull. Piano is the same architect who co-designed the Centre Pompidou in Paris, a building that wears its pipes on the outside. Here he chose a different idea — one bold form that rises from the water and points toward the old city. From the quay, the building really does look ready to sail out into the harbour.

There is a practical reason for the strange slope. The building sits directly above the entrance to the IJ Tunnel, a road tunnel that runs under the river behind it. Piano had to build on a site where the ground drops away for traffic. So the structure climbs as the road descends. The ramp that carries cars underground becomes the reason the building tilts upward. It is a clever answer to an awkward plot of land — and most visitors never notice the tunnel at all.

The centre opened in 1997, and in its first years it carried a different name: newMetropolis. A few years later it became NEMO. The new name fits the setting well. Nemo is the captain in Jules Verne's novel about a submarine, a man who explores the deep sea with machines and curiosity. For a science museum shaped like a ship and surrounded by water, it is a neat choice.

Before you go inside, think about the neighbourhood around you. This stretch of water is the Oosterdok, the eastern dock, part of the old harbour of Amsterdam. For centuries, ships loaded and unloaded goods here for one of the great trading cities of Europe. Today the warehouses have become offices, homes and cultural buildings. Just across the water, you may see the tall masts of a sailing ship. It is a replica of an eighteenth-century merchant vessel, moored beside the National Maritime Museum.

Now look again at the green copper. Fresh copper is bright orange-brown, like a new coin. Over time, air and rain turn it green, the same colour you see on old church roofs across Europe. On a building this size, the colour changes how it sits in the city. It does not fight the water and the sky. On a rainy day it looks almost like part of the harbour itself. In bright sun it glows.

Inside, the mood shifts completely. The calm outer shell gives way to noise, colour and movement. NEMO spreads its exhibitions across several floors, and almost everything is meant to be touched. You will hear the click of buttons, the rumble of rolling balls, and children shouting questions to each other. This is not a museum of glass cases. The idea is simple: you learn science by doing it, by testing, failing and trying again.

The themes cover a wide range. You will find displays about light and sound, about energy and electricity, and about the human body and the brain. Some floors focus on how technology works in daily life. Others explore bigger questions about the world around us. The exhibits change over the years, so what you see today may be different from what a friend saw a decade ago. Wander slowly. The best moments often come at a table you nearly walked past.

Here is something many adults miss. NEMO is not only for children, even though families fill it on weekends. Some of the exhibitions deal with serious subjects that grown-ups find surprising. Watch how other visitors react — a parent pressing a button out of politeness, then staying for five minutes to understand the result. Science centres work best when the adults stop supervising and start playing too.

The centre also runs live demonstrations and shows through the day. Staff mix chemicals, build chain reactions, or set up experiments in front of small crowds. Check the schedule near the entrance when you arrive. These short shows give a rhythm to the visit, and they are a good chance to sit down for a while. If you are travelling with children, plan your route around one or two of them.

Now for the second surprise — and it may be the best part of the building. The roof is a public space. That long slope you saw from outside is in fact a wide stepped terrace, climbing all the way to the top. In good weather, people sit on the steps like they would in an open-air theatre. They eat lunch, read, and watch the boats. Many locals come up here without visiting the exhibitions at all.

From the top, the city opens around you. You look across the rooftops of the old centre, with church towers and the gabled houses of the canal ring. Behind you lies the wide river, the IJ, with ferries crossing to the north bank. Trains move in and out near Central Station. On a clear evening, the light turns gold on the brick and the water. It is one of the easiest high viewpoints in Amsterdam to reach.

Wherever you are standing now, take a moment before you move on. Feel the wind that comes off the water — it is often stronger here than in the narrow streets. Listen to the mix of sounds: gulls, bicycle bells, the low engine of a passing boat. As you walk away, turn back once and look at the green bow behind you. A building made to teach curiosity, shaped like a ship, sitting quietly on top of a tunnel. Not a bad place to start wondering how things work.
