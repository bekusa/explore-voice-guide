The guide

The story

Listen first. Under your feet, the planks give a faint hollow knock with every step. Bicycle tyres hum across the boards behind you, and bells ring sharply as riders ask you to move aside. Below, the Amstel slides past, dark green and slow, slapping softly against the stone. This is the Magere Brug. In Dutch, the name means the Skinny Bridge. Stand still for a moment near the middle and feel how the whole wooden deck answers the traffic crossing it.

Look up at the shape above you. This is a double drawbridge. Two white wooden frames rise on either side, linked by beams and chains, like a pair of open gates set against the sky. Each half of the deck can lift upward, so that boats can pass between them. The design is old and very Dutch. Similar white lifting bridges cross small canals across the Netherlands. But few sit over water as wide and as busy as the Amstel at this point.

Why skinny? The simplest answer comes from history. The first bridge on this spot, from the late seventeenth century, was very narrow. Records place its origin around 1691. It was a modest crossing for people on foot, linking the two banks of the river along the line of Kerkstraat, the street that runs away from the bridge on both sides. It was practical, light and cheap. A thin bridge for a growing city that needed many crossings and could not afford grand stone on every one.

Now, there is a second answer, and you will hear it from many locals. Legend has it that two sisters with the surname Mager lived on opposite banks of the Amstel. They wanted to visit each other every day without a long walk around. So, the story goes, they paid for a bridge between their houses. It is a charming tale. But it is folklore, not documented history. Most historians think the name simply described the bridge itself... narrow, slight, and a little fragile.

The bridge you stand on today is not that first crossing. Over the centuries, the Skinny Bridge has been rebuilt several times. In the nineteenth century, the old narrow version gave way to a wider one, as traffic grew. Later in the twentieth century, the city rebuilt it again in wood, keeping the traditional double drawbridge form. So what you see is a careful copy of an old idea. The look is historic. The timber under your shoes is much younger than the story.

Here is something many visitors miss. Amsterdam decided to keep this bridge in wood, even when steel and concrete were easier choices. Wood needs constant care. It swells, it weathers, it must be painted again and again. That white paint is not only for beauty. It protects the timber from the damp air of the river. Keeping a wooden drawbridge alive here is a deliberate choice. It says the city values the old shape of the Amstel more than the cheapest solution.

And it still works. The Magere Brug is a real, moving bridge, not a decoration. When larger boats need to pass, the two halves of the deck lift up. Cyclists and walkers wait at the edges while the beams rise. Then the boat glides through the gap, and the deck lowers back into place. If you are lucky enough to see it happen, stay and watch. It is a small, slow piece of theatre that has played out on this river for generations.

Today, the bridge belongs to people on foot and on bicycles. Cars no longer cross here. That change made the bridge calmer, but not quiet. Bikes are the real traffic of Amsterdam, and they move fast. So keep to the side when you stop to look. Locals cross this bridge on their way to work, to school, to the market. For them it is not a sight. It is simply the shortest way home across the river.

Turn now and look along the water. The Amstel is the river that gave Amsterdam its name. The city grew from a dam on this river, many centuries ago. From the bridge, you can follow the wide, open curve of the water in both directions. Tour boats pass low under the deck. Houseboats rest along the edges. Tall brick houses with narrow fronts line the quays. This open view is rare in a city of tight, enclosed canals.

The bridge has also had a life on screen. It appears in the James Bond film Diamonds Are Forever, released in 1971, in scenes set in Amsterdam. Many other films, adverts and travel photographs have used it too. Its white frames are easy to recognise, even in a single quick shot. For decades, this simple wooden crossing has stood in for the whole idea of Amsterdam... water, light, old houses, and a bridge that opens to let boats pass.

Come back after dark if you can. At night, the bridge is outlined with hundreds of small light bulbs. They trace the arches, the beams and the edges of the deck. The reflection falls onto the black water below and shivers when a boat goes by. It is one of the gentlest night views in the city. Many couples walk here in the evening. The lights turn a working bridge into a quiet drawing of light on the river.

The area around the bridge rewards a short walk. On the eastern bank, along the river, you will find the large former almshouse that now holds the H'ART Museum, once known as the Hermitage Amsterdam. Close by stands the Royal Theatre Carré, home to circus and stage shows. To the north, the Blauwbrug, the Blue Bridge, crosses the Amstel in stone. Comparing the two bridges is worthwhile. One is heavy and formal. The other is light, white and wooden.

Before you go, stand once more at the centre of the deck. Feel the boards shift slightly as a bicycle rolls past. Look at the white chains, the dark river, the brick houses leaning gently over the water. Notice how small this bridge is against the width of the Amstel. It was never meant to impress. It was meant to connect two neighbours across the water. As you step off onto the cobbles, listen for that last hollow knock of wood behind you.

Practical tips

Return after dark to see the bridge outlined in lights.
Stand at the edges to stay clear of fast cyclists.
Wait a while to catch the deck lifting for boats.
Walk north along the Amstel to compare with the Blauwbrug.
Shoot from the riverbank for the full double-arch shape.
