The story

The Rijksmuseum did not begin in the building you see today. Its official history starts on 19 November 1798, when the government decided to establish a national museum. The National Art Gallery first opened on 31 May 1800 in Huis ten Bosch in The Hague, with more than 200 paintings and historical objects.

The collection moved to Amsterdam under Louis Napoleon in 1808 and later became the Rijks Museum. The current Pierre Cuypers building officially opened in 1885 after years of debate. Its Gothic and Renaissance language was meant to give the Netherlands a national museum building with symbolic force.

The modern visitor route follows the museum's 2013 renovation logic: painting, applied arts, and history come together to tell a chronological story of Dutch art and history. The official visitor information says the museum uses 8,000 objects to tell 800 years of Dutch art and history, from the Middle Ages to Mondrian.

The Gallery of Honour is the emotional centre, but the museum is richer when you notice how ships, silver, dollhouses, weapons, prints, furniture, and Asian objects complicate the story told by famous paintings.

The Rijksmuseum tells a national story, but it is not only a patriotic picture gallery. Its official route joins painting, sculpture, prints, furniture, ship models, weapons, textiles, ceramics, and objects from daily life. That mix is important because Dutch history cannot be understood through paintings alone.

The museum's long institutional path matters: a national museum decision in 1798, an opening in The Hague in 1800, a move to Amsterdam under Louis Napoleon, and finally the Cuypers building in 1885. Each stage reflects changing ideas about nationhood, monarchy, public access, and cultural memory.

The building itself was controversial because its Gothic and Renaissance language made a strong symbolic claim. Today that architecture is part of the visit. The passage under the museum, the Gallery of Honour, and the restored 2013 layout all turn the building into a narrative device.

Use the famous Dutch Golden Age rooms carefully. They are central, but the museum also asks visitors to think about trade, domestic life, global contact, religious change, craft, violence, wealth, and colonial history. A balanced guide should let admiration and historical complexity coexist.

Do not treat The Night Watch as the only destination.
Look sideways at furniture, ships, ceramics, and silver to understand daily life and trade.
Use the official app or map; the building is beautiful but easy to drift through.
Check current display notes for Operation Night Watch and gallery changes.
