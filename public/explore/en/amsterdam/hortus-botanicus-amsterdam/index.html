The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind the garden wall, the trams of the Plantage still rattle past. In here, the sound softens. Gravel crunches under your shoes, leaves move in the breeze, and somewhere a sparrow is arguing with a pigeon. The air smells of damp soil and cut grass. You are standing in a small walled garden on the eastern side of the old city. It is also one of the oldest botanical gardens in the world.

This is the Hortus Botanicus Amsterdam. Its story begins in 1638, when the city created a medicinal herb garden. In those years, doctors and apothecaries, the pharmacists of the day, worked with plants rather than pills. They needed a reliable place to grow them, study them, and teach students which leaf healed and which leaf killed. So the first purpose of this garden was practical. It was a living pharmacy and a classroom.

The garden did not begin on this spot. It started elsewhere in the city and moved here in 1682. At that time, the Plantage was a new district of green lots and pleasure gardens on the edge of town. The name itself means 'the plantation'. Wealthy families came out here to walk under the trees. The garden settled into this quiet corner, and it has stayed here ever since, for well over three centuries.

Now think about what Amsterdam was in the seventeenth century. The ships of the Dutch East India Company — the trading giant the Dutch call the VOC — sailed to Asia and back. The West India Company sailed to the Americas and Africa. Alongside spices and silk, the ships carried seeds, bulbs, and cuttings. Many of them came here. This garden became a receiving room for plants from across the world, and a place where Europeans tried to understand them.

That history has a darker side, and it is worth saying plainly. Many of these plants were collected through colonial trade, and some were grown for profit on plantations worked by enslaved people. The garden today addresses this openly in parts of its displays. As you walk, you can hold both ideas at once: the curiosity of the botanists, and the system of empire that carried the plants to their doors.

Here is the first surprise. Look for the coffee plants in the glasshouses. The story goes that a coffee plant was brought from Java to this garden in the early eighteenth century. Amsterdam then gave a young plant to King Louis the Fourteenth of France. Descendants of that French plant are said to have travelled on to the Caribbean and South America. If the tale is right, a great deal of the world's coffee has roots in this small garden.

Walk on toward the Palm House, the elegant glasshouse with its ironwork and tall windows, built in the early twentieth century. Inside, the air turns warm and heavy. Here lives the garden's most famous resident. It is a giant cycad from the Eastern Cape of South Africa. Cycads look a little like palms, but they belong to a far older family of plants, one that grew alongside the dinosaurs.

And here is the second surprise. This particular cycad is believed to be more than three hundred years old. It has lived in a pot for its whole life in Amsterdam. That makes it one of the oldest potted plants in the world. Take a moment with it. Its rough, scaly trunk and stiff, dark leaves have outlasted wars, floods, and generations of gardeners who carried it, repotted it, and kept it alive.

Nearby stands a very different building — the modern greenhouse with three separate climate zones. It opened in the 1990s, and its glass and steel lines contrast sharply with the older structures. Moving through it feels like a short journey across the planet. You pass from humid tropical forest, to a subtropical zone, to a dry desert room. Pay attention to your skin and your breathing as the air changes. Your glasses may fog as you step inside.

In the tropical section, the light is green and filtered. Water drips from leaves. In the desert room, the air is dry and bright, and cacti and succulents stand in sandy beds. Many of these plants store water in thick stems or leaves to survive long droughts. Look closely at the spines. They are not decoration. They shade the plant, collect moisture, and protect it from hungry animals.

Back outside, the open garden follows a calmer rhythm. There are formal beds, a pond, and large old trees that throw deep shade in summer. Look at the small labels at the base of the plants. Each one gives a scientific name in Latin. That system was shaped in the eighteenth century, partly by botanists who visited and studied in Dutch gardens. The labels are a quiet reminder that this place is still a scientific collection, not just a park.

There is also a butterfly house, warm and close, where tropical butterflies drift between flowers. Move slowly there. They may land on your sleeve. Afterwards, many visitors rest at the café in the old orangery, a building once used to shelter citrus trees and tender plants through the Dutch winter. Sitting there with a coffee, you may remember that coffee story, and smile at the coincidence.

When you leave, look around the neighbourhood. The Plantage is a district of wide streets, old trees, and handsome nineteenth century buildings. The Artis zoo is close by. So are reminders of the area's Jewish history, which was devastated during the Second World War. The garden sits quietly in the middle of all this, a green pause in a city of canals and brick.

Before you go, stop near the entrance and listen again. The trams, the birds, the gravel underfoot. For nearly four centuries, people have walked these paths to learn about plants — to heal, to trade, to wonder. Look once more at the nearest tree or flower bed. It carries a long line of human care behind it. Then step back out into the city, a little greener than when you came.

Practical tips

Move slowly in the butterfly house so butterflies settle.

Combine your visit with a walk through the Plantage district.
