The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the cork oaks around you, and the dry grass ticks softly in the heat. Underfoot, the red-brown earth of the Alentejo is packed hard and scattered with small stones. Ahead, on a gentle slope, dozens of rounded granite blocks stand in loose rows. Some are as tall as a person. Some are smaller, like a crowd of figures that has gathered and then fallen silent. This is the Almendres Cromlech, and it is older than almost anything else you will see in Europe.

A cromlech is a circle or enclosure of standing stones. Each single stone is called a menhir, from old Breton words meaning long stone. Here, roughly ninety-five menhirs survive, which makes this the largest group of its kind on the Iberian Peninsula. It is also one of the largest in all of Europe. The stones are local granite, shaped and set upright by farming communities of the Neolithic, the late Stone Age, when people first settled the land and began to herd and plant.

Here is the first surprise. Archaeologists believe the earliest phase of this site goes back to around the sixth millennium before Christ. That is roughly eight thousand years ago. The monument was then changed and extended over many centuries, perhaps into the fourth and third millennia. Put simply, people were raising stones on this hillside long before the famous circle at Stonehenge in England took its final shape. You are standing in one of the oldest ceremonial places on the continent.

Look at how the stones are arranged. They do not form one neat ring. Instead, researchers describe two connected enclosures, built at different times. The older one is smaller and roughly circular. The later one is larger and more oval, and it runs down the slope. The whole layout faces east, toward the rising sun. Many of the stones have been moved, toppled or re-erected over the millennia, so the pattern you see today is partly a reconstruction of the original plan.

That eastward direction matters. Some researchers suggest the monument was linked to the movement of the sun and the moon. One popular idea connects the site to sunrise at the equinoxes, the two days each year when day and night are of equal length. These astronomical readings are debated, and nobody can say for certain what the builders intended. Still, it is worth noticing how open the eastern view is, and how the slope seems to lift the stones toward the morning sky.

Now walk closer to a few of the menhirs. On several of them, you may find small round hollows carved into the surface. These are called cup marks. On others there are faint lines, circles, and shapes that some scholars read as shepherd's crooks, or as sun-like rays. A few stones have a rounded top and a narrower body, and some visitors see a human outline in them. The carvings are worn and shallow, so slow down and let your eyes adjust.

The best way to see these carvings is with low, angled light. Early in the morning or late in the afternoon, shadows fall across the granite and the engravings appear sharper. At midday, under the flat Alentejo sun, the same marks can almost disappear. Run your eyes along the surface rather than looking straight at it. It is a small trick, but it often turns a plain-looking stone into a carved one.

Here is the second surprise. For thousands of years, this monument sat almost unnoticed among the trees. It was only identified for science in the 1960s, during work on the geological map of the region. The researcher who recognised it was mapping rocks, not searching for ancient temples. Before that, local farmers and shepherds surely knew the stones were here, but the wider world did not. A site this large stayed quiet simply because the landscape kept it.

That landscape is a key part of the experience. Around you stretches the montado, the traditional Alentejo mix of cork oaks, holm oaks and open pasture. Cork is harvested by hand, stripped from the trunk every several years. Look at the nearby trees. Some may show a darker, bare lower trunk, sometimes painted with a number. That number tells farmers when the bark was last cut. It is a living tradition, carried on beside a monument made by the region's very first farmers.

The cromlech is not alone. A short drive away, along a separate track, stands the Almendres Menhir, a single tall stone among the olive and cork trees. Some researchers think it was placed in relation to the main enclosure, perhaps as part of a wider sacred landscape. The whole area around the city of Évora is in fact dense with prehistoric monuments, including dolmens, which are stone burial chambers. This hillside was clearly important, not just one isolated site.

What was this place for? The honest answer is that we do not know. There are no written records from the people who built it. Archaeologists suggest it was a ritual and gathering place, possibly tied to the seasons, the sky, and the rhythms of farming. Communities may have met here to mark key moments of the year. The questions stay open, and that uncertainty is part of the power of the site. You are free to stand here and wonder.

As you walk among the stones, treat them gently. They have survived for thousands of years, but they are not indestructible. Climbing on them causes wear, and the carvings are already faint. Stay on the paths where they are marked. Take a moment, too, to step back from the centre and see the cromlech as a whole. From a little distance, the rows of granite stand out against the trees, and the shape of the enclosures becomes clearer.

Before you leave, pause once more and listen. The wind is still moving through the oaks. The light is shifting slowly across the grey granite and the red earth. Somewhere nearby, you may hear birds, or the far-off sound of a car on the dirt road. The stones in front of you were raised by hands that worked this same soil, under this same sky. As you walk back toward the trees, glance over your shoulder. The quiet crowd of stones will still be standing there, facing the morning.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning or late afternoon for clearer carvings.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven dirt paths.
Bring water and sun protection; shade is limited.
Never climb on the stones or touch carvings.
Drive slowly; the access road is unpaved.
