The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here there is almost nothing to hear: the wind moving over flat ground, the tick of small stones settling, perhaps a bird far off. The earth under your shoes is hard, pale and gravelly, with a light crust of sand. The horizon runs in every direction without a single tree to break it. It is hard to believe that this quiet patch of the Libyan desert once gave its name to a bridge and a metro station in Paris.

The name itself is simple. In Arabic, bir means a well. For centuries this spot mattered for one plain reason: water. Travellers, herders and caravans crossing the desert south of the coast needed places like this to survive. Long before any army arrived, Bir Hakeim was a waypoint, a small dot of life in a very large and very dry landscape. Keep that in mind as you look around. The desert has always decided who could pass through here.

In 1942, that old logic of water and position pulled the Second World War to this exact ground. The British Eighth Army had built a long defensive line running south from the coast near Gazala, west of Tobruk. It was made of fortified boxes, strong points linked by huge minefields. Bir Hakeim sat at the far southern end of that line. It was the corner, the hinge, the place where the defences simply stopped and the open desert began.

Holding that corner was a brigade of Free French soldiers. These were the men who had refused to accept France's surrender in 1940 and chose to keep fighting alongside Britain. Their commander was General Marie-Pierre Koenig. His force numbered a few thousand men, and it was remarkably mixed. There were Foreign Legionnaires, marines, and colonial troops from Africa and from the Pacific islands. Many of them had travelled across half the world to dig into this gravel.

At the end of May 1942, the German commander Erwin Rommel launched his offensive. His plan was to swing around the southern end of the British line, and that meant going past, or through, Bir Hakeim. Italian armoured units struck the position first. The attack broke against the French defences and the minefields. What followed was not a quick action. It became a siege that lasted around two weeks, from late May into the second week of June.

Try to picture the conditions. The defenders lived in trenches and dugouts scraped out of this hard ground. Water, the very thing that once made this place valuable, was now strictly rationed. Dust from shellfire and moving vehicles hung in the air. The heat during the day was heavy, and the nights could turn cold. Day after day, German and Italian artillery and dive bombers hammered the position. And still the garrison held its ground.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Rommel had assumed the position would fall quickly. Instead, the French resistance cost him time, fuel and men at a critical moment. Historians still debate exactly how much this mattered. But many argue that the delay at Bir Hakeim helped British forces pull back in better order toward Egypt, where the decisive battles at El Alamein were fought later that year.

By the night of the tenth of June, the position could no longer be held. Supplies were nearly gone, and the ring around the garrison was tightening. So Koenig ordered a breakout. In darkness, the French tried to drive and walk out through gaps in the enemy lines and the minefields. It was chaotic and costly. Many men were killed or captured. But a large part of the brigade reached British lines and lived to fight again.

One of the drivers that night is remembered for a second surprise. Susan Travers was a British-born woman serving with the Free French, and she drove General Koenig's vehicle during the breakout, under fire, through the dark. She later became the only woman officially enrolled in the French Foreign Legion. Her story is a reminder that the people at Bir Hakeim were more varied than any simple war film would suggest.

For France, the battle carried a meaning far larger than its size. After the defeat of 1940, French pride was badly hurt. Bir Hakeim showed that French soldiers were fighting, and fighting hard, on the Allied side. The name travelled home quickly. In Paris, a bridge across the Seine near the Eiffel Tower and a metro station both carry the name Bir-Hakeim today. Millions of Parisians say these words without knowing they point here.

Now look again at the ground around you. The fortifications were never grand buildings. They were trenches, gun pits and low walls of piled stone and earth. Desert wind and the decades since have softened most of them. In places you may still see shallow depressions, scattered stones or old lines in the soil. Simple memorials mark the site and honour those who fought and died here. They are modest, and that suits the place.

A practical word of care. This is open desert, far from the coast and from Tobruk, and the area saw heavy mine-laying during the war. Not every remnant has been cleared. Stay on established tracks, follow your local guide closely, and never pick up metal objects from the ground. Travel in Libya also requires planning and local arrangements. The emptiness that makes the site so moving also means help is a long way off.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Notice how far you can see, and how little there is to stop the eye. That flatness is why armies wanted this corner, and why holding it was so hard. Feel the wind on your face and the gravel under your feet. Thousands of men from many countries knew this same view. As you walk back to your vehicle, the quiet will follow you.
