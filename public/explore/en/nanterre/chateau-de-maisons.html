The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel shifts under your shoes, and somewhere beyond the trees the Seine moves slowly past. Pale stone rises in front of you, cream in the shade and almost white in full sun. The roofs are steep and dark, covered in grey slate. Everything here feels measured and calm, as if each window was placed by someone holding a ruler and a great deal of patience.

A quick note on where you are standing. This château is not inside Nanterre itself. It sits in the town of Maisons-Laffitte, a little further west along the Seine and on the same RER A line. Many visitors combine the two in a single day. So if your map shows Nanterre, you are only a short ride away, and the journey along the river is part of the story.

The man behind this building was François Mansart, one of the most important French architects of the seventeenth century. His name lives on in an everyday word. The mansard roof, with its steep lower slope and windows set into it, carries his name in English and in French. Look up at the roofline now. You are seeing the kind of design that made him famous.

Mansart built the château for René de Longueil, a powerful magistrate in the Parlement of Paris who later served as superintendent of finances. Work began around 1640 and continued through the following decade. Longueil wanted a country house close to the royal residences of Saint-Germain-en-Laye. Being near the court mattered. A great house this close to the king was both a home and a statement of ambition.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. Mansart had a reputation for being difficult, and for changing his mind. According to a well-known story, he tore down finished parts of the building because they did not satisfy him, and started again. Whether every detail of that tale is true is hard to say. But his clients clearly gave him unusual freedom, and the result is a building where almost nothing feels accidental.

Step back and look at the whole façade. Notice how the central block comes forward slightly, and how the wings balance each other on either side. The columns follow the classical orders, the system of column styles inherited from ancient Greece and Rome. The simplest style sits lower down, and the more decorative styles appear higher up. It is a quiet lesson in proportion, written in stone.

Look also at the ground around the house. A dry moat runs along the base, a ditch with no water in it. It was never really for defence. By this period, a moat had become a sign of noble status, a memory of medieval castles. Here it lifts the house slightly and makes it seem to float above the lawns, which is exactly the effect Mansart wanted.

Inside, the vestibule is one of the great rooms of French architecture. Pale stone covers the walls and the ceiling, with carved eagles and sculpted figures above. There is very little colour. The beauty comes from carving, light, and shadow. Then comes the staircase, which rises around an open centre. Many architects still study it today as a model of how stone, space, and movement can work together.

Now for a second surprise. In the 1720s, a young writer called Voltaire fell seriously ill with smallpox while staying here. He survived, and went on to become one of the most famous thinkers of the French Enlightenment, the eighteenth century movement that championed reason and free debate. Later accounts say a fire broke out in his room soon after he left. It is a strange footnote, and a reminder that great houses were also places of ordinary human danger.

The house passed through many hands. In the late eighteenth century it belonged to the Count of Artois, the younger brother of Louis the Sixteenth, who later became King Charles the Tenth. He brought new interior decoration in the fashion of his time. Then came the Revolution, and after it, Napoleon's era. One of Napoleon's marshals, Jean Lannes, owned the estate for a time before his death in battle.

The name of the town comes from the next owner. Jacques Laffitte was a banker and politician who bought the estate in the early nineteenth century. In the 1830s, short of money, he divided the enormous park into plots and sold them for villas. That is how a royal hunting landscape became a residential town. Maisons became Maisons-Laffitte, and horses arrived too. The town is still known today for racehorses and its racecourse.

At the start of the twentieth century, the house was close to being lost to development. The French state bought it in 1905, and it has been protected as a historic monument ever since. Today it is cared for by the national body that looks after France's historic buildings. Much of the original park has gone, but the house itself still stands on its slight rise above the river, as it did when Mansart finished it.

Before you leave, take one more slow look. Follow the line of the roof against the sky. Notice how the stone changes colour as clouds pass over. Listen again for the gravel, the birds, the distant trains. As you walk away through the streets of villas around you, remember that each of these gardens was once part of the same great estate, all of it built around this one calm, careful house.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the RER A west from Nanterre to Maisons-Laffitte.
Combine the visit with a walk along the Seine.
Check interior access before travelling, rooms may close.
Bring comfortable shoes for the gravel paths and villas.
Visit on a sunny day to see the stone glow.
