The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Footsteps on a wide stone deck. A rolling suitcase clicks over the joints between the slabs. Somewhere below your feet, out of sight, trains and traffic move through tunnels. Above you, two pale towers rise side by side, their glass catching whatever the sky offers today. This is Cœur Défense, the heart of La Défense, the business district just west of Paris. The name means exactly that. It sits at the centre of a district that stretches across several towns, Nanterre among them.

Before we look up, look down. The ground you are standing on is not really the ground. La Défense was built on a vast raised platform, a deck of concrete laid over roads, railway lines and car parks. Planners in the mid twentieth century wanted to separate people from cars completely. Pedestrians would walk on top. Vehicles would move underneath. That simple idea shaped everything here, including the building in front of you. Its entrances face the open esplanade, not a street.

Now let your eyes travel up the two towers. They are twins, nearly identical, standing close together like two pages of an open book. Their ends are rounded rather than sharp. That softening matters more than it seems. Many older towers in the district are hard-edged blocks. Here the corners curve away, so the light slides across the facade instead of stopping at a crisp edge. As you walk around, the towers seem to change shape slightly.

The complex was designed by the French architect Jean-Paul Viguier and completed around the turn of the millennium. It is more than just the two tall towers. At their feet sits a long, lower building, and the whole ensemble is linked by a grand interior hall. Seen from the esplanade, it reads as one composition, not as separate buildings that happen to share a plot.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. Cœur Défense is not famous for being the tallest tower in La Défense. Its neighbours rise higher. Its distinction is size of another kind. By floor area, it has often been described as one of the largest office complexes in Europe. Spread its floors out flat and they would cover an area far greater than its footprint suggests. Thousands of people work inside on a typical weekday.

Think about what that means at nine in the morning. The metro and suburban trains arrive beneath the deck. Crowds rise up the escalators and spread across the esplanade. A large share of them flow straight towards these doors. In the evening the tide reverses. If you stand here during those hours, you can watch the building breathe in and breathe out its working population, almost like a small town filling and emptying each day.

The second surprise is financial rather than architectural. In 2007 the complex was bought in a deal linked to the American bank Lehman Brothers. At the time it was reported as one of the most expensive property purchases ever made in Europe. Within a year, Lehman Brothers collapsed, and the global financial crisis began. The ownership of Cœur Défense then became tangled in long legal disputes. For years this calm glass building sat at the centre of a very noisy argument.

That story tells you something about La Défense as a whole. These towers are not only places of work. They are also investments, traded between funds and banks like large, heavy assets. Cœur Défense changed hands again in later years. The people at their desks inside may never have noticed. The lights stayed on, the lifts kept running, and the coffee machines kept working while the owners changed far above their heads.

Let us return to the physical building. Step back far enough to see the full height of both towers. Notice the horizontal lines that run across the facades, floor after floor. They give the towers a calm, layered rhythm, like the lines on a sheet of writing paper. On a grey Parisian day the glass turns silver and soft. On a clear day it can reflect the blue sky and the neighbouring towers, so the building seems to hold the district inside its skin.

The area around the base deserves a slow walk. La Défense is known for its open-air art, with sculptures and murals placed across the esplanade and its edges. Some are bold splashes of colour against all the grey stone and glass. Wander a little and you will find benches, planted areas, and quieter corners away from the main flow of commuters. On warm days, office workers eat lunch outside, and the deck feels more like a public square than a corporate plaza.

A practical word about access. Cœur Défense is a working office complex, not a museum. The interior is mainly for the companies and staff who work there, and security controls access beyond the entrance areas. The best way to appreciate it is from outside, from the esplanade and from a distance. That is also where the architecture makes the most sense, because the towers were designed to be read against the wider skyline.

If you have time, walk towards the Grande Arche, the huge hollow cube that closes the far end of the district. From there, turn and look back. You will see the long axis of La Défense, with Cœur Défense among the towers along its line. That axis continues all the way to the Arc de Triomphe and the centre of Paris. This straight line was a deliberate choice, tying the modern business district to the historic city.

So take one last look at the twin towers in front of you. Notice how the light sits on the curved glass right now, at this hour, on this day. Listen again to the footsteps and the wheels on the stone, and to the quiet hum coming up from below the deck. As you walk away, glance back once. From each new angle, the two towers line up a little differently. Watch them slowly come apart and close together again as you go.
