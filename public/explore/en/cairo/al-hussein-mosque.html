The guide

The story

Listen first. Pigeons lift off the stone square in a sudden clatter of wings. Somewhere a tray of tea glasses chimes as a waiter weaves between tables. Then, rising above the noise of the bazaar, the call to prayer rolls out across the plaza. You are standing at the edge of Khan el-Khalili, in the old heart of Cairo. In front of you is Al-Hussein Mosque, one of the most loved places of worship in all of Egypt.

Look at the building for a moment before we talk about it. The walls are pale stone, almost cream in the sunlight. The windows end in pointed arches. Along the roofline runs a row of small decorative crenellations, like the top of a castle wall. If something about it feels faintly European, you are not imagining it. Much of what you see today was rebuilt in the nineteenth century in a Gothic Revival style, a fashion borrowed from medieval European church architecture.

But the story of this place begins far earlier, in the mid-twelfth century. Cairo was then ruled by the Fatimids, a dynasty that founded this part of the city. At that time, Crusader armies were pressing along the coast of the eastern Mediterranean. According to the tradition, a sacred relic was moved from the coastal city of Ascalon to Cairo for safekeeping. That relic, many believe, is the head of Husayn, grandson of the Prophet Muhammad.

Who was Husayn? For listeners outside the Islamic tradition, here is the short version. Husayn was the son of Ali and Fatima, the Prophet's daughter. In the year 680, he was killed at the Battle of Karbala, in present-day Iraq. His death became one of the most mourned events in Islamic history. For Shia Muslims it is central to their faith. For Sunni Muslims, who are the large majority in Egypt, he is also deeply honoured as a member of the Prophet's family.

Here is the first surprise. Cairo is not the only city that claims to hold Husayn's head. Shrines in Damascus and in Karbala carry similar traditions, and historians have debated the question for centuries. Nobody can settle it with certainty. Yet for the people who come here, the debate hardly matters. Locals often speak of Husayn as a living presence in the neighbourhood. They greet him, ask for his help, and call this mosque simply "Sayyidna al-Hussein" — our master Hussein.

Notice who is around you. There are families from the countryside in their best clothes. There are old men with prayer beads and students from the university next door. You may see women touching the outer walls or murmuring a quiet prayer at the doorway. This is not a museum. It is a working shrine, and the feeling of personal devotion is strong. Visitors are welcome, but the mosque belongs first to the people who pray here.

Now turn your eyes across the road. The large complex facing the square is Al-Azhar Mosque, founded by the same Fatimid rulers more than a thousand years ago. It grew into one of the oldest centres of learning in the world, and it remains a major university today. The two mosques face each other almost like partners. One is a place of scholarship, the other a place of heartfelt devotion. Between them lies the busy crossroads of old Cairo.

Look up at the plaza beside the mosque. You may see tall steel posts topped with folded white canopies. On hot days or crowded festival nights, these open like giant umbrellas to shade the worshippers below. They are a modern addition, and they look surprisingly elegant against the old stone. When they are open, the square becomes an outdoor prayer hall. On a busy Friday, rows of worshippers can stretch far beyond the doors.

Here is the second surprise. Walk along the side of the building and look for an older doorway with a very different character. It is often called the Green Gate, and it survives from a much earlier phase of the shrine than the walls around it. Its carved stonework is older, plainer, and more weathered. Many visitors pass it without a second glance. Yet it is one of the few visible links to the medieval mosque that stood here before the rebuilding.

If you are here during the Islamic month of Ramadan, the whole area changes after sunset. Lanterns glow above the alleys. Families break their fast together and then stay late into the night. The square fills with talk, music, and the smell of grilled corn and sweet pastries. Another major moment is the Mawlid of Husayn, a festival that honours his birth. Sufi groups gather, chanting and swaying, and crowds pour in from across Egypt.

Before you leave the square, follow the narrow lane into the bazaar. Just a few steps away is El Fishawy, one of the oldest coffeehouses in Cairo. Its walls are lined with old mirrors in heavy wooden frames. Waiters carry mint tea, thick coffee, and water pipes through a crowd of locals and travellers. Over the years, writers and artists have come here to talk and think. Sitting down for a glass of tea is one of the best ways to watch the life of this square.

If you wish to go inside the mosque, a little care goes a long way. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. Women should bring a scarf for their hair. You will remove your shoes at the entrance. Try to avoid the main prayer times, when the hall is full and visitors can feel out of place. Access to the inner shrine area may be separate for men and women, so follow the guidance of the attendants at the door.

Inside, the light is softer. Carpets cushion your steps, and the sound of the bazaar fades to a low hum. Worshippers sit against the columns reading the Quran. Near the shrine, people often stand quietly with raised hands. Whatever you believe, it is worth pausing here for a moment. You are in a room where generations of Egyptians have brought their grief, their hopes, and their thanks.

Step back out into the light now. Feel the warm stone under your feet. Look once more at those pointed windows, the cream walls, the small crenellations against the sky. Listen for the tea glasses, the pigeons, and the voices of the bazaar. As you walk into the lanes of Khan el-Khalili, glance back over your shoulder. The minaret will still be there, rising above the crowd — a quiet marker for the heart of old Cairo.
