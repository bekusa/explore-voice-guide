The guide

The story

Outside, Port Said Street roars with car horns and the hiss of buses. Then you pass through the doors, and the noise drops away. The air feels cooler here. Soft light falls across brass, glass, and old carved wood. For a moment, the loudest thing in the room is the sound of your own footsteps on the stone floor.

Welcome to the Museum of Islamic Art in Cairo. It stands at Bab al-Khalq, a short walk west of the lanes of Khan el-Khalili and the old Fatimid city. Many visitors spend a morning in the market and never learn that one of the largest collections of Islamic art anywhere sits just a few streets away. It holds objects from Egypt, Syria, Iran, Turkey, Spain, and far beyond.

Look back at the building from the street before you go deeper inside. The facade follows a style called neo-Mamluk. That means it borrows the look of buildings from the Mamluk period, when soldier-sultans ruled Egypt from the thirteenth to the early sixteenth century. You can see the pointed arches, the carved stone bands, and the striped decoration. The design is a tribute. It was built in the early twentieth century, but it speaks the visual language of medieval Cairo.

The story of the collection begins earlier, in the late nineteenth century. At that time, old mosques and houses across Cairo were falling into ruin. Carved panels, lamps, and inscriptions were being lost or sold abroad. A group of officials and scholars began to gather these pieces to protect them. For years, the objects were kept in a part of the old Mosque of al-Hakim, near the northern gates of the medieval city.

In 1903, the collection moved into this building. For many decades it shared the structure with the national library, which used the upper floor. Back then the institution had a different name, the Museum of Arab Art. In the early 1950s it took its present name. The new name reflected a wider view. The objects here come from many peoples, languages, and empires — not only Arab ones.

Now, the first surprise. This calm, ordered building has survived real violence. In January 2014, a large car bomb exploded outside, aimed at the police headquarters across the street. The blast shattered the museum facade and destroyed or damaged many objects inside. Conservators spent the next three years cleaning, gluing, and rebuilding thousands of fragments. The museum reopened in 2017. When you look closely at some glass and ceramic pieces, you may see fine lines where they were joined back together.

The galleries are arranged in two broad ways. One path follows time, moving through the great dynasties that ruled Egypt — the Umayyads, the Abbasids, the Fatimids, the Ayyubids, the Mamluks, and the Ottomans. Another path follows themes, such as science, writing, gardens, and daily life. You do not need to follow a strict route. Many people simply drift from case to case, and that works well here.

Look for the glass mosque lamps. Most come from the Mamluk period. They have a wide neck, a round body, and a narrow foot. Craftsmen painted them with coloured enamel and gold, often in blue, red, and white. Many carry lines of Arabic script. A common choice was the Verse of Light from the Quran, which compares God to a lamp. These lamps once hung on chains in mosques across Cairo, and oil and a wick burned inside them.

The woodwork is just as important. Fatimid craftsmen, who worked here between the tenth and twelfth centuries, carved panels with hunters, musicians, birds, and animals. This often surprises visitors, who expect Islamic art to avoid living figures. In religious spaces, figures were rare. But in palaces and homes, artists showed people and animals with real joy. Keep an eye out for the wooden prayer niches too. A prayer niche, called a mihrab, shows the direction of Mecca.

Here is a second surprise. The museum shows how closely art and science grew together in the Islamic world. Look for astrolabes. An astrolabe is a flat brass instrument that looks a little like a clock. Astronomers and travellers used it to measure the height of the stars and the sun. With it, they could find the time of day, and the direction of prayer. Each one is both a tool and a work of fine metalwork.

The ceramics show how ideas travelled. You will see lustreware, a kind of pottery with a metallic shine, made by firing the glaze in a special way. You will also see tiles from Iznik in Turkey, with deep blue, turquoise, and a bright tomato red. Some pieces copy Chinese porcelain. Merchants carried goods along trade routes from China to Egypt, and potters here tried to match the white and blue designs they admired.

As you move through the rooms, notice the screens of turned wood called mashrabiya. They are made of hundreds of small wooden pieces fitted into a lattice. In old Cairo houses, these screens covered windows. They let breeze and light into the room, and they let people inside look out without being seen. Stand behind one and look through it. The light breaks into small points, and the gallery beyond turns into a pattern of shadow and brightness.

The manuscripts and calligraphy deserve slow attention. Arabic script was the highest art form in much of the Islamic world. You will see Qurans with gold letters, decorated page borders, and careful margins. Notice how the same script appears everywhere — on paper, on metal bowls, on lamps, on stone, and in wood. Writing was not only for reading. It was also decoration, and a way to place blessing and meaning on everyday objects.

Before you leave, pause for a moment near the entrance. Think about the journey of these objects — from mosque floors and palace walls, to an old mosque courtyard, to this building, through a bomb blast, and back onto clean shelves. Then step outside into the noise and heat of Port Said Street. As you walk back toward Khan el-Khalili, look up at the old doorways, lamps, and carved windows above the shops. Some of their cousins are behind you now, safe in the quiet galleries.

Key facts

What to look for

Enamelled Mamluk glass lamps with gold Arabic script.
Fatimid wood panels carved with hunters and musicians.
Brass astrolabes engraved with star names and scales.
Iznik tiles in cobalt blue and tomato red.
Mashrabiya screens scattering light into small points.
Before you go

Practical tips

Pair your visit with Khan el-Khalili, a short walk east.
Visit in the morning before the street traffic peaks.
Follow the dynasty galleries if you prefer a timeline.
Allow at least two unhurried hours for the galleries.
Check bag and photography rules at the entrance desk.
