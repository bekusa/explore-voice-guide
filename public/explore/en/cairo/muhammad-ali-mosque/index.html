The guide

The story

Feel the change underfoot first. The dusty stone of the Citadel gives way to smooth, cool paving. Then lift your eyes to pale walls that glow a soft milky white, almost like candle wax. Two needle-thin minarets rise above a grey cascade of domes. Somewhere below, the traffic of Cairo hums. Up here, the air feels lighter, and the sound of the city arrives softened, as if from very far away.

You are standing at the Muhammad Ali Mosque, on the highest point of the Citadel of Cairo. Many visitors call it the Alabaster Mosque, and the reason is all around you. The lower walls, inside and out, are clad in alabaster, a soft translucent stone that takes on the colour of the light. In the morning it looks cream. Late in the day it can turn honey and gold.

The man behind this building was Muhammad Ali Pasha. He was an Albanian-born commander who arrived in Egypt with the Ottoman army and rose to rule the country from 1805. He set out to modernise Egypt — its army, its schools, its industry — and to found a dynasty. His family went on to rule Egypt for well over a century. This mosque was his statement, built where the whole city would see it.

Construction began around 1830 and the main work finished in the late 1840s. Decoration continued for some years after that. The design is widely credited to an architect from Istanbul. And that is the first surprise for many visitors. This mosque does not look Egyptian at all. It looks Ottoman — like the great imperial mosques along the Bosphorus, with one large central dome supported by half-domes stepping down around it.

That choice was deliberate. For centuries, Cairo's mosques followed local traditions — carved stone domes, square minarets, deep courtyards. Muhammad Ali was technically a governor under the Ottoman sultan, yet he was also his rival. By building in the capital's own imperial style, he placed himself on equal terms with Istanbul. Look at those pencil-thin minarets. In Ottoman tradition, that slender form was associated with imperial patrons, not ordinary builders.

The ground beneath your feet carries older layers too. The Citadel was founded in the late twelfth century by Salah al-Din, the sultan known in Europe as Saladin. For roughly seven hundred years, it was the seat of power in Egypt. Earlier palaces of the Mamluks — the soldier-sultans who ruled Egypt for centuries — once stood near this spot. Muhammad Ali cleared much of what remained to make room for his mosque.

That connection to the Mamluks has a dark edge. In 1811, Muhammad Ali invited the leading Mamluk commanders to a ceremony here at the Citadel. As they rode out through a narrow passage, the gates closed and his troops opened fire. Most of them were killed. The event ended Mamluk power in Egypt for good. It is worth remembering that this calm, pale building rose only a generation later, on the same hill.

Now walk into the open courtyard. It is wide and surrounded by arcades topped with small domes. In the centre stands the ablution fountain, where worshippers wash before prayer. Its carved roof and ornate canopy are pure Ottoman Baroque — a style that mixed Islamic forms with European curves and flourishes. Notice how the decoration here feels lighter and more playful than the heavy stone of older Cairo.

At one end of the courtyard rises a small clock tower. This is the second surprise. The clock was a gift from King Louis-Philippe of France in the 1840s. In return, Egypt sent France an ancient obelisk from Luxor, which still stands in the Place de la Concorde in Paris. Locals like to say the clock barely kept time from the day it arrived. Whether or not that is fair, many people feel Egypt got the worse side of that exchange.

Before you enter the prayer hall, you will need to remove your shoes. Step inside, and the scale changes completely. The central dome floats high above you, painted and gilded, with rows of small windows around its base. Hundreds of glass lamps hang on long chains, arranged in great circles close above head height. When they are lit, the hall glows. Even in daylight, the effect is soft and layered.

To your right as you enter, behind an ornate bronze grille, lies the tomb of Muhammad Ali himself. He died in 1849 and was buried inside the mosque he built. The cenotaph is white marble, carved with delicate floral patterns. It is a striking choice. Most rulers of Cairo built separate tomb chambers. He chose to rest under his own dome, at the centre of the city he had reshaped.

Look up again at the dome and think about its weight. In the twentieth century, the structure developed serious problems, and the dome had to be extensively restored. What you see today is the result of that careful work. Look too for the two pulpits. One is the tall original, richly gilded wood. The other, smaller one in alabaster, was added much later, during the reign of Egypt's twentieth-century kings.

When you step back outside, walk to the terrace along the edge of the Citadel. This is one of the finest viewpoints in Cairo. Below you spread the domes and minarets of Islamic Cairo, layer upon layer, dating back many centuries. On a clear day you may even pick out the pyramids of Giza on the far horizon, faint shapes beyond the haze. It is the whole story of the city in one long view.

Before you leave, turn around once more. Watch how the light moves across the alabaster, how the grey domes catch the sky. Listen for the call to prayer, which may rise from this mosque and be answered by others far below. This building was made to be seen from everywhere in Cairo. Now, as you walk away down the hill, notice how often it appears again — above rooftops, between buildings, still watching over the city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring socks; shoes come off before entering the prayer hall.
Women should carry a scarf to cover their hair inside.
Visit late afternoon for warmer light on the alabaster walls.
Avoid visiting during Friday midday prayers.
Walk to the Citadel terrace for the widest view over Cairo.
