The guide

The story

Start with the steps. They are worn smooth, and they rise from the street toward a round building of pale stone. Below you, the noise of Cairo fades into something quieter. You hear pigeons, low voices, and the soft scrape of shoes. Inside, the air smells of candle wax and incense. This is the Church of Saint George, and it sits on one of the oldest foundations in the whole city.

The church stands in the district many visitors call Coptic Cairo. Locals often call it Mari Girgis, after the saint, and the nearby metro station carries the same name. This small quarter holds churches, a synagogue, and a mosque within a short walk of each other. People have lived and prayed here for a very long time. The narrow lanes stay cooler than the wide roads outside, and the walls rise close on both sides.

Here is the first surprise. The church is built on top of a Roman tower. In the Roman era, this area held a fortress called Babylon. The name has nothing to do with the famous Babylon in Iraq. It was a military stronghold that guarded the river and the routes into Egypt. Its massive walls once stood beside the Nile, which has since shifted its course to the west. Parts of the old fortress still survive around you today.

That explains the unusual shape. Most churches follow a long rectangle, with a nave leading to an altar. This one is round. The builders followed the circular outline of the Roman tower beneath them. So when you walk inside, you are standing on stone laid by soldiers of an empire that ruled here roughly two thousand years ago. A modern church, an ancient foundation, and one continuous footprint.

The building you see today is not the original church. An older church stood on this spot for centuries. In the early twentieth century, a fire destroyed most of it. The community rebuilt the church soon after, and the present structure dates mainly from that rebuilding. So the stones under your feet are ancient, but much of what rises above them is little more than a century old. Both layers belong to the story.

This church belongs to the Greek Orthodox community, not the Coptic Church. That is the second surprise for many visitors, who assume every church here is Coptic. The Greek Orthodox Patriarchate of Alexandria is one of the oldest Christian institutions in the world. Greek-speaking communities lived in Egypt for many centuries, especially in Alexandria and Cairo. You may notice Greek lettering on icons and inscriptions as you move through the space.

Now, the saint himself. Saint George is one of the most widely honoured figures in Christianity. Tradition holds that he was a soldier in the Roman army who refused to give up his faith. According to the story, he was tortured and executed in the early fourth century, during a period of persecution. He is often shown on horseback, killing a dragon. That dragon story is legend, not history, but it became one of the best-known images in Christian art.

Step inside and let your eyes adjust. The interior is dim, lit by candles, hanging lamps, and coloured glass. Light falls through stained glass windows in patches of red, blue, and gold. Icons cover the walls — flat, painted images of saints with gold backgrounds. In the Orthodox tradition, an icon is not just decoration. Believers see it as a window toward the holy figure it shows, so people kiss them and light candles before them.

Watch the people around you for a moment. Some visitors come as tourists with cameras. Others come as worshippers, and they move differently. They touch an icon, cross themselves, and stand quietly with a candle. Saint George is honoured by many Egyptians, and some people of other faiths also visit his shrines to ask for help or healing. This mix of visitors is part of the character of the place.

The area beside the church holds more history. A Greek Orthodox monastery connects to the church, and nearby lanes lead to a Coptic convent also dedicated to Saint George. The two sites share a saint but belong to different churches. It is easy to confuse them, so check the signs as you walk. The whole quarter works like a small archive of the many Christian traditions that took root in Egypt.

Saint George has an annual feast day, and the church becomes much busier around that time. Pilgrims arrive, families gather, and the lanes fill with people. On an ordinary day, the mood is calmer. You can sit for a few minutes, listen to the murmur of prayers, and watch the candle flames move in the draft from the door. Small moments like this often stay with visitors longer than any date or name.

As you leave, look at the walls of the old fortress nearby. The large blocks and the lines of red brick show Roman building methods. Those layers of brick and stone helped the walls stand for centuries. Then look back at the round church sitting on top. One building speaks of empire and defence. The other speaks of faith and memory. Here, one simply grew on top of the other.

Take a final moment on the steps before you go. Notice the curve of the walls, the smell of incense still on your clothes, and the quiet of the lane below. Cairo is waiting just beyond these streets, loud and fast. But here, under your feet, there are many centuries of stone. As you walk away, listen for the change in sound — the moment the old quarter ends and the modern city begins again.
