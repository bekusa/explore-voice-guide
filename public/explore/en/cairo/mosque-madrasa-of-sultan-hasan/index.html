The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the great portal and put your palm against the stone. It is cool, even in the Cairo heat. Above you, the doorway climbs higher than most buildings on this square. Car horns and traffic noise drift up from Salah al-Din Square below. Then you step inside, and the sound of the city falls away behind thick walls.

This is the Mosque-Madrasa of Sultan Hasan. It was built in the middle of the fourteenth century, under the Mamluks. The Mamluks were a ruling class of soldiers, many of them originally enslaved and trained for war, who governed Egypt for more than two centuries. Construction began in the 1350s. Even in a city with hundreds of medieval monuments, this one is on a different scale. Many historians count it among the most ambitious buildings of the medieval Islamic world.

The timing makes that ambition harder to explain. Only a few years earlier, the Black Death had swept through Cairo. The plague killed a huge share of the city's people. Yet the young Sultan an-Nasir Hasan put enormous wealth into this project. One explanation is grim and practical. The plague left many estates without heirs, and much of that property passed to the state. A city that had lost so many people produced, in part, the funding for one of its largest buildings.

Now look back at the way you came in. The entrance does not lead straight to the courtyard. Instead, a dark, turning passage carries you through the building. This bent entrance was common in Mamluk architecture. It hides the interior from the street and controls how you arrive. You move from bright sun into shadow, and then out again into open sky. The effect is deliberate. The courtyard opens in front of you all at once.

The plan is simple to read once you know it. Four enormous vaulted halls, called iwans, face the central courtyard from four sides. An iwan is a large room closed on three sides and fully open on the fourth, like a giant stage. Together they form a cross shape. Each iwan served one of the four schools of Sunni Islamic law. Behind and between them, smaller rooms held students and teachers. So this was never only a mosque. It was also a university, with places for people to live, study, and pray.

In the middle of the courtyard stands a domed fountain for ritual washing. Worshippers wash their hands, faces, and feet here before prayer. Take a moment beside it. Look up at the four iwans around you. The walls are mostly plain, smooth stone. The builders put most of the decoration in a few chosen places. That restraint makes the size of the vaults easier to feel. People who stand here often notice how small they suddenly look.

The largest iwan faces Mecca. This is the qibla iwan, the direction of prayer. Here the decoration becomes rich. A long band of inscription runs around the walls. It carries verses from the Quran in a bold, angular script called Kufic. Below it you will see the mihrab, the niche that marks the direction of prayer, set in coloured marble. Beside it stands the minbar, a tall marble pulpit with steps where the preacher stood. Long chains hang from the vault above. They once held glass lamps.

Those lamps are part of the first surprise. Mamluk craftsmen made famous lamps of enamelled glass, painted with inscriptions and colour. Many lamps from this mosque survived. Today the best ones are in museums, including the Museum of Islamic Art here in Cairo. The chains you see stayed behind. A second surprise concerns the great bronze-covered doors that once closed the main entrance. A later sultan, al-Mu'ayyad, took them for his own mosque near Bab Zuwayla. So parts of this building still exist in other corners of the city.

Behind the qibla wall, a doorway leads into the mausoleum. This is a domed tomb chamber, built for the sultan himself. But Hasan never rested here. In 1361, his own commanders turned against him, and he was killed. His body was never recovered. The building was finished after his death. The tomb meant to honour its founder stands without him. That small, quiet fact changes how many visitors feel when they enter this room.

The building had other troubles too. Chroniclers of the period describe a minaret that collapsed during construction. According to those accounts, many people died, including children from a school nearby. Later, the mosque found a very different use. It stands directly below the Citadel, the hill fortress where Egypt's rulers lived. During revolts, rebels climbed onto its roof and fired at the Citadel. More than once, rulers blocked the stairs or the entrance to stop this. A house of prayer became, for a while, a military position.

There is also a story about the stone itself. Locals say some of the blocks came from the pyramids at Giza. This is a popular tale. Builders in medieval Cairo did reuse stone from older monuments, so the idea is not impossible. But treat this one as legend rather than proven fact. What you can see for yourself are traces of an unfinished plan. On the outside, parts of the decoration around the main portal were never completed. Some carving stops part way, and the outline of the design is still visible.

When you leave, look across the narrow street. A second huge mosque faces this one. That is the Al-Rifa'i Mosque. It looks medieval, but it was built mostly in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Its designers chose a style that would sit comfortably beside Sultan Hasan. It holds royal tombs of the modern Egyptian dynasty. The last Shah of Iran is also buried there. Standing between the two buildings, you can compare six centuries of Cairo in a few steps.

Around you, the neighbourhood carries the weight of that history. The Citadel rises on the hill above. Minarets from other periods appear above the rooftops in many directions. Street sellers, taxis, and families pass through the square. The call to prayer may sound from several mosques at once, each voice a little out of time with the next. This building has stood through plague, rebellion, and centuries of change. Today it still opens for prayer.

Before you go, step back into the courtyard if you can. Notice how the light changes on the stone. In the morning it is pale. Later in the day it turns warmer, almost golden. Watch the pigeons crossing the open square of sky. Listen to the quiet. Then walk out through that dark, turning passage once more. As the noise of the city returns, look back once at the portal. You have just walked through a sultan's grand plan... and through everything that happened to it afterwards.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly, covering shoulders and knees, before entering.
Carry a scarf so women can cover their hair inside.
Remove your shoes at the entrance to the prayer spaces.
Avoid visiting during Friday midday prayers out of respect.
Plan time to visit Al-Rifa'i Mosque directly across the street.
