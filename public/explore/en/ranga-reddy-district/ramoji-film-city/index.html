The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere a generator hums, a bus engine idles, and a recorded fanfare drifts across a lawn that looks almost too green. The air carries dust from the Deccan plateau, warm and slightly sweet. Around you, the buildings look solid, yet some of them are only a single wall deep. Welcome to Ramoji Film City, on the eastern edge of Hyderabad, where almost nothing is quite what it first appears to be.

This is a working film studio, and also a place built to be visited. Those two ideas sit side by side everywhere you walk. On one street, a film crew may be quietly setting up lights. On the next, a family is posing in front of a painted palace. The complex spreads across a huge area of rocky, rolling land in Ranga Reddy district. Guinness World Records has recognised it as the largest film studio complex in the world.

The man behind it was Ramoji Rao, a businessman from Andhra Pradesh. He built a media empire around Eenadu, a Telugu-language newspaper that became one of the most widely read in India. His group later moved into television, publishing, and food products. He died in 2024, and his name still sits on the gates. Film City opened in the mid-1990s, and it grew from his idea of one place where a whole film could be made.

That idea matters. Before studios like this, a production often had to travel between many locations, cities, and laboratories. Here, the plan was different. Sets, sound stages, post-production facilities, hotels for the crew, and even catering could all sit inside one boundary. A director could, in theory, arrive with only a script. Everything else, from a railway platform to a royal court, would already be waiting.

Now look at the land itself. This part of the Deccan plateau is old, hard rock, broken into rounded granite boulders and low ridges. The builders did not flatten all of it. Instead, they used the slopes and the stones as natural scenery. A ridge becomes a fortress wall. A cluster of boulders becomes a bandit hideout. When you ride the tour bus, watch how the landscape changes every few minutes, almost like turning pages.

Here is the first surprise most visitors miss. Many of the streets you pass are designed to stand in for somewhere else. A row of shopfronts can become a north Indian bazaar, a colonial town, or a modern city lane. The trick is in the details that cameras notice: signboards, window frames, the colour of the paint. Change those, and the same street plays a new role. Look closely, and you may find a facade with nothing behind it but scaffolding.

The complex has been home to a very large number of films in many Indian languages. Telugu, Tamil, Hindi, Kannada, Malayalam and others have all used these sets. The most famous recent connection is the Baahubali films, the epic two-part story directed by S. S. Rajamouli. Large parts of their ancient kingdom were built here. Today, a preserved set from those films is one of the places visitors most want to see.

Standing inside that set, notice the scale and the materials. From a distance, the walls look like carved stone. Up close, many surfaces are moulded and painted to imitate stone, wood, or metal. This is the craft of the art department. They build what the camera needs, and only that. A column may be beautifully finished on the side facing the lens, and plain on the side nobody was meant to see.

The second surprise is how much of the visit is about the process, not just the result. Several shows and attractions explain how filmmaking tricks work. You may see how sound effects are created, how a scene is edited, or how a simple movement on a small set becomes a dramatic moment on screen. For many visitors, this is the moment the magic of cinema turns into something more interesting: skilled human work.

Between the sets, there are gardens, fountains, and themed areas. Some are formal and neatly clipped. Others are playful, with bright statues and painted animals. There are also spaces aimed at children, live stage performances, and areas with birds and butterflies. The mood here is cheerful and theatrical. Music plays from hidden speakers, and costumed performers sometimes appear. It is less like a museum, and more like a stage that never quite closes.

Film City also has its own hotels inside the grounds. They serve film crews who stay for long shoots, and tourists who want more than one day. This is part of the original design. A production can live here for weeks. Weddings, conferences and large events also take place on the grounds, because the same sets that suit a film can suit a celebration. A palace backdrop works for a hero, and for a bride.

As you move around, think about the people you do not see. Carpenters, painters, electricians, set dressers, drivers, cooks and cleaners keep this place running. Many of the most impressive things here exist because someone built them by hand, quickly, under pressure, to a deadline. The guides on the buses often point out sets from well-known films. The listener who knows Indian cinema will recognise more. But anyone can admire the craftsmanship.

Before you leave this spot, pause and look around once more. Pick one building and try to decide: is it real, or is it a set? Often, the honest answer is both. It is a real structure, built for an imagined world. Notice the light on the painted stone, the sound of the next bus arriving, the granite ridge behind it all. When you walk on, watch the edges of things. That is where Film City shows how it was made.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early; the grounds are huge and need a full day.
Wear comfortable shoes for long walks between sets.
Carry water and sun protection for the open plateau.
Use the internal tour buses to cover long distances.
Ask staff which areas are closed for active filming.
