The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here the wind moves through dry grass and over old stone, and the sound is very different from the town below. The air feels thinner and cleaner. Under your feet the path is uneven, worn smooth in places by centuries of boots, hooves and bare feet. Around you the Satpura hills roll away in long brown and green ridges. This is Asirgarh Fort. For a very long time, whoever held this hilltop held something much bigger than a hill.

The fort stands north of the old city of Burhanpur, in the Indian state of Madhya Pradesh. Look at the landscape and you can see why it mattered. The Satpura range forms a natural wall between northern India and the Deccan, the great plateau of the south. Armies and traders moving between the two had to pass through a few narrow routes. Asirgarh sits above one of the most important of them. For this reason, people came to call it the key to the Deccan, or the gateway to the south.

The origin of the fort belongs to legend, not to documented history. According to a popular local tale, a herdsman named Asa Ahir first fortified this hill. Ahir is the name of a pastoral community. In the story, Asa needed a safe place for his family and his herds, so he built walls on the summit. Locals say the name Asirgarh comes from him, with garh meaning fort. Nobody can confirm the tale today. Still, it is worth noticing that the story gives the fort a humble beginning, with a cattle keeper instead of a king.

The firmer history begins with the Faruqi dynasty. The Faruqis were Muslim rulers of Khandesh, the region around Burhanpur, during the late medieval period. They made Burhanpur their capital and treated Asirgarh as their great stronghold. A capital on the river plain and a fortress on the hill worked as a pair. The city held the court, the markets and the workshops. The fort held the treasure, the soldiers and the last line of defence. When danger came, the hill was where power retreated.

As you walk, you will notice that the fort is not one single enclosure. It rises in layers. The defences are usually described in three tiers, known as Asirgarh, Kamargarh and Malaygarh. Each tier has its own walls and gateways. An attacker who broke through the lower level then faced another climb and another wall. The design uses the shape of the hill itself. Builders did not fight the slope. They used every cliff edge and every fold of rock as part of the defence.

Now, here is a detail that many visitors miss. The most famous event at Asirgarh was not really a battle of storming walls. In 1601, the forces of the Mughal emperor Akbar took the fort from the last independent Faruqi ruler, Bahadur Shah. The siege was long. Historians describe how disease, hunger and pressure inside the fort mattered as much as cannon fire. Many accounts also say that bribery and negotiation helped open the gates. The strongest fortress in the region did not fall to a single heroic assault. It was worn down.

That conquest is often seen as one of the last great campaigns of Akbar's reign. With Asirgarh in Mughal hands, the empire had a secure door into the Deccan. Burhanpur grew into a major Mughal city and a base for campaigns further south. So when you look out from these walls toward the south, you are looking along the road that imperial armies later followed. This hilltop changed the balance of power across a very large part of India.

After the Mughals, the fort passed through other hands. The Marathas, the powerful Hindu confederacy that rose in western India, held it for a period. Later the British East India Company captured it in the early nineteenth century. The British used the fort as a military post. You can find traces of their time here too, including a small cemetery with weathered European gravestones. It is strange and quiet to find English names carved in stone on top of a Faruqi and Mughal fortress.

Look for the mosque inside the fort. It has tall, slender minarets that you can often see from a distance as you approach. It is built in a style connected with the Faruqi period. The stone is dark and the lines are simple and strong. Stand in front of it for a moment. In a place made for war, this building was made for prayer. Soldiers, officials and their families would have gathered here, far above the plains, under the same wide sky you see now.

Then there is the temple of Shiva, known as Gupteshwar Mahadev. It carries the most remarkable legend of Asirgarh. Locals say that Ashwatthama, a warrior from the Mahabharata, the great Indian epic, still wanders the earth. According to the story, he comes to this temple before dawn and leaves fresh flowers on the Shiva lingam, the sacred stone form of the god. Some people say they have found flowers there that no one remembers bringing. This is folklore, but it is told with real seriousness, and it gives the temple a special atmosphere.

Water was the real secret of survival on this hill. A fortress can have strong walls, but without water it cannot hold out. Walk around and you will find tanks and reservoirs cut into the rock or lined with stone. These collected rain during the monsoon season and stored it through the dry months. When you see green water resting in an old tank, think of it as the fort's life support. A long siege here was always a question of who would run out first, the people inside or the army outside.

Take time at the edges of the walls. The views here are the reward for the climb. On a clear day the plain stretches out in soft colours, with fields, villages and the distant line of hills. In the monsoon season the whole landscape turns green and clouds sometimes drift close to the ramparts. In the dry season the colours are gold and dusty brown. Either way, you can understand the fort best by looking out, not only by looking at its stones.

Before you leave, pause somewhere quiet. Put a hand on the stone of a wall and feel how warm or cool it is. Listen again to the wind and to the birds that nest in the old gateways. A herdsman in a legend, a regional dynasty, an emperor, a Maratha garrison and British soldiers all stood on this same hill. Now it is your turn. As you walk back down, look over your shoulder once or twice. The walls look different from below, and that is exactly how every traveller on the road to the Deccan once saw them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the steep, uneven paths.
Carry plenty of water, since shade is limited.
Visit early morning to avoid the strongest heat.
Combine the trip with a day in Burhanpur town.
Allow extra time to walk all three defensive tiers.
