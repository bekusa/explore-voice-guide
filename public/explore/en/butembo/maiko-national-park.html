The guide

The story

Stand at the western edge of Butembo and feel the air. Up here in the highlands, mornings are cool. Red earth sticks to your shoes, and motorbike engines rise from the streets behind you. But look west, past the last terraced hills. The land falls away toward a vast green lowland. Somewhere beyond that horizon is Maiko National Park, one of the most remote protected forests in Africa.

Most guides take you to a place you can touch. This one is different. Maiko is not a park you stroll into from Butembo. It sits deep in the Congo Basin rainforest, far from paved roads. For most travellers, it stays a distant presence, a forest you learn about rather than walk through. So let us travel there together, in the mind, while you stand here at its doorstep.

The park was created in 1970. It covers more than ten thousand square kilometres of lowland rainforest. That is an area larger than many small countries. Its land stretches across three Congolese provinces: North Kivu, where you are now, together with Tshopo and Maniema. Rivers cut through the forest, and the canopy is so dense that much of the ground stays in shade all day.

Why does this forest matter so much? Because it shelters three animals that live nowhere else on Earth except the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Conservationists sometimes call them Maiko's three great residents. They are the okapi, the eastern lowland gorilla, and the Congo peafowl. Few protected areas hold all three together. That makes this forest a rare refuge.

Start with the okapi. At first glance, it looks like a strange mix of animals. It has the striped legs of a zebra and the dark, velvet body of a horse. But the okapi is actually the closest living relative of the giraffe. It has a long, dark tongue, which it uses to strip leaves from branches. European science only described the okapi in 1901. Local forest peoples, of course, had known it for generations.

Next comes the eastern lowland gorilla, also known as Grauer's gorilla. It is the largest of all the gorilla subspecies. A grown male can be enormous, with a silver saddle of hair across his back. These gorillas live only in the eastern Congo. Their numbers have fallen sharply in recent decades because of hunting and conflict. Maiko is one of the forests where small populations still survive.

The third resident is the one that surprises most people. Here is the story. In 1913, an American naturalist named James Chapin noticed an unusual feather in a headdress in the Congo. He could not identify it. More than twenty years later, in 1936, he found similar feathers on old museum specimens in Belgium. Only then did science recognise a new bird: the Congo peafowl. It is the only true peafowl native to Africa.

The Congo peafowl is shy and hard to see. The male has deep blue and green feathers that shine in low forest light. Unlike its Indian cousin, it does not carry a long, dramatic train. It lives quietly on the forest floor. Even today, very few people have seen one in the wild. In Maiko, the bird remains mostly a rumour of colour moving between the trees.

The forest holds much more. Eastern chimpanzees call through the canopy. African forest elephants, smaller than their savanna relatives, move along hidden paths. Many birds, frogs, and insects here have barely been studied. Scientists believe that remote forests like Maiko still hold species that science has not yet described. The forest is not empty wilderness, though. People have lived in and around it for a very long time.

Now comes the difficult part of the story, and it is important to tell it honestly. Maiko's remoteness made it a hiding place. After the Simba rebellion of the 1960s, armed groups remained in the forest for decades. Later conflicts in eastern Congo brought more armed groups. Illegal mining, hunting for bushmeat, and logging have all damaged parts of the park. For many years, rangers had little control over large areas.

The Congolese Institute for the Conservation of Nature, known by its French initials as ICCN, manages the park. Its rangers work in very hard conditions. Some have lost their lives protecting Congo's parks. International conservation groups have supported surveys and ranger patrols here. Their work is slow and often dangerous. But each survey that finds okapi tracks or gorilla nests gives the forest new hope.

What does this mean for you, standing in Butembo? It means Maiko is not a casual day trip. There is very little tourist infrastructure in the park. Security conditions in the region can change quickly. Anyone who hopes to go further should speak with ICCN and check current travel advice first. For most visitors, the best way to honour Maiko is to understand it, and to support the people who protect it.

Butembo itself is part of this landscape. It is a trading city, full of energy and movement. Goods from the highlands travel through here toward the lowland forests and beyond. The vegetables in the market, the wood in the workshops, the motorbikes heading west on dirt roads... all of them connect this city to the wider region that includes Maiko.

So before you turn away, look west one more time. Notice the line where the hills meet the haze. Beyond it, in the deep shade, an okapi may be pulling leaves from a branch right now. A peafowl may be stepping across wet leaves. You cannot see them from here. But they are there, in one of the last great forests of their kind. Carry that thought with you as you walk back into the noise of the city.
