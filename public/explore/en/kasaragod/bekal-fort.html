The guide

The story

Listen first. Waves break against dark rock below you, steady and patient, and the wind comes straight off the Arabian Sea. It carries salt, and a faint smell of warm stone. Under your feet the path is rough and reddish, the colour of rust left out in the rain. That colour is the first clue to this place. Almost everything around you is cut from laterite, a local stone that forms in hot, wet climates and hardens once it meets the air.

You are standing inside Bekal Fort, on the northern coast of Kerala, in the district of Kasaragod. It is the largest fort in Kerala. The walls curve out over a headland, so the sea surrounds you on three sides. From above, the outline is often compared to a keyhole. On the ground, you feel it differently. You notice that the walls never stay straight for long. They bend and turn to follow the shape of the rock.

Most historians link the fort as it stands today to the Keladi Nayakas, a dynasty from the hills of present-day Karnataka. Their ruler Shivappa Nayaka is usually named as the builder, sometime around the middle of the seventeenth century. Some local traditions suggest that an older stronghold stood here earlier, under the Kolathiri rajas of northern Kerala. The evidence for that earlier phase is less certain. What is clear is that this headland was too useful to leave empty.

Why here? Look out to sea and you will understand. This coast was a trade route for centuries. Ships carried pepper, cardamom, and other spices north toward Arabia and beyond. A fort on this point could watch every sail on the horizon. It could also guard the small harbours and the inland roads behind them. Bekal was not built to hold a palace or a court. It was built to watch.

That purpose shows clearly in the walls. Walk along the ramparts and look at the openings cut into the stone. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The holes sit at different heights. The upper ones were angled to fire at targets far out at sea. The lower ones point down toward the ground near the walls, where attackers would come closest. One wall, two jobs — distance and defence.

Now find the observation tower. It rises from the centre of the fort like a stubby lighthouse. A ramp leads up to the top, wide enough that it is said cannons could be hauled along it. From up there, the view opens in every direction. To one side lies the long sweep of beach. To the other, the coconut palms run inland in a green line. Pause at the top for a moment. This is the view the guards would have had.

As you came in, you probably noticed that the entrance does not run straight. The path turns sharply, more than once. This zig-zag design was a common trick in Indian fort building. An enemy charging the gate could not build up speed, and could not see what waited around the next corner. Defenders above could fire down on anyone caught in the turns. It feels like a simple bend in the road. It was, in fact, a trap.

Near that entrance stands a small Hanuman temple. Hanuman is the monkey god of Hindu tradition, a figure of strength and loyalty. Not far away, outside the walls, there is also an old mosque. Local tradition connects it with Tipu Sultan, the ruler of Mysore. The two buildings sit close together on this coast. Together, they show how many communities have lived around this fort, and still do.

Tipu's name brings us to the next chapter. In the eighteenth century, the Kingdom of Mysore pushed down this coast. First under Hyder Ali, then under his son Tipu Sultan, Bekal came under Mysore control. It served as a military post during their campaigns in Malabar, the old name for this northern part of Kerala. After Tipu died in battle against the British in 1799, the fort passed to the British East India Company. Its time as a frontline stronghold slowly came to an end.

There is more under your feet than you might think. Inside the fort you can find a large water tank with steps leading down into it. On a headland surrounded by salt water, fresh water was everything. A garrison under siege could survive only as long as its supply lasted. There are also signs of an underground passage, a tunnel opening toward the south. Where it led, and how it was used, is not fully certain. That uncertainty is part of its pull.

Here is a second surprise, from a very different century. In the 1990s, Bekal Fort became famous across India for a reason that had nothing to do with war. The film director Mani Ratnam shot a song for his film Bombay here, and the scenes of rain, sea and stone walls stayed in people's memories. Many Indian visitors still arrive humming that song. If you see someone posing dramatically on the ramparts, now you know why.

Today the fort is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India, the national body that cares for historic monuments. Paths have been cleared, and lawns cover parts of the inner ground. Families spread out under the trees. Students sit on the walls and watch the water. Just beside the fort, Bekal Beach draws people down to the sand, especially as the day cools. The old defences have become a place to slow down.

Before you leave, stand still on the seaward wall for a moment. Feel the rough, pitted laterite under your palm. It holds the heat of the day long after the sun drops. Listen again to the waves on the rocks below — the same sound the soldiers heard through every night watch. Watch how the light moves across the water. And as you walk back through the turning gateway, notice how each bend slows your steps, exactly as it was designed to do.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in late afternoon when the sea breeze cools the walls.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven laterite paths.
Climb the observation tower for the widest coastal view.
Carry water since shade on the ramparts is limited.
Walk down to Bekal Beach afterwards for sunset.
