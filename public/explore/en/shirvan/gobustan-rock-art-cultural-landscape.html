The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind moves across this hillside with a dry, steady hiss. It pushes through gaps between huge boulders and carries a faint smell of dust and salt. Under your feet the ground is pale, crumbly limestone, warm in the sun and rough to the touch. Look east and you can see the Caspian Sea, a flat grey-blue line on the horizon. Then look closer, at the rock right beside you. Somewhere on that surface, a person cut a picture into the stone thousands of years ago.

This is Gobustan, a stretch of semi-desert hills on the Caspian coast of Azerbaijan, south of Baku. It may look empty and hard. But for a very long stretch of human history, people found shelter here. They lived among these rocks, hunted on the plains, fished in the sea, and buried their dead. Most importantly for us, they left images. In 2007, UNESCO added this landscape to its World Heritage List. It was recognised as one of the most important collections of rock art in the world.

The numbers are worth a moment. Researchers have recorded more than six thousand rock engravings across the protected area. These engravings are called petroglyphs, which simply means images carved or scratched into stone, not painted onto it. UNESCO describes the art as spanning roughly forty thousand years. That reaches from the Upper Paleolithic, the late part of the Old Stone Age, to the Middle Ages. Few places on Earth show one landscape used and marked by people across such a long stretch of time.

Why here? Part of the answer is the rock itself. Long ago, large blocks of limestone broke away from the ridges above and tumbled down the slopes. They came to rest at angles, forming overhangs, small caves and narrow passages. These spaces gave shade in summer and protection from the wind. Archaeologists have found traces of settlements, hearths and burials inside such shelters. The landscape was also wetter and greener in earlier periods, with richer grazing for animals and the sea closer at hand.

Now to the images. As you walk the paths, look for human figures first. Some are tall men, drawn with broad shoulders and narrow waists, often carrying bows. Others are women, sometimes shown with full, rounded bodies. Look also for animals. There are wild goats with long curved horns, deer, and heavy bulls that many scholars identify as aurochs. The aurochs was a huge wild ox that once roamed across Europe and Asia and is now extinct. Here, it still stands in stone.

One group of images surprises many visitors. In several places, lines of small figures stand side by side, holding hands. They look as if they are dancing in a chain. Many people in Azerbaijan link these scenes to the yalli, a traditional circle and chain dance still performed at weddings and festivals today. Nobody can prove a direct line across so many thousands of years. But it is a striking thought — a dance you might see at a modern celebration, echoed on a boulder in the desert.

Then there are the boats. Look for long, curved shapes with many short vertical strokes above them. Those strokes are usually read as rowers. Some boats seem to carry a circle near the prow, often interpreted as a sun symbol. The Norwegian explorer Thor Heyerdahl visited Gobustan more than once and was fascinated by these boats. He suggested possible links with ancient seafaring peoples further north. That remains a theory, debated by specialists, not settled fact. Still, the boats show that the sea mattered deeply to the people here.

Here is a detail that many visitors walk past. Among the boulders lies a flat stone known locally as the Gaval Dash, which means tambourine stone. The gaval is a traditional Azerbaijani frame drum. When you strike this rock with a smaller stone, it gives a clear, ringing sound, not the dull thud you might expect. It is often suggested that ancient people used it as a kind of instrument, perhaps during rituals or dances. Guides often demonstrate it, so listen for that metallic note carrying across the rocks.

The second big surprise is much younger, and it is written in Latin. On a rock at the foot of Boyukdash mountain, there is an inscription left by soldiers of a Roman legion, the Twelfth Legion, called Fulminata, meaning armed with lightning. It dates to the reign of the emperor Domitian, in the late first century after Christ. It is often described as the easternmost Roman inscription ever found. Picture it — Roman soldiers, far from home, carving their names on the same rocks as Stone Age hunters.

The protected area is not one single site. It covers several rocky zones. The best known are Boyukdash, which means big stone, Kichikdash, which means small stone, and Jingirdag mountain, along with the hill of Yazili, whose name relates to writing. Most visitors explore the main trails near Boyukdash, where walkways and signs guide you between the most important panels. Before or after the walk, the visitor centre has a modern museum. It helps you understand what you are seeing, with replicas, finds from excavations and clear explanations.

As you walk, notice how the light changes the art. When the sun is high, many engravings almost disappear into the pale stone. When the light falls at a low angle, early or late in the day, the carved lines fill with shadow and suddenly stand out. Take your time at each panel. Step a little to the left or right. Crouch down. Often a figure you missed at first will appear once your eyes adjust to the texture of the rock.

The wider area holds another strange sight. Not far from the rock art, the land is dotted with mud volcanoes. These are low cones that slowly bubble and push out cold, grey mud, driven by gas from deep underground. Azerbaijan has a large share of the world's mud volcanoes, and many visitors combine both places in one trip. The contrast is memorable. In one place, the earth itself is moving and breathing. In the other, human beings have left a record that has barely changed for thousands of years.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment among the boulders. Feel the wind on your face, the same wind that has moved across these hills for countless generations. Look once more at the figures in front of you — the hunters, the dancers, the boats heading out to sea. Then turn toward the Caspian on the horizon. The people who carved these images looked at that same water. As you walk back down the path, keep glancing at the rocks you pass. There may be one more picture, waiting quietly in the stone.
