The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your shoes. It crunches softly as you walk toward the gate, and then the sound changes. The ground narrows into a tunnel of brick, and the air turns cooler and damp. Above you, the walls are thick, dark red, and covered in grass on top. This is the Small Fortress at Terezín. It is a quiet place now, but it was built for war, and it was later used for something far worse.

Before we go through the gate, turn around for a moment. In front of the fortress lies a large cemetery. Rows of low graves stretch toward a tall wooden cross and a Star of David. This is the National Cemetery, and it holds the remains of thousands of victims from this prison and the nearby camps. Many visitors walk past it quickly. It is worth pausing here first, because it frames everything you will see inside.

The story begins long before the twentieth century. In the 1780s, the Habsburg Emperor Joseph the Second ordered a new fortress here, close to the meeting of the Elbe and Ohře rivers. He named the town after his mother, the Empress Maria Theresa. The plan had two parts. A large fortress became the garrison town you can see across the river. This smaller fortress stood on the other bank, to guard the approach and the water.

The fortress was designed in the star-shaped style of its time. Low, angled walls deflected cannon fire, and wide ditches could be flooded with water from the river. From the air, the shape looks almost like a snowflake. Down here at ground level, you only feel it as a maze of earth banks and brick passages. The fortress never faced a serious siege. Instead, the empire soon found another use for these thick, quiet walls.

Under the Habsburgs, the Small Fortress became a prison for soldiers and political prisoners. Its most famous inmate arrived during the First World War. Gavrilo Princip, the young Bosnian Serb who shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo in 1914, was held here. He was too young for the death penalty under the law at the time. He spent his last years in a cell at this fortress, and he died here of tuberculosis in 1918, before the war ended.

That single shot in Sarajevo helped start a war that changed Europe. So it is a strange thought... the man who fired it ended his life in a small, cold room only an hour's drive from Prague. His cell is part of the visitor route today. Many people walk through without knowing this connection. Look for it as you move through the older sections of the prison.

Now we reach the darkest chapter. In 1940, after Nazi Germany occupied the Czech lands, the Gestapo in Prague turned the Small Fortress into a police prison. It held mainly political prisoners — Czech resistance members, students, workers, and people who had simply said the wrong thing. Later, prisoners from other occupied countries also came here. The fortress was separate from the Jewish ghetto in the main town, although some ghetto inmates were also sent here as punishment.

As you pass through the main gate, look up at the words painted above the arch: Arbeit macht frei. In English, it means work makes you free. The same cruel phrase appeared at other Nazi camps. Here, prisoners were forced into hard labour, often in local factories and building sites. Historians estimate that around thirty-two thousand people passed through this prison during the war. Thousands of them died here, from hunger, disease, beatings, and executions.

Inside, the prison is divided into several courtyards. You will walk through cells where dozens of people slept on bare boards or on the floor. Some rooms are large and bare. Others are tiny solitary cells, with almost no light. Pay attention to the sound in these spaces. Your own footsteps come back to you from the walls. In a crowded cell, prisoners heard every cough and every whisper around them, day and night.

Here is a detail many visitors find hard to understand. Near the prisoners' area, the guards and their families had their own comforts. There was a swimming pool for the staff, and a building with a cinema hall. The prisoners could sometimes hear the sounds of this ordinary life — splashing water, music, conversation. That contrast is one of the most uncomfortable things about this place. Cruelty and comfort existed only a few metres apart.

Further along, you will reach a long, narrow tunnel cut through the old walls. It leads to the execution ground. In the final weeks of the war, in the spring of 1945, executions continued here even as the Nazi regime collapsed. The fortress was liberated in May 1945. After the war, the prison was used for a time as an internment camp for Germans. That later chapter is also part of the story told by the memorial today.

Today the Small Fortress is part of the Terezín Memorial, which also covers sites in the main town across the river. The memorial runs exhibitions on the prison, the ghetto, and the wider history of persecution. It is worth giving yourself time. Many people visit the fortress in the morning and the ghetto museum later. The two places tell connected but different stories, and together they show the full weight of what happened here.

Before you leave, stand for a moment near the gate again. Notice the birds in the trees along the walls, and the green grass on top of the old earthworks. Across the river, the town of Terezín goes about its everyday life. As you walk back out past the cemetery, look once more at the long rows of graves. Each one stands for a person who once walked through this same gate — but did not walk back out.

Practical tips

Visit the fortress before the ghetto museum in town.
Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and uneven tunnels.
Bring a warm layer for the cool, damp cells.
Allow at least two hours for the full route.
Pause at the National Cemetery before entering the gate.
