The guide

The story

Your feet find the cobblestones first. The stones are smooth and uneven, worn by long use. Around you rise low houses of stone and brick, their roofs covered in red tile. The air smells of fresh bread and wood smoke. Close by, a bell rings from a church tower. You are standing in the old town of Mtskheta, one of the oldest places in Georgia.

Mtskheta lies about twenty kilometers north of Tbilisi. It sits where two rivers meet. The Aragvi flows down from the mountains and joins the wider Mtkvari here. In ancient times, this meeting of waters made the town a natural crossroads. Roads and trade passed through it. The town grew around the rivers and the hills that frame them.

Long ago, this was the capital of a kingdom called Iberia. Kings ruled here for centuries before the seat of power moved south to Tbilisi. Even after the kings left, Mtskheta kept its importance. It became the spiritual center of the country. To this day, the head of the Georgian Church holds his seat in this small town.

It was here, in the fourth century, that the kingdom turned to Christianity. A woman named Nino is remembered as the one who carried the new faith to these lands. The king and queen were baptized. From that time, Mtskheta became holy ground for Georgians. Pilgrims have walked these streets for more than a thousand years.

At the heart of the old town stands a great cathedral. Its stone walls rise above the rooftops, pale and heavy. This is the main reason most visitors come. The streets spread out from its gates like spokes from a wheel. Wherever you walk, you can turn a corner and see its tower again.

One old story draws many pilgrims here. People say that a robe worn by Christ was carried to Mtskheta long ago and buried in this ground. A great tree is said to have grown on the spot. From its wood, the legend goes, came a pillar with healing power. Whether you hold the tale as history or as faith, it has shaped this town for many hundreds of years. The cathedral takes its name from that living pillar.

The streets you walk today have been repaired and repaved. Workers rebuilt the old houses and laid fresh stone underfoot. The result is a town that feels cared for and calm. Cars are kept to the edges. In the center you walk freely, with space to stop and look.

Small shops line the lanes. Many sell things made nearby. You will see wine in clay jars, for Georgia has made wine in clay vessels for thousands of years. You will see felt hats, painted plates, and woven cloth. Sellers sit in their doorways and greet those who pass.

The smell of food follows you through the town. Bakers pull flat bread from hot ovens set in the wall. Strings of a sweet called churchkhela hang in the windows. It is made from grape juice and nuts, dipped and dried until firm. Try a piece. It is chewy and rich, a food that travelers have carried on long journeys for ages.

Walk to the edge of the town and you reach the water. The two rivers meet below, one paler, one darker, mixing slowly as they flow on. The sound of the current is soft and steady. Children skip stones across the shallows. Benches let you sit and watch. It is a good place to rest your feet.

Lift your eyes to the hill across the river. On its bare top sits a small, ancient church, plain and strong against the sky. It has watched over the town for many centuries. From up there, the view of Mtskheta and its rivers is wide and open. Many visitors climb to it after walking the old town.

The old town is a place of quiet movement. Pilgrims come in groups, some lighting candles, some simply standing in thought. Families wander with their children, stopping for ice cream in the shade. Old men play games at small tables. Cats stretch on warm stone steps. The town carries its age lightly, going about an ordinary day.

Stand for a moment in the middle of it all. The stones beneath you have felt many feet. The bell sounds again from the tower. Two rivers meet below, as they have for longer than anyone can remember. In this small town, the long memory of a country gathers into a few quiet streets. Take your time here. There is no need to hurry.
