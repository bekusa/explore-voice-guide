The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. It is dry and stony here, with tough grass growing between broken blocks of pale stone. A breeze comes up off the river below. Across the water, the roofs of Mtskheta sit low in the valley. This quiet hillside was once the political heart of a kingdom.

You are standing on Bagineti, a hill on the right bank of the Mtkvari River, just southwest of Mtskheta. The Mtkvari is the great river of eastern Georgia. Most maps outside Georgia call it the Kura. Here the river turns, and the Aragvi River joins it from the north. Where two rivers meet, valleys meet. Valleys meant trade routes, and trade routes needed watching. That is why people built here.

The ruins around you are known as Armazi, or Armaztsikhe. The word tsikhe means fortress in Georgian. In antiquity this was the acropolis of Kartli, the kingdom that Greek and Roman writers called Iberia. It has no connection to Spain. The name simply belonged to the ancient Georgian state in the eastern Caucasus. Roman authors knew this fortress as Harmozica.

The name Armazi points to a god. According to the medieval Georgian chronicles, Armazi was the chief deity of pre-Christian Kartli. The chronicles describe a great idol on this hill, which looked down on the city. Scholars still debate where the god came from. Many link the name to Iranian religious traditions, because Kartli sat between the worlds of Persia and Rome for centuries.

That in-between position shaped everything here. Rome and Persia both wanted influence over the mountain passes of the Caucasus. In the first century before Christ, Roman armies under the general Pompey marched into Iberia. Later, the relationship changed into an uneasy partnership. A Greek inscription found near Mtskheta, issued in the name of the Roman emperor Vespasian, records Roman help in strengthening fortifications for an Iberian king. Here is the surprise. This small hill once appeared in the official records of an empire.

Now look at the stonework closest to you. The lower courses are cut stone, laid carefully. In many ancient buildings in this region, walls of mud brick once rose on top of stone foundations like these. Mud brick does not survive long once a roof falls in. So what you see today is mostly the base of the city. The rest has melted back into this soil over many centuries.

Modern excavations here began in the late 1930s. Georgian archaeologists then worked on the hill and in the gorge below for years. They uncovered palace buildings, defensive walls, and a bath complex. A bathhouse might seem like a strange luxury for a mountain fortress. But it shows how closely the elite of Kartli followed the fashions of the Roman and Hellenistic world. Hellenistic means the Greek-influenced culture that spread east after Alexander the Great.

The richest finds came from Armaziskhevi, the gorge just nearby. Here archaeologists found the tombs of the pitiakhshes. A pitiakhsh was a high official, something like a viceroy, second in power to the king. The graves held gold jewellery, silver vessels, and finely carved gems. Most of these treasures are now in the Georgian National Museum in Tbilisi. If you visit the museum, you can see objects that left this hill only a few generations ago.

One find changed how historians understand the region. It is a gravestone with the same text in two languages, Greek and Aramaic. It honours a young noblewoman named Serapita, from a family of high officials. Aramaic was a language of administration across the Persian world. The Aramaic here is written in a local form that scholars now call the Armazi script. So the stone tells us something quiet but important. The court of Kartli could write in the languages of both empires at once.

The end of Armazi as a sacred place is part of Georgia's best-known story. According to tradition, Saint Nino brought Christianity to Kartli in the fourth century, and King Mirian converted. The chronicles say the idol of Armazi was thrown down. Legend has it that a storm destroyed the old gods on this hill. That part is faith and folklore. What is clear is that Christianity became the state religion, and the centre of gravity moved across the river.

Turn and look toward Mtskheta. Somewhere among those roofs stands Svetitskhoveli Cathedral, the spiritual heart of Georgian Christianity. On the high ridge beyond the Aragvi, you may see the small silhouette of Jvari Monastery. From here you can take in the whole story in one view. The old pagan acropolis is behind you. The Christian city is in front of you. Only a river separates them.

Later, the royal capital moved from the Mtskheta area to Tbilisi, a short drive downstream. Armazi slowly lost its purpose. Today the sounds are mostly of the modern world: traffic on the highway, perhaps a train along the river, dogs barking in a village. Visitors are few. That is part of the value of this place. Here you can think about ancient Kartli without crowds around you.

Before you go, stand still for a moment and look at the ground beneath your feet. Somewhere under this dust are floors where officials walked, wrote, and argued over treaties with Rome and Persia. The river below still follows the same bend it followed then. As you walk back down, notice how the city across the water comes into view. Notice, too, how quickly the hill behind you returns to quiet grass and stone.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven, stony hillside paths.
Bring water and sun protection, as there is little shade.
Visit Svetitskhoveli afterwards to compare pagan and Christian Mtskheta.
See the Armazi finds at the Georgian National Museum in Tbilisi.
Come in the late afternoon for softer light over the valley.
