The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the square and listen. Trams bell their way across the Augustus Bridge behind you. The Elbe runs wide and slow beyond the railings. And in front of you rises a wall of sandstone, honey-coloured in some places and almost black in others, as if the building was painted in light and shadow at the same time. Look up. Dozens of stone figures stand along the roofline, watching the square below. This is Dresden Cathedral, the Cathedral of the Holy Trinity.

Many locals still call it by its older name, the Hofkirche — the court church. That name holds the key to the whole building. This was not built as a church for the city. It was built as a church for the royal court of Saxony. And here is the first surprise: it is a Catholic church, built in the middle of one of the heartlands of the Protestant Reformation. Saxony was Martin Luther's country. So why is it here?

The story begins with Augustus the Strong, Elector of Saxony. In 1697 he converted to Catholicism. His reason was ambition more than faith. Only a Catholic could be elected King of Poland, and Augustus wanted that crown. He won it. But his Saxon subjects stayed firmly Lutheran. For decades, the ruling family worshipped in a faith that most of their people did not share. A Catholic court in a Protestant land needed a church of its own.

That church came under his son, Augustus the Third. Construction began in 1739, and the building was consecrated in 1751, with work continuing for several years after. The architect was an Italian, Gaetano Chiaveri, a man from Rome. He brought the language of Roman Baroque to the banks of the Elbe. Baroque, for anyone unfamiliar with the term, is the dramatic style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries — curves, movement, statues, and light used almost like theatre.

Chiaveri did not come alone. Italian stonemasons and craftsmen travelled north to work on the site, and they lived in a small settlement close by the river. People called it the Italian village. The name survives today. Just across the square, near the opera house, you can still find a restaurant called the Italian Village, built where those workers once had their homes. It is a small trace of a big migration of skills.

Now look again at those figures on the roof and the balustrades. There are seventy-eight of them, saints and church figures carved in sandstone. Most were made by the sculptor Lorenzo Mattielli and his workshop. From the ground they seem to float, set against the sky. Their gestures turn outward, toward the river and the city. It is worth walking slowly around the outside of the building. The figures change as the light moves across them through the day.

The colour of the stone also tells a story. This is Elbe sandstone, quarried upriver from Dresden. When it is freshly cut, it is pale and warm. Over time, it darkens naturally and turns almost black. So when you see bright stones beside dark ones, you are looking at old stone next to new. And that brings us to the hardest chapter in the cathedral's life.

In February 1945, at the end of the Second World War, Allied bombing destroyed much of Dresden's historic centre. The cathedral was badly damaged. The roof and vaults collapsed, and parts of the walls fell. In the decades after the war, the building was carefully restored, under the government of East Germany. In 1980, it gained new status. The court church became the cathedral of the Diocese of Dresden-Meissen. The patchwork stone on the walls is a record of that rebuilding.

Step inside, and the scale changes. The interior is light, high, and largely white, with soft grey and gold. Notice something unusual about the plan. A walkway runs all the way around the main nave, like a corridor inside the church. According to one common explanation, Catholic processions were not welcome in the streets of Protestant Dresden. So the court built a route for processions indoors. Whatever the exact reason, it gives the space a quiet, circling rhythm.

At the far end, above the high altar, hangs a large painting of the Ascension of Christ, by Anton Raphael Mengs. Mengs was one of the most celebrated painters of his time and worked for the Saxon court. Then turn toward the pulpit. It was carved by Balthasar Permoser, the same sculptor who worked on Dresden's Zwinger palace. Its wooden forms seem to swirl upward, full of movement. Take a moment with each before moving on.

Now listen for the organ, if it happens to be playing. This instrument was the final and largest work of Gottfried Silbermann, the most famous organ builder in eighteenth-century Saxony. Silbermann died in 1753, before the organ was finished. His colleague Zacharias Hildebrandt completed it. During the war, the pipes were removed and stored away from the city, and so they survived the bombing. If you can attend a concert or a service here, the sound fills the whole white space.

Beneath your feet lies the crypt of the Wettin family, the dynasty that ruled Saxony for centuries. Kings and electors are buried here. And here is the last surprise. Augustus the Strong, the man whose conversion started all of this, is not buried in Dresden. His body lies in Kraków, in Poland, where he was king. But a single capsule rests in the crypt. Tradition holds that it contains his heart. Dresden still keeps a part of him.

Before you leave, look for the memorial chapel, with its Pietà made of Meissen porcelain — Mary holding the body of her son. It remembers the victims of the bombing and of injustice. Then step back out onto the square. Look up at the covered bridge that links the cathedral to the Royal Palace next door. The court once crossed it to reach their church. Let the trams and the river noise return... and watch the light move slowly across the saints on the roof.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk a full circle outside to see every roofline saint.
Check the schedule for an organ concert or sung service.
Visit in late afternoon when low sun warms the sandstone.
Ask staff whether the Wettin crypt is open to visitors.
Cross the Augustus Bridge for a wide view back toward the cathedral.
