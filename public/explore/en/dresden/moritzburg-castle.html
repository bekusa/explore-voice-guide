The guide

The story

Listen first to the water. Wind moves across the lake, and small waves slap against stone embankments. Somewhere a duck calls, and gravel shifts under the feet of people on the causeway. Ahead of you, a yellow palace with four round towers sits on its own island. Its reflection lies on the surface of the water. On a calm morning the two images meet almost perfectly at the waterline, and you see the castle twice.

This is Moritzburg Castle, a short drive northwest of Dresden, in the forests and ponds of Saxony. The colours are simple and strong. The walls are a warm ochre, and white trim frames the tall windows. The roofs are deep red. From a distance the building looks almost like a toy, symmetrical and neat. As you walk closer, its true size becomes clear, and so does its purpose. This was a place for hunting.

The story begins in the sixteenth century. Duke Moritz of Saxony built a hunting lodge here in the 1540s, in the Renaissance style of his time. The town and the castle still carry his name. The surrounding woods were full of red deer and wild boar, and the lodge gave the Saxon court a base for the chase. That first building was smaller and plainer than the palace you see today. Its bones, however, are still inside.

The transformation came in the 1720s, under Augustus the Strong. He was Elector of Saxony and also King of Poland, and he loved display. Augustus wanted a setting for grand hunts and lavish court festivals. His builders, including the court architect Matthäus Daniel Pöppelmann, rebuilt the old lodge in the Baroque style. Baroque is the dramatic European style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, full of symmetry, ornament and theatre. The lake around the castle was also shaped and enlarged in this period.

Now look at the approach. The causeway, the stairs and the terraces are all designed as a stage. Sandstone figures stand along the balustrades. Many show hunters, hounds and figures with horns and spears. Guests arrived by carriage or by boat, and every step brought them closer to the king. Even the water was part of the show. On the lake, the court staged mock sea battles and festivals with boats and fireworks. The whole landscape served as one great outdoor theatre.

Inside, the first surprise is the antlers. Hundreds of red deer antlers hang on the walls of the halls, row after row. Some are enormous, with a dense crown of points. A few of these trophies are very old and came from animals across Europe. Locals and guides often point out one especially heavy set of antlers with sixty-six points. Its exact origin carries some legend, so enjoy the story, but treat it as a tale rather than a record.

The second surprise is on the walls behind the antlers. Many rooms are covered not with fabric or paint, but with leather. These are gilded leather wall hangings, embossed with patterns and painted in gold, silver and colour. In the Baroque era they were a sign of luxury. Leather also resisted damp and smoke better than silk. Moritzburg holds one of the largest surviving collections of these hangings in Europe. Look closely, and you can see the raised texture of the pressed designs.

Then comes the room that many visitors remember most — the Feather Room. Its centrepiece is a state bed with hangings made almost entirely of bird feathers. The feathers come from birds such as pheasants, ducks and peacocks. They were knotted into a fine net, piece by piece, to form patterns and colours. Over the centuries the delicate work faded and broke. Conservators spent many years restoring it, and today it is shown under careful light to protect it.

The castle also has a chapel. It was built for Catholic worship at the end of the seventeenth century, after Augustus converted to Catholicism to claim the Polish crown. That was a significant step in Saxony, which was a Protestant heartland. The chapel sits inside a Protestant land like a quiet political statement. Its interior is more restrained than the hunting halls. It reminds us that this pleasure palace was also a place of power and careful choices.

Moritzburg has a second, more modern fame. In 1973, a Czech and East German film company shot a winter fairy tale here, called Three Hazelnuts for Cinderella. The film became a Christmas tradition in Germany, the Czech Republic, Norway and beyond. In the film, the princess loses her shoe on the castle stairs. Many visitors come for that one moment. In winter, the castle often hosts an exhibition about the film, with costumes and scenes from the production.

The landscape around the castle is part of the story too. Beyond the lake you find forest paths, more ponds and old hunting avenues. A short walk away stands the Little Pheasant Castle, a small and delicate building from the late eighteenth century. Nearby, the town is home to the Saxon State Stud, where horses are bred and trained. The artist Käthe Kollwitz spent her final months in Moritzburg at the end of the Second World War.

If you have time, walk all the way around the lake. Each side gives a different view of the towers. From the north, the castle looks formal and closed. From the south, the grand stairs open toward the water. In autumn, the trees turn copper and the ponds steam in the cold mornings. In summer, the light stays late and turns the ochre walls almost orange. The castle changes colour with the hour.

Now, before you go, stand still for a moment by the water. Look at the four towers and the red roofs, and then at their reflection. Think of the carriages, the hounds and the boats with lanterns. Then listen again — the small waves, the ducks, the gravel under your shoes. As you walk back across the causeway, turn around once. The castle will still be there, floating on its island, exactly where the hunters left it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early on a calm morning for clear lake reflections.
Walk the full lakeside loop for changing views of the towers.
Visit in winter for the Cinderella film exhibition.
Combine the castle with a walk to the Little Pheasant Castle.
Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and forest trails.
