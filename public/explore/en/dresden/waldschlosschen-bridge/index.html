The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the Elbe slides past without much noise at all. What you hear instead is the soft hiss of tyres on the deck, the tick of bicycle wheels, and the wind moving across a wide sweep of open grass. Look out along the river. The banks here are not walled or built up. They are meadows, green and flat, running away from the water on both sides. That openness is the whole story of this bridge.

You are standing on the Waldschlösschen Bridge. It crosses the Elbe in the east of Dresden, linking the Johannstadt district on the south bank with the Radeberger Vorstadt on the north. It is a working bridge, built to carry cars, cyclists and walkers between two halves of the city. But few bridges in Europe have caused this much argument. For a while, this crossing was at the centre of a struggle between a city and the world's leading heritage body.

Start with the name. Waldschlösschen means something like "little forest castle" in German. It refers to a small, castle-like building on the northern slope above the river, from the late eighteenth century. Later, a brewery grew up next to it and took the same name. So the bridge is named after a modest country house and a beer, not after a king or a general. That feels fitting, because the debate about it was about ordinary life as much as grand history.

Now the surprise that many visitors do not know. In 2004, UNESCO added the Dresden Elbe Valley to its World Heritage list. The listing was not for one building. It was for a whole landscape — about twenty kilometres of river, meadows, villas, vineyards and palaces, with the baroque old town at its heart. UNESCO valued the way the city and the river valley had grown together over centuries. The open meadows under your feet were part of that picture.

Plans for a bridge at this spot already existed. Traffic across the river was heavy, and the existing bridges were crowded. In 2005, the people of Dresden held a public vote on the project. A clear majority — around two-thirds — voted in favour of building it. For many residents, it was a simple question of getting across the river more easily. For heritage experts, it was a threat to the very view that had earned the listing.

UNESCO warned that a large modern crossing would cut through the protected landscape. In 2006, it placed the Dresden Elbe Valley on its list of World Heritage in Danger. Years of talks followed. Courts heard cases. Alternatives were discussed, including a tunnel under the river. In the end, the city went ahead with the bridge, bound by the result of the public vote. The disagreement could not be resolved.

In 2009, UNESCO removed the Dresden Elbe Valley from the World Heritage list. This was very rare. At that time, Dresden became only the second site in the world ever to lose its status, and the first in Europe. Think about that for a moment. The ground you are looking at is one of very few places on Earth that was listed by UNESCO and then taken off again. And the reason stands right beneath your feet.

There is a smaller twist in the story, and it concerns a very small animal. During the planning and building, environmental groups raised concerns about the lesser horseshoe bat, a protected species living in the area. For a time, work was held up while this was argued in court. To this day, the bat is part of the bridge's identity. Locals still mention it with a smile when the bridge comes up in conversation.

Construction began in 2007, and the bridge opened to traffic in 2013. Look at its shape. The central part is a low, flat steel arch that spans the river channel. The designers tried to keep the structure shallow and light, so it would sit low in the valley. Whether they succeeded is still a matter of opinion in Dresden. Some people see a clean, modest line. Others still see a scar across the meadows.

Now turn and look downstream, toward the west. On a clear day, you can make out the skyline of the old town in the distance. Watch for the dome of the Frauenkirche, the Church of Our Lady, and the tall tower of the Catholic Court Church. That distant line of domes and towers, seen across open river meadows, is the view the heritage experts wanted to protect. From the middle of the bridge, you can judge for yourself what changed.

Then look the other way, upstream to the east. The northern slopes rise toward the hills, with trees and large houses along the ridge. Further along this bank stand three Elbe castles, nineteenth-century palaces on the hillside, though they are hard to see from here. Beyond them, out of sight around the bend, is the Blue Wonder, an older steel bridge with a much longer history. Dresden has always been a city of crossings.

What strikes many visitors is how the meadows are used. They are not a park in the formal sense. People walk dogs here, cycle along the riverside paths, sit on the grass, and watch the water. In summer, the banks fill with picnics and joggers. The Elbe also floods from time to time, and these wide, low meadows give the water room to spread. That is one practical reason they were never built over.

So what does this bridge mean today? For daily commuters, it is simply a faster route across the river. For historians and planners, it is a lesson in how hard it is to balance a living city with a protected landscape. Neither side was entirely wrong. A city must move. A landscape, once changed, does not easily return. The bridge holds both truths at once.

Before you walk on, stand still for one more minute. Feel the slight movement of the deck as a bus passes. Watch the light on the water and the long grass on the banks. Then look again at the old town on the horizon. As you leave, you will be walking across one of Europe's most debated pieces of engineering — and through a landscape that the world once called a treasure, and then let go.
