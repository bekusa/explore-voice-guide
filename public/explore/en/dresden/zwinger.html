The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Gravel crunches under passing feet. Somewhere water falls into a stone basin, and pigeons settle on the rooflines above. Around you, pale sandstone rises in curves, arches and garlands. In places the stone is the colour of honey. In others it has weathered almost to charcoal. This is the Zwinger in Dresden, and it was built to impress the people who first walked here, much as it impresses you now.

The name sounds strange, even to many Germans today. A Zwinger was the strip of open ground between the inner and outer walls of a fortress. It was a practical, military space where defenders could move along the walls. This palace complex stands on part of Dresden's old fortifications. So a place made for war became a place for pleasure. It was a stage for gardens, festivals and display.

The man behind it was Augustus the Strong. He was Elector of Saxony, a ruler of one of the German states. He was also elected King of Poland. He wanted his court to rival the great courts of Europe, and Versailles was the model everyone watched. In the early eighteenth century he commissioned this complex. It started as an orangery, a place to protect citrus trees and exotic plants through the cold Saxon winter. Over the following years it grew into something far grander.

Two names stand behind what you see. The architect was Matthäus Daniel Pöppelmann. The sculptor was Balthasar Permoser, and his workshop carved much of the figurative stone. Together they created one of the finest examples of late Baroque architecture in Germany. Baroque is the style of curves, movement and theatrical decoration that spread across Europe in that era. Here it feels lighter than in many churches of the same period. The pavilions seem designed for celebration rather than prayer.

If you turn toward the Crown Gate, you will see the clearest symbol of Augustus's ambition. The gate rises over a moat and carries a golden crown on top. That crown points to Poland. It reminded every visitor that the Elector of Saxony was also a king. Look at how the gate frames the sky. From the bridge over the water, the reflection doubles the whole structure. On a calm day, the moat holds a second Crown Gate upside down.

Now look for the Wall Pavilion, the most richly carved of all the buildings. At its summit stands Hercules, the strong hero of Greek myth, carrying the globe on his shoulders. The message was not subtle. Augustus liked to compare himself to Hercules, and his own nickname, the Strong, made the link easy. Below the hero, the facade is crowded with satyrs, garlands, shells and masks. Stand close and you see how deeply the stone was cut. Faces seem to push out of the walls.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Tucked behind the main courtyard is the Nymphs' Bath, a fountain grotto. Water cascades down terraces of stone. Female figures stand in niches around the basin, among carved shells and rockwork. It feels secret, cooler and quieter than the open courtyard. On a warm afternoon the sound of water fills the space. Many people walk past the entrance without knowing it is there, so it is worth seeking out.

The courtyard itself was designed for spectacle. The Zwinger was used for court festivities, and the celebrations for the marriage of Augustus's son to an Austrian archduchess in 1719 are often linked to it. Imagine torches along the galleries, music drifting across the lawns, nobles in silk watching performances below. The long galleries were not only decoration. They were viewing platforms, and the whole courtyard worked rather like an outdoor theatre.

Listen carefully, and you may hear another surprise. In one pavilion hangs a set of bells made of porcelain. They come from Meissen, the town near Dresden where Europe first learned to make true hard porcelain. Saxony guarded that secret closely for years. The bells were added in the twentieth century, but they fit the spirit of the place well. When they play, the tone is softer and more delicate than bronze. It sounds almost like glass.

For more than a century, one side of the courtyard stayed open toward the River Elbe. Then, in the nineteenth century, the architect Gottfried Semper closed it with a long gallery building. That building houses the Old Masters Picture Gallery. Its most famous painting is the Sistine Madonna by Raphael, the Italian Renaissance master. At the bottom of that painting sit two small, bored-looking angels. You have almost certainly seen them on postcards, mugs and posters without knowing where they came from.

The Zwinger is also home to other collections. The Porcelain Collection displays the treasures Augustus gathered from China, Japan and Meissen. His passion for porcelain was so strong that people later called it an illness. There is also the Mathematical and Physical Salon, full of historic clocks, globes and scientific instruments. Each museum needs its own ticket, so it helps to choose in advance what interests you most.

The beauty around you hides a harder story. In February 1945, near the end of the Second World War, Allied bombing raids destroyed much of Dresden. The Zwinger was badly damaged. After the war, restorers rebuilt it piece by piece. Much of that work took place in the decades that followed. This is why the stone looks so uneven. Darker blocks are often older stone, blackened by time and weather. Lighter blocks are often replacements.

That patchwork of colour is worth studying. Sandstone from the Elbe valley darkens naturally as it ages, so the contrast is not a flaw. It is a record of survival and repair. When you look at a single wall, you are seeing the eighteenth century and the twentieth century side by side. Few visitors stop to notice it. Once you see it, though, the whole building feels different.

Before you leave, walk up onto the gallery terraces if they are open. From there, the courtyard opens like a garden laid out for you alone. The fountains sparkle, the lawns form clean shapes, and the pavilions frame the sky. Take one last look at Hercules with his globe, and at the golden crown above the gate. Then listen again for the water and the gravel. The festival guests are long gone, but the stage they used is still here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning for a quieter courtyard and soft light.
Walk the upper gallery terraces for the best overview.
Seek out the Nymphs' Bath behind the main courtyard.
Choose which museum interests you most before buying tickets.
Photograph the Crown Gate reflected in the moat on calm days.
