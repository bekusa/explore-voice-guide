The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Footsteps echo off sandstone that has turned almost black with age, and pale patches show where new blocks sit beside old ones. Somewhere a tram rattles past. Above you, a slender tower rises over the rooftops, and the light here falls in long, cool shadows. This is Dresden Castle, the Residenzschloss. For centuries, it stood at the centre of power in Saxony.

The castle was the home of the Wettin family, the dynasty that ruled Saxony first as dukes, then as electors, and later as kings. An elector was one of the few princes allowed to vote for the Holy Roman Emperor, so this was no small court. What began as a medieval fortress near the river Elbe grew, wing by wing, into a large residence. Each generation added something, and each one wanted the building to say more about its owners.

The look you see today is largely Renaissance. In the sixteenth century, the rulers rebuilt the castle around a great courtyard, in the style that came north from Italy. That style is about order and symmetry, about stairs and arcades that were made to impress guests. Later centuries added Baroque touches and, around 1900, a large historicist restoration. So the walls around you carry several layers of taste, one on top of another.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. Look closely at the walls of the great courtyard. Many surfaces are covered in sgraffito — a technique where an artist scratches through a top layer of plaster to show a darker layer underneath. The result looks like a drawing in two colours, grey and white. Figures, columns, and ornaments seem to stand out from the flat wall. Much of it is a careful modern reconstruction, based on old sources.

That word, reconstruction, matters in this city. In February 1945, near the end of the Second World War, air raids burned much of Dresden. The castle was gutted. For years it stood as a roofless shell, its windows empty. Rebuilding started slowly in the decades that followed, and the work has continued well into the twenty-first century. When you see a bright, clean stone beside a dark one, you are looking at that long story of repair.

The dark stones have their own explanation. Saxon sandstone darkens naturally as it weathers and reacts with the air. So the black surfaces are not only the marks of fire. Dresden's builders chose to reuse old stone wherever they could. The patchwork you see is honest. It shows exactly what survived and what had to be replaced.

Now walk to the outer wall of the old stable courtyard, on the lane called Augustusstrasse. Here you will find the Procession of Princes. It is a long mural of Saxon rulers on horseback, riding one after another. The painting was first made with sgraffito, but it faded. Around 1900, it was copied onto tiles of Meissen porcelain, from the famous factory just down the river. There are more than twenty thousand tiles, and the mural runs for roughly a hundred metres. Remarkably, it came through the bombing with little damage.

But the greatest treasures are inside. The castle holds the Green Vault, one of the oldest museums of its kind in Europe. It was created by Augustus the Strong, the elector who ruled in the early eighteenth century. He loved display. He filled a suite of rooms with gold, silver, ivory, amber, and precious stones, and he showed them to selected guests. The name comes from the green paint once used in some of these rooms.

Today the collection is split into two parts. The Historic Green Vault recreates the original Baroque rooms, with objects crowded against mirrored walls, just as visitors would have seen them centuries ago. The New Green Vault shows pieces one at a time in modern cases, so you can study each one up close. Among them is the Dresden Green, a rare natural green diamond. It is small enough to hold in your palm, yet people travel across the world to see it.

Here is the second surprise. In November 2019, thieves broke into the Historic Green Vault and stole jewellery set with diamonds. It was one of the most famous museum robberies in recent memory. Members of a criminal family from Berlin were later tried and convicted, and much of the stolen jewellery was recovered. The story made headlines around the world, and it reminded everyone how fragile these collections are.

The Green Vault is not the only collection here. The castle also holds the armoury, with parade armour made for princes and their horses. There is the Turkish Chamber, a collection of Ottoman objects gathered by Saxon rulers, including a large tent that you can walk beside. There is a coin cabinet, and a large collection of prints and drawings. You could spend a full day inside these walls and still not see everything.

Before you leave, look up at the tall tower, the Hausmannsturm. In some seasons, visitors can climb it for a view over the Old Town, the river, and the domes nearby. Also look at the small courtyard, which now has a modern transparent roof. That roof turned an old open space into a covered entrance hall. It is a quiet example of how old and new meet in this building.

Now take one last look at the walls around you. Pale stone and dark stone, standing side by side. Real carving beside careful copies. Nothing here is hidden away — the castle simply shows you where it was broken and how it was rebuilt. As you walk away, toward the river or the cathedral next door, listen again for those footsteps on the stone. People have walked here for hundreds of years, and today you are one of them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a timed ticket for the Historic Green Vault in advance.
Plan half a day if you want several museum collections.
Walk Augustusstrasse to see the full Procession of Princes.
Check whether the Hausmannsturm tower is open for climbing.
Combine your visit with the cathedral and Zwinger nearby.
