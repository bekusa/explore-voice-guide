The guide

The story

Listen for the river first. The Elbe moves past here slowly, wide and brown-green, and on a calm day you can hear water against the stone steps below the palace. Gravel crunches underfoot. Above you, the roofs curve upward at the corners, like the edges of a paper lantern. You are only a short journey upstream from the centre of Dresden, but the air feels different here. It is softer, greener, and closer to the water.

This is Pillnitz, a summer residence of the Saxon royal court. It is not one single building. It is a group of palaces arranged around formal gardens. The building closest to the river is the Water Palace. Its twin, facing it across the garden, is the Hill Palace, set towards the vineyard slopes. Between them, closing the courtyard, stands the New Palace. Together they form an open square. They were designed to be walked through, lived in during warm months, and enjoyed from the water.

The man behind most of what you see is Augustus the Strong. He was Elector of Saxony and also King of Poland, and he spent extravagantly on art and architecture. In the early eighteenth century he decided to turn Pillnitz into a pleasure palace. He did not want it as a fortress or a seat of government. He wanted a stage for summer festivals, boating parties, and court entertainment. The main design work is generally credited to Matthäus Daniel Pöppelmann. He was the same architect associated with the Zwinger in the city centre.

Now look closely at the roofs and the eaves. Pillnitz is one of the most important examples in Europe of a style called chinoiserie. It was a European fashion for imagining China, and it drew on porcelain, lacquerware, and travellers' descriptions. The builders did not try to copy real Chinese buildings. Instead, they mixed curved, pagoda-like roofs with a Baroque layout. Under the eaves you will find painted figures and scenes in a Chinese style. They show a European dream of a faraway East.

That dream had a real connection to Saxony. Augustus collected Asian porcelain obsessively. During his reign, Meissen, not far downriver, began producing the first true hard-paste porcelain made in Europe. So the Chinese motifs here are not a random fashion. They reflect a court that was passionate about porcelain and the lands it came from. When you see these painted eaves, you are looking at the same taste that filled Dresden's porcelain collections.

Walk down to the broad staircase that drops from the Water Palace into the Elbe. These steps are one of the details visitors most often overlook. In the age of Augustus, many guests arrived by boat. Court festivities included gondolas and decorated vessels on the river. The steps were a ceremonial entrance from the water. Stand at the top and imagine the palace from a boat — that was the view the architects had in mind.

Pillnitz also has a more personal story. Before Augustus rebuilt it, the estate was linked to Countess Cosel, one of his best-known favourites at court. She later fell from his favour and spent many years confined far from Dresden. Her story is often told with dramatic additions. The broad outline, however, is part of Saxon history. It is a reminder that this pleasant summer world rested on the moods of one powerful man.

Here is a surprise that many visitors miss. In 1791, Pillnitz briefly became a place of European diplomacy. The Holy Roman Emperor Leopold the Second and King Frederick William the Second of Prussia met here. They issued what became known as the Declaration of Pillnitz. It concerned the French Revolution and the safety of the French king. Revolutionary France saw it as a threat, and it helped to raise the tension before the wars that followed. Few people expect that among these garden paths.

The building that closes the courtyard has its own history. The original palace in this position was damaged by fire in the early nineteenth century. The New Palace replaced it in a more restrained, classical style. Inside, it includes a domed hall and a Catholic chapel. Look at how it connects the two older palaces. Its builders kept the overall harmony of the ensemble, even though fashions in architecture had changed.

Now step into the gardens, because the park is as important as the buildings. It includes formal sections, a more natural English-style landscape garden, and a Chinese pavilion added in a later period. The garden's most famous resident is a Japanese camellia. It is more than two hundred years old and one of the oldest in Europe. It has grown into a large tree. In winter, a movable glasshouse protects it from the cold. In late winter and early spring, it can be covered in thousands of red blossoms.

As you walk, look up towards the slopes behind the Hill Palace. These hillsides have carried vineyards for a long time. The Elbe valley around Dresden is one of the most northerly wine regions in Europe. Among the vines stands a small vineyard church, also linked to Pöppelmann. It is a quieter, simpler building, and it is a good contrast to the theatrical palaces below. The walk upward rewards you with views across the river.

Today, the Water Palace and the Hill Palace house the Museum of Decorative Arts, part of the Dresden State Art Collections. The galleries show furniture, textiles, ceramics, and design objects. Some exhibitions open only in the warmer months, so check the current plans before you come. You can also come here the way the court once did — by river. Paddle steamers from Dresden stop at the landing near the palace. The trip lets you see the palace slowly appear from the water.

Before you go, stand for a moment in the centre of the courtyard. Turn slowly. On one side is the river, and on the other side are the vineyards. Between them are the curved roofs, with the painted figures looking down. Listen again to the water and the gravel. As you walk away, look back once at the roofline. From a distance, its corners rise against the sky, and you can see the summer world that Augustus wanted to build.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive by paddle steamer from Dresden for the river approach.
Visit in late winter or early spring for camellia blossoms.
Check which museum galleries are open before your visit.
Climb the vineyard slopes for wide views over the valley.
Wear comfortable shoes for gravel paths and hillside walks.
