The guide

The story

Listen to the water first. A soft slap against the shore, the hiss of wind across a wide, flat lake. The light here is hard and pale, and it bounces off the surface until the far bank looks like a thin grey line. Somewhere out there, under that moving water, lies one of the most important villages in the story of humankind. You cannot walk into it. But you can stand close, and listen, and imagine.

This place is called Mureybet. It was a tell, a low mound built up by people living and rebuilding on the same spot, layer upon layer. It rose on the east bank of the Euphrates, in what is now Raqqa Governorate in northern Syria. For thousands of years it held the remains of a community that lived through one of the great turning points of our species. That turning point was the slow shift from hunting and gathering toward farming.

Here is the first surprise. The village you are thinking about is older than writing, older than the wheel, older than pottery in this region. People first settled here around twelve thousand years ago, near the end of the last Ice Age. The earliest layers belong to a culture that archaeologists call the Natufian. These were people who gathered wild grains and hunted game, yet chose to stay in one place for much of the year.

Why here? Look at the river landscape around you. The Euphrates valley offered water, fish, reeds and fertile terraces. On the steppe beyond, herds of gazelle and wild ass moved across the open ground. Wild cattle, called aurochs, grazed near the river. Excavations found many of their bones in the village. A family living here could eat well without travelling far. That simple fact may explain why people began to put down roots.

The first scientific digging at Mureybet came in the 1960s. An American team led by the archaeologist Maurits van Loon excavated part of the mound in 1964 and 1965. Then, in the early 1970s, the French prehistorian Jacques Cauvin led new excavations. Both teams worked against the clock. A great dam was rising downstream at Tabqa, and everyone knew the valley would soon disappear under water.

That is the second surprise, and it is a sad one. The archaeologists were not digging at leisure. They were part of an international rescue campaign. Teams came to the Euphrates to record as much as they could before the lake filled. When the Tabqa Dam closed in the 1970s, the water rose and created the reservoir you see today, Lake Assad. Mureybet, and several neighbouring prehistoric sites, slipped beneath the surface.

What did the diggers find before the water came? In the oldest levels, people lived in round houses, partly sunk into the ground. Later, the buildings changed. Walls became straighter, and some houses had several small rooms, perhaps for storage. This change from round to rectangular is one of the patterns archaeologists follow across the ancient Near East. At Mureybet, they could watch it happen, layer by layer, in a single mound.

The plant remains tell an even richer story. Researchers found grains of einkorn wheat, barley and rye. Einkorn is one of the earliest kinds of wheat, small and hardy. At first these plants appear to be wild, gathered from the hillsides. Some scholars argue that people at Mureybet began to cultivate them, planting and tending wild cereals before the plants changed into fully domestic crops. The debate continues, but this village sits at its centre.

Now for an idea that changed how many people think about the past. Jacques Cauvin noticed something in the later layers. Small female figurines appeared. And the horns and skulls of wild bulls were set into the houses, kept inside the living space. To Cauvin, these were signs of a new set of beliefs. He suggested that a change in symbols and religion came before farming, not after it.

Cauvin called this a revolution of symbols. In simple terms, he argued that people first changed how they saw the world, and only then changed how they produced food. Not every archaeologist agrees. Some see economic pressures as more important. But the idea still shapes discussions today, and Mureybet remains one of its foundation stones. A small village on the Euphrates helped start a very large argument.

The site also gave its name to a culture. Archaeologists speak of the Mureybetian, a phase of the early Neolithic in the middle Euphrates. The word Neolithic means new stone age, the period when farming and village life took hold. Stone tools from this phase have recognisable shapes, especially their arrowheads. When researchers find similar tools elsewhere, they often compare them back to what came out of this drowned mound.

Think about the people themselves for a moment. They knapped flint by the river, the sharp click of stone on stone carrying across the water. They ground grain on heavy stones, buried their dead near their homes, and rebuilt their houses again and again on the same ground. Generation after generation, they chose this spot. Their whole world measured only a few kilometres across, yet the choices they made reach us today.

So take a last look at the lake. The surface moves, the light shifts, and nothing above the water marks the village below. Yet down there, under silt and stillness, lie the floors where some of the first farmers stood. When you turn away, notice the river terraces, the open steppe, the wide sky. This is the same landscape those villagers saw, twelve thousand years ago.
