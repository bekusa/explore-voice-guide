The guide

The story

Feel the ground under your feet. It is not quite flat. You are standing on a mound, a tell, built up layer by layer from thousands of years of mud brick, ash, broken pottery and fallen walls. The wind comes across the open plain of the Orontes valley. To the west, on a clear day, the long grey line of the Lebanon mountains rises beyond the fields. This quiet hill near Al-Qusayr was once ancient Kadesh, a city whose name travelled as far as the temples of Egypt.

Today most people know the mound as Tell Nebi Mend. A tell is an artificial hill, formed when people build new houses on top of old ones for centuries. The name comes from a small Muslim shrine linked to a holy figure called Nebi Mend, or the Prophet Mend. A modern village sits on and around the slopes. So you are in a place where ordinary life still goes on, on top of one of the most argued-over cities of the ancient Near East.

Why here? Look at the water. The Orontes River runs close beside the mound, and a smaller stream joins it nearby. Together they wrapped the old city in a natural defence, almost like a moat. The valley here also forms a corridor between the coast and the inland plains of Syria. Armies, traders and herders all had to pass this way. Whoever held Kadesh could watch the road north and the road south. That simple fact of geography shaped its whole story.

People lived on this spot for a very long time, from early prehistoric farming villages through the Bronze Age and well beyond. By the second millennium before the common era, Kadesh was a strong city-state with its own king. It sat at a dangerous edge. To the south lay the reach of Egypt. To the north grew the power of the Hittites, a kingdom based in the highlands of what is now central Turkey. Kadesh often had to choose a side, and the choice usually cost it dearly.

The city first appears clearly in Egyptian records in the fifteenth century before the common era. The pharaoh Thutmose the Third marched into the Levant and fought a coalition of local rulers at Megiddo, in today's northern Israel. Egyptian texts name the king of Kadesh as one of the leaders of that alliance. Thutmose later campaigned further north against Kadesh itself. For Egypt, this city became a symbol of stubborn resistance far from home.

Then came the event that made the name famous. Around 1274 before the common era, the young pharaoh Ramesses the Second led a large army north to take Kadesh. Facing him was the Hittite king Muwatalli the Second. The clash that followed, somewhere on the plain around you, is the Battle of Kadesh. It is often described as one of the earliest battles in history that we can reconstruct in some detail, because both sides left written records.

Here is the part many visitors do not expect. According to the Egyptian accounts, Ramesses was tricked before the fighting began. Two men, presented as deserters, told him the Hittite army was still far to the north. Ramesses believed them and hurried ahead with only part of his force. The Hittites were in fact hidden behind the city. Their chariots struck an Egyptian division on the march and then swept into the pharaoh's camp.

The Egyptian army marched in divisions named after gods, including Amun, Ra, Ptah and Seth. In the Egyptian telling, Ramesses fought almost alone, with the god Amun at his side, until reinforcements arrived and turned the day. It is a dramatic story. But historians read it carefully. The battle seems to have ended without a clear winner. Kadesh itself stayed under Hittite influence, and Ramesses returned to Egypt without the city he came to capture.

And yet, back home, Ramesses presented Kadesh as a great victory. He had scenes and texts of the battle carved on the walls of major temples, including Abu Simbel, Karnak, Luxor and his memorial temple at Thebes. So the best-known images of this hill are not here at all. They are more than a thousand kilometres away, on Egyptian stone. That is a second surprise: one of the most carefully recorded battles of the ancient world is also one of its finest pieces of royal publicity.

The story has a quieter ending. Some years later, Ramesses and a later Hittite king, Hattusili the Third, agreed a formal peace treaty. It promised an end to war and mutual help between the two powers. Versions survive in Egyptian hieroglyphs and on a Hittite clay tablet written in Akkadian, the diplomatic language of the age. It is often called one of the oldest known peace treaties. A copy of it is displayed at the United Nations headquarters in New York.

Kadesh did not vanish after the Bronze Age. In later centuries, under the Greek-speaking kingdoms that followed Alexander the Great and later under Rome, a town on this site was known as Laodicea ad Libanum, which means Laodicea near Lebanon. Archaeologists have worked here since the early twentieth century, first with a French expedition and later with a long British-led project from the 1970s. Their trenches cut down through the mound like slices through a cake, showing each age resting on the one before.

As you walk, keep your expectations honest. This is not a site of tall columns or restored palaces. Much of what remains is earth, low walls and the shape of the hill itself. The value is in the setting. Stand where you can see the river, the fields and the line of the mountains. Picture tents, horses and chariots across that plain. The landscape has changed, but the basic layout of river, mound and valley is still the one that ancient soldiers saw.

Before you leave, pause on the slope for a moment. Listen to the village around you: a motorbike, a call from a doorway, perhaps the call to prayer drifting over the rooftops. Under your shoes are the floors of houses where people cooked, argued and slept for thousands of years. A pharaoh came here to take this hill and went home without it. The hill is still here. Look once more toward the Orontes as you walk back down.
