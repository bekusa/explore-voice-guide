The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the great metal roof, sound behaves differently. Footsteps on the walkways ring softly, voices drop to a murmur, and the wind from the Tlaxcala valley hums across the open edges. The light comes in filtered and even. It falls on earthen walls, on old plaster, and on colours that should not have survived — deep blues, warm reds, the black outlines of painted figures.

This is Cacaxtla, a hilltop city in the state of Tlaxcala, in central Mexico. It is not famous for its size. Other sites in Mexico have taller pyramids and wider plazas. Cacaxtla is famous for what is painted on its walls. Here you will find some of the best-preserved murals in all of ancient Mesoamerica — the cultural region that stretched from central Mexico down into Central America.

Start with the setting. The site sits on a raised ridge above the farmland of the Puebla-Tlaxcala valley. On clear days you can see far across the fields. The big volcanoes of central Mexico may rise on the horizon. A hill like this was a natural fortress. Its people could watch the valley, control the trade routes below, and defend themselves. Keep that idea in mind. War and power are written all over these walls.

The main structure is a huge platform, usually called the Gran Basamento, or Great Platform. It is not a single building. It is many layers of construction, built one on top of another over generations. Rooms, patios, stairways and altars were filled in and covered as new phases rose above them. That habit of burying the old to build the new is one reason the paintings survived. They were sealed inside the hill for more than a thousand years.

The city flourished in what archaeologists call the Epiclassic period, roughly between the seventh and tenth centuries. This was a restless time. The great city of Teotihuacan, to the northwest, had lost its power. Smaller regional centres competed for land, trade and prestige. Cacaxtla was one of the winners of that moment. Many scholars link it to a group known as the Olmeca-Xicalanca, though the exact identity of its rulers is still debated.

Here is the first surprise. The murals were found in 1975, and the discovery did not begin with a planned excavation. Local people, digging on the hill, uncovered painted walls. Archaeologists followed. What they found changed how experts understood this whole period. Nobody expected paintings of this quality, in this condition, in the highlands of central Mexico.

Now look at the style. Many visitors notice something unexpected. The figures do not look like typical central Mexican art. Their proportions, their profiles, their flowing poses and many of their symbols recall the art of the Maya, hundreds of kilometres to the southeast. Yet other symbols are clearly from central Mexico. Cacaxtla mixes both traditions on the same wall. It is a picture of a connected world, where ideas, artists and goods moved across great distances.

The best-known painting is the Battle Mural. It runs along the base of a platform and shows a violent scene. Warriors dressed in jaguar skins attack warriors who wear bird costumes. Some of the bird warriors are wounded. Some lie on the ground. Some figures are shown with terrible injuries. The scene is not decorative. It is a record, or a statement, of victory and defeat. Historians still argue about whether it shows a real battle, a ritual, or a political message.

In another part of the complex, near a structure known as Building A, two tall figures stand on either side of a doorway. One is a man dressed as a bird, standing on a feathered serpent. The other is a man dressed as a jaguar. They hold staffs and ceremonial objects. Plants, water and animals surround them. Many scholars read them as opposing forces — sky and earth, perhaps, or two ruling groups. Stand between them for a moment. You are standing where the doorway once led.

The second surprise is the colour itself. Look closely at the blues. Some of this blue is the same kind of pigment the Maya used, often called Maya blue — a mix of indigo dye and a special clay. It is known for being extremely stable. That stability helps explain why the blues here still look so strong. Reds, yellows and blacks fill out the palette. Together, they give the walls a brightness that photographs rarely capture.

Now look up at the roof. It is one of the largest protective roofs over any archaeological site in Mexico. It was built to shield the platform and its paintings from rain, sun and wind. The murals are delicate, and once exposed to air, the plaster and pigments can suffer. The roof changes the experience. You walk through ancient rooms under modern steel, along raised paths, looking down into courtyards that were once open to the sky.

Cacaxtla also has a neighbour. Just a short distance away stands Xochitécatl, an older site with pyramids on another hilltop. Many visitors combine the two. The site museum near the entrance holds objects from the excavations and helps explain the layers you have just walked through. Around the hill, the countryside of Tlaxcala carries on as it has for centuries — fields of maize, small towns, church towers and the slow traffic of rural roads.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the platform and look out over the valley. The people who painted these walls watched the same horizon. They saw rival cities, trade caravans and coming storms. Then turn back toward the shade of the roof. Listen again to the quiet. Somewhere in that dim light, a jaguar warrior is still raising his weapon, and a bird man is still standing on his serpent — painted, sealed, and found again.
