The guide

The story

Listen first. On a quiet morning, the square around you is wide and open, and the sound of traffic seems to fall away. Pigeons move across the paving stones. Then the light catches the domes above you — green copper and bright gold, stacked one above another like a small mountain. You are standing in front of Saint Alexander Nevsky Cathedral, in the heart of Sofia, and the building is large enough that it takes a moment to see all of it at once.

Let us begin with the reason this church exists. In the late nineteenth century, Bulgaria had lived under Ottoman rule for nearly five centuries. The Russo-Turkish War of 1877 and 1878 changed that. Russian armies, fighting alongside Bulgarian volunteers, pushed the Ottoman forces back, and Bulgaria became a free state again. Many thousands of soldiers died in that war. This cathedral was conceived as a memorial to them — a church built in gratitude, and in grief.

The name carries a second layer of meaning. Saint Alexander Nevsky was a Russian prince of the thirteenth century, remembered as a defender of his land and later honoured as a saint. He was also the patron saint of Tsar Alexander the Second, the Russian ruler who led the war that freed Bulgaria. So the dedication honours a medieval saint, a nineteenth-century emperor, and the soldiers who fought under him — all in one name.

The idea came quickly after liberation, but the building came slowly. A foundation stone was laid in the 1880s. The main construction, however, took place in the early twentieth century, roughly between 1904 and 1912. The design came from the Russian architect Alexander Pomerantsev. Even after the walls and domes were finished, decoration continued for years, and the cathedral was formally consecrated only in 1924. From the first idea to the final blessing, more than four decades passed.

Now look at the shape of the building. The style is called Neo-Byzantine. That means it borrows from the churches of the Byzantine Empire — the eastern, Greek-speaking half of the old Roman world, centred on Constantinople. You can see it in the rounded arches, the heavy walls, and above all in the domes. There is not just one. Smaller domes and half-domes climb toward a large central dome, so the roofline seems to build upward in steps, the way a hillside rises.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The gold on the domes is real gold leaf — thin sheets of gold pressed onto the surface. It catches the sun even on grey days, which is why the cathedral seems to glow from far across the city. The tall bell tower beside the main entrance holds a set of bells, and when they ring together on feast days, the sound carries well beyond the square. If you hear them, stop for a moment. The whole space changes.

The square itself deserves attention. Walk a short distance and you will find the small, dark-brick Church of Saint Sofia. It is far older than the cathedral, with roots going back to late Roman times. The city of Sofia takes its name from this church — and the word sophia means wisdom in Greek. So within a few minutes' walk, you move from one of the oldest churches in the city to one of the grandest. The contrast in scale is worth noticing.

Now step toward the entrance. Before you go inside, look at the main doors and the arches above them. The surfaces carry carved stone, mosaics, and painted figures of saints. Notice how the decoration grows richer as you get closer to the door. This is deliberate. In Orthodox Christianity, the church building represents a passage from the everyday world into a sacred one. The facade prepares you for that crossing, step by step.

Inside, the first thing most people notice is the darkness. Your eyes need a few seconds to adjust. Then the details appear — candles flickering, the smell of beeswax and incense, gold glinting on the walls. The interior uses marble and onyx brought from several countries, along with alabaster. Murals cover the walls and the inner surfaces of the domes. Artists from Bulgaria, Russia, and other parts of Europe worked on them, which gives the interior a mix of hands and styles.

Look for the iconostasis. This is the tall screen at the front of the church, covered with icons — painted images of Christ, the Virgin Mary, and the saints. In Orthodox churches, the iconostasis separates the main space, where people stand, from the altar area, where the priests serve. Notice also that there are few or no pews. Traditionally, Orthodox worshippers stand during the service. The open floor is not empty space — it is designed for people to gather, move, and pray on their feet.

Here is a second surprise, and it reflects how politics can touch even a church. During the First World War, Bulgaria and Russia fought on opposite sides. For a short time, the cathedral was given a different name, dedicated to Saints Cyril and Methodius, the brothers linked to the Slavic alphabet. After the war ended, the original name returned. It is a small episode, but it shows how closely this building is tied to the relationship between two nations.

There is more beneath your feet. The crypt below the cathedral holds a collection of Orthodox icons, gathered from churches and monasteries around Bulgaria. Some are centuries old. The space is quiet and dim, very different from the height of the nave above. If you have time, it is a good place to see the painting tradition up close — the gold backgrounds, the long faces, the steady eyes. Access to the crypt is usually separate from the church itself.

Today the cathedral serves many roles at once. It is a working church, where services, weddings, and baptisms still take place. It is a national symbol, printed on postcards and seen in countless photographs of Sofia. And it is a meeting point for the city — people sit on the edges of the square, students cross it on the way to class, and visitors stand where you are standing now, looking up.

Before you leave, take one last look at the domes. Watch how the light moves across the gold as clouds pass. Listen for the bells, the footsteps on the stone, the low murmur of voices near the doors. This building was raised as a thank-you, and as a memorial to people who never saw it finished. As you walk away across the square, glance back once more. From a distance, the domes still hold the light.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning for a quieter square and softer light.
Dress modestly with shoulders and knees covered inside.
Keep voices low and avoid flash during services.
Walk over to the nearby Church of Saint Sofia.
Check the crypt icon collection, which has separate access.
