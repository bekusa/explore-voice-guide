The guide

The story

Stand for a moment at the edge of the garden. You may hear the soft rush of the fountain, and somewhere behind you, the click of chess pieces on a stone table. In front of you rises a long building in deep red and cream, with a row of white columns and a sculpted triangle above them. In the afternoon the light turns the red walls warmer. This is the Ivan Vazov National Theatre, the oldest and most important stage in Bulgaria.

To understand why this building matters, it helps to remember how young modern Bulgaria was when it rose. The country became independent from Ottoman rule in the late nineteenth century. Sofia was then a small town, and it was quickly being rebuilt as a European capital. New ministries, boulevards and churches went up within a few decades. A national theatre was part of that same project. It was a public statement that Bulgarian language and Bulgarian stories now belonged on a grand stage.

The company existed before the building did. In the 1890s, a troupe of actors in Sofia worked under a cheerful name that translates roughly as Tears and Laughter. They performed in modest halls, often in difficult conditions. In 1904 the troupe received official status as the national theatre. The actors needed a permanent home that matched that new title... and the city wanted one too.

For the design, Bulgaria turned to Vienna. The architects Ferdinand Fellner and Hermann Helmer ran one of the most successful theatre practices in Europe. Their firm designed dozens of opera houses and playhouses, from Austria to Croatia to Ukraine. If you have travelled in Central Europe, this building may look familiar. That is not a coincidence — it shares a family style with many of their other theatres. The new house in Sofia opened in 1907.

Now look closely at the front. The style is neoclassical, which means it borrows from the temples of ancient Greece and Rome. You can see this in the columns, the heavy cornice and the triangular pediment on top. The pediment holds a sculpted group of classical figures, a reminder of the old link between theatre and the arts of antiquity. The red and cream colour scheme is less common in Sofia. It makes the theatre easy to pick out from far across the garden.

Here is a detail many visitors never hear. In 1923, during a performance, a fire broke out inside the theatre. The blaze badly damaged the building. The interior had to be rebuilt, and the work was led by the German architect Martin Dülfer. The theatre reopened at the end of the 1920s. So the walls you see carry the original Viennese design, but much of the inside comes from this second life.

The building was hit again during the Second World War. Allied air raids struck Sofia in 1943 and 1944, and the theatre was among the damaged buildings. Once more it was repaired. Later in the twentieth century there were further renovations. Today the structure carries layers of these repairs, though from the garden it looks like one calm, complete design. That calm surface hides a story of fire, bombing and patient rebuilding.

The name on the building honours Ivan Vazov. For Bulgarians, he is the national poet — a writer whose verses many people learn at school. His best-known novel, Under the Yoke, tells the story of the April Uprising against Ottoman rule in 1876. Vazov also wrote plays, and some of them were staged here. The theatre took his name in the second half of the twentieth century. It links the building to the writer who gave modern Bulgarian literature much of its voice.

Inside, the main hall follows the classic pattern of a European horseshoe auditorium. Tiers of balconies curve around the stalls, so many seats look down on the stage. There is red upholstery, gilding and a painted ceiling. The building also holds smaller stages for more intimate and experimental work. If you can attend a performance, even in a language you do not speak, the room itself is worth the evening. Watching a Bulgarian audience settle into their seats tells you a lot about the city.

What plays here? The repertoire mixes Bulgarian classics with world drama. Shakespeare, Chekhov and modern European writers appear alongside local playwrights. Most performances are in Bulgarian. Some productions travel abroad, and the theatre hosts guest companies too. For Sofia residents, a night here is still a small occasion. People dress a little better, meet friends at the fountain, and talk in the garden during the interval.

That garden deserves its own moment. It is the City Garden, one of the oldest public parks in Sofia. Turn around and you will see benches, old trees and, often, groups of men gathered around chess and backgammon boards. The games can draw small crowds of silent watchers. Children run around the fountain, and in summer students sit along its edge. The theatre is the backdrop to all of this daily life, not only to the evening performances.

From here, the centre of Sofia is easy to read. A short walk takes you toward the wide boulevard called Tsar Osvoboditel, which means Tsar Liberator. Along it you will find the former royal palace, now home to the National Art Gallery, and the small Russian Church with its golden domes. The theatre sits at the heart of this cluster of state and cultural buildings. It was placed here on purpose, among the institutions of the young nation.

Before you move on, look once more at the front of the building. Notice how the white columns catch the light against the red walls. Listen to the fountain and the chess pieces, and the voices of people crossing the garden. This stage has burned, been bombed and rebuilt, and still it opens its doors almost every evening. As you walk away, glance back from the far side of the park. From there the whole facade fits in one view — calm, symmetrical and very much alive.
