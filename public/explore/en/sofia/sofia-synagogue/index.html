The guide

The story

Stand still on the pavement for a moment and listen. Trams grind past on the boulevard nearby. Traders call out from the market hall across the street. Then lift your eyes. Above the noise rises a wide building of pale stone, striped with bands of contrasting colour. It has arched windows and a dome that looks more like Vienna or Granada than central Sofia. This is the Sofia Synagogue, the main house of prayer for the city's Jewish community.

The building opened in 1909, after several years of construction. It was designed by the Austrian architect Friedrich Grünanger. He drew on a style that was popular across Central Europe at the time, usually called Moorish Revival. In simple terms, this style borrows the horseshoe arches, geometric patterns and domes of medieval Islamic Spain. Many European synagogues of that era used it. The Moorish look reminded congregations of the Sephardic Jewish past on the Iberian Peninsula.

That Spanish connection is not just decoration. Most Jews in Bulgaria are Sephardic. Their ancestors were expelled from Spain at the end of the fifteenth century. Many of them found refuge in the Ottoman Empire, which then ruled these lands. For centuries, families here spoke Ladino, a language based on old Spanish and written in Hebrew script. So when you look at these arches, you are looking at a memory of Spain, carried across the Mediterranean and rebuilt in the Balkans.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. This is the largest Sephardic synagogue in Southeastern Europe. It is often described as one of the largest synagogues in all of Europe. Inside, the main hall was built to hold more than a thousand worshippers. That scale tells you something about Sofia in the early twentieth century. The Jewish community was large, confident and closely woven into the life of the young Bulgarian capital.

The opening day was a major public event. Tsar Ferdinand of Bulgaria attended the consecration in 1909. His presence mattered. Bulgaria had only recently become a fully independent kingdom. A royal visit to the new synagogue sent a clear signal that the Jewish community belonged to the nation. Look at the street around you, and you can see that idea in stone. Few places in Europe gather so many faiths within a few minutes' walk.

Turn slowly and take in the neighbourhood. Just behind you stands the Central Market Hall, with its own early twentieth century facade. A short walk away is the Banya Bashi Mosque, built in the Ottoman period, with its single minaret. Nearby are the Orthodox Cathedral of Saint Nedelya and the Catholic Cathedral of Saint Joseph. Locals sometimes call this area the square of religious tolerance. It is one of the clearest pictures of Sofia's layered past.

If the doors are open, step inside. The interior is a single, tall space under the central dome. The light falls in from windows high above. Colour covers almost every surface... deep blues, warm golds, reds and greens arranged in intricate patterns. There are slender columns, carved balconies and painted arches. The women's galleries run along the upper level, a traditional layout in Orthodox synagogues. The hall feels grand, but also enclosed and calm after the street.

Now look up, toward the very centre. Hanging from the dome is an enormous brass chandelier. This is the second surprise. It weighs more than two tonnes and is often described as the largest chandelier in Bulgaria. Local tradition says it was made in Vienna. Its many arms spread out in rings, catching the light from the windows. When it is lit, the gold tones of the walls warm noticeably. Take a moment to stand beneath it.

The building carries harder memories too. During the Second World War, Bulgaria was allied with Nazi Germany and passed harsh anti-Jewish laws. In 1943, plans were made to deport Bulgaria's Jews. Public protest followed. Members of parliament, Orthodox church leaders and ordinary citizens spoke out. The deportations from Bulgaria's own territory were stopped. Most Bulgarian Jews survived the war. However, more than eleven thousand Jews from Bulgarian-occupied areas of Thrace and Macedonia were deported and murdered. Both facts belong to this story.

The synagogue itself was damaged during Allied bombing of Sofia in 1944. Part of its library was lost. After the war, life changed again. When the State of Israel was founded in 1948, the great majority of Bulgaria's Jews emigrated within a few years. Under the communist government that followed, the congregation that remained was small. A hall built for a thousand people often held only a handful of worshippers. The building waited, half asleep, for decades.

After the end of communism in 1989, attention returned to the synagogue. Restoration work took place over the following years. Painters and craftspeople repaired the decoration, the stonework and the dome. Today the building is again an active synagogue for the Jewish community of Sofia. It also houses a museum of Jewish history in Bulgaria. The exhibits tell the story of the community over the centuries, including the events of 1943. Allow some extra time if you plan to visit it.

A practical note. This is a working place of worship, so the rules are those of a religious space. Security checks are normal at the entrance. Dress modestly, keep your voice low, and follow any guidance from the staff. Photography rules may vary, so ask before you raise your camera inside. On the Sabbath and on Jewish holidays, access for visitors may be limited. Treat your visit as a guest's visit, and the building will reward your attention.

Before you walk on, look once more at the facade. Notice how the stone stripes, the arched windows and the dome all work together. A Viennese architect, a Spanish memory and a Balkan city met here more than a century ago. Behind you, the market still hums. The trams still pass. Somewhere a little way off, a call to prayer or a church bell may rise above the traffic. Listen for it as you leave.
