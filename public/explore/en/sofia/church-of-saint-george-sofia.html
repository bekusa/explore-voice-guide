The guide

The story

Leave the traffic of the boulevard behind and walk into a quiet courtyard. Grey stone government buildings stand on every side. In the middle, lower than the street around it, sits a small round building made of thin red bricks. It looks almost too modest for its setting. Yet this little rotunda is usually described as the oldest standing building in Sofia, a capital that has grown up and around it for well over sixteen centuries.

This is the Church of Saint George, often simply called the Rotunda. It is an early Christian building from the late Roman period, generally dated to the fourth century. Its shape is simple. A round drum of brick rises from the ground and is topped by a dome. Nothing about it shouts. Take a moment to walk slowly around it before you go in, because the outside tells its own story.

Look first at the walls. The builders laid rows of flat, thin bricks, separated by thick bands of mortar. This was a common Roman technique. It made walls strong and flexible, and it gives the surface a striped, layered look. In some places the brickwork is crisp. In others it is patched and uneven. That patchwork is honest evidence of many centuries of repair, damage, and care.

Now notice the ground level. The church sits in a hollow, below the modern pavement. This is not an accident of design. When this building was new, the street level was much lower. Over the centuries, the city rose on its own debris — layer after layer of rubble, soil, and new foundations. Standing here, you are looking down into the ancient city, not up at it.

That ancient city was called Serdica. It was an important Roman town on the roads that linked the eastern and western parts of the empire. Around the Rotunda you can see excavated remains: the outlines of walls, the bases of columns, and the paving of an old Roman street. These ruins belonged to a larger complex of buildings. Historians still discuss exactly what that complex was, and what this round building was first used for.

Some scholars think the rotunda may have served as a baptistery, a building where new Christians were baptised. Others connect it with a public or official function within the Roman complex. The honest answer is that its earliest purpose is not fully certain. What is clear is that it became a Christian church, and that it has carried the name of Saint George, a soldier saint deeply loved across the Orthodox world.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The courtyard around you is not a historic square at all. It is enclosed by large buildings from the mid-twentieth century, including the offices of the Bulgarian Presidency and a large hotel. They were built around the church and its ruins. So the oldest building in the city sits inside the walls of one of its most modern political spaces. Ancient and official Sofia share a single small yard.

Step inside now, if the church is open. Your eyes will need a moment. The interior is round, dim, and surprisingly tall for such a small building. The light falls from windows high in the drum. The air is cool and often carries the smell of candle wax. This is an active Orthodox church, so speak softly and move with care, especially if a service is taking place.

Look up into the dome. It is covered with painted figures — a ring of prophets around the centre, each one standing in long robes. Below them, on the walls, you can see more paintings. These are frescoes, which means the paint was applied to wet plaster so that the colour bonds with the wall itself. The faces are calm and solemn. The colours are faded, but still warm: ochre, deep red, soft blue.

The second surprise is that you are looking at several layers of painting at once. Specialists have identified frescoes from different periods of the Middle Ages, painted one on top of another over hundreds of years. In some places restorers have exposed an older layer beneath a newer one. So one wall can carry the work of painters who never met, separated by generations. Look closely at the edges where one layer ends and another begins.

There is a reason these paintings survived at all. During the long Ottoman period, the Rotunda was converted into a mosque. The Christian images were covered over with plaster. For a long time they were hidden from view. That covering protected much of the painting from light and wear. In the twentieth century, restoration work uncovered the frescoes again, and the building returned to use as a church.

Think about everything this one room has seen. Roman officials, early Christians, medieval painters, Ottoman worshippers, modern restorers, and today, you. Each group used the same round space in its own way. Very few buildings anywhere have changed purpose so many times and still kept their basic shape. The walls show all of these lives. Nothing here was wiped clean and started again.

When you step back outside, walk once more along the edge of the ruins. Look for the old street paving, worn smooth by traffic long before the city had its present name. Notice how the red brick of the church glows warmly against the grey stone around it, especially in late afternoon light. Listen too. The city noise softens here, as if the courtyard holds it back.

As you leave, glance back once from the courtyard entrance. From there, the Rotunda looks small again, almost hidden between the larger buildings. That is perhaps the best way to remember it. Not as a grand monument, but as a quiet survivor. Sofia has changed its rulers, its languages, and its faiths many times. This round red church has simply stayed where it stands.
