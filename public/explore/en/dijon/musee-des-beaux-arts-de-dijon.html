The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Footsteps on worn stone, a low murmur of voices, and somewhere a door that closes softly. You are standing inside the old palace of the Dukes of Burgundy. The pale limestone around you has been touched by many generations. It feels cool under the hand, even in summer. Before we talk about paintings or princes, notice the light. It falls through tall windows in long bars and lands on polished floors. This museum lives inside a building that was once the centre of power in a whole region.

Outside, the palace faces the Place de la Libération. That curved square was laid out in the seventeenth century, and it was designed to frame the ducal palace like a stage set. Jules Hardouin-Mansart, an architect who also worked at Versailles, shaped this classical face. Behind it, older medieval walls remain. So the building is not one thing. It is several centuries stacked together. Some parts are Gothic, some are classical, and some are recent. City hall still shares the complex today, so officials and art lovers walk through the same courtyards.

Here is the first surprise. This is one of the oldest museums in France. Its roots go back to the late eighteenth century, before the Revolution. It began beside a local drawing school, where students needed good models to copy. Then the Revolution changed everything. Churches and monasteries were closed, and their treasures needed a new home. Many of them came here. That is why so much of what you see once stood in chapels and abbeys around Dijon.

Now, to understand this place, you need to meet the dukes. In the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, the Valois Dukes of Burgundy were among the richest rulers in Europe. Their lands stretched far to the north, into what is now Belgium and the Netherlands. Philip the Bold, John the Fearless, Philip the Good, and Charles the Bold—each had a nickname, and each wanted to be remembered. Their wealth paid for Flemish painters and sculptors, who travelled south to work for them.

The heart of the museum is the great hall known as the Guards' Room. Walk in slowly. The ceiling rises high above you. At the centre lie two enormous tombs. One belongs to Philip the Bold. The other holds John the Fearless and his wife, Margaret of Bavaria. Their figures rest on black stone slabs, hands joined in prayer. Angels with folded wings kneel at their heads. Lions lie quietly at their feet.

But the real wonder is below the slabs. Look down at the arcades around the base. Small alabaster figures walk in a slow procession beneath delicate stone arches. These are the mourners. Each one is different. Some hide their faces in deep hoods. Some hold a book. Some wipe a tear, or seem lost in thought. The sculptor Claus Sluter, a master from the Low Countries, gave these figures a weight and emotion that was new for his time.

Here is the second surprise. Philip the Bold's tomb took decades to finish. Several sculptors worked on it in turn. When one master died, another continued the work. These tombs did not begin here, either. They stood in the Chartreuse de Champmol, a monastery the dukes founded on the edge of Dijon. After the Revolution, the tombs were damaged and later restored, and they eventually came to this hall. And some years ago, while the room was renovated, the mourners went on tour. They crossed the Atlantic and visited museums in the United States.

At the end of the hall, look for the great carved altarpieces. They also came from Champmol. Their gilded wooden figures are carved in deep relief, almost like tiny theatres. Their painted wings once opened and closed for religious services. These are rare survivors of late medieval art. Stand close, and you will see small faces, folds of cloth, and patterns of gold leaf. Then step back, and the whole scene comes together as one shining wall.

Nearby, in the rooms of early painting, you can find work by Flemish and Burgundian masters. One small Nativity scene is attributed to the Master of Flémalle, a painter often identified with Robert Campin. Look at the landscape behind the figures. There is a road, a winter field, and a low sun. It was painted in the fifteenth century, yet it feels very close to life. This was the moment when painters in the north began to look carefully at the real world.

Now let us leave the princes and visit the servants. The ducal kitchens date from the fifteenth century. They are a high stone room with great fireplaces built into the walls. Smoke rose through a vaulted opening at the top. Imagine the heat, the noise, the smell of roasting meat, and the cooks shouting across the floor. Banquets for the dukes were famous for their size. This room was where that display of power was cooked, carried, and served.

As you move upward through the museum, the centuries move with you. There are paintings from Italy and France, sculpture from the classical period, and work from the nineteenth century. A recent renovation, finished in the late 2010s, opened new routes through the palace. It also brought more daylight into the galleries. In the upper rooms, modern art appears, including a generous collection given by private donors. You may find works by painters such as Nicolas de Staël, whose thick colours feel like slabs of light.

Above the palace rises the Tower of Philip the Good. It is a slim stone tower that watched over the city for centuries. Visitors can sometimes climb it, usually as part of a guided visit. From the top, the roofs of Dijon spread out below you. Look for the coloured glazed tiles on some roofs, set in patterns of yellow, green, red, and black. These tiles are a famous sign of Burgundy. From up there, you understand how the palace sat at the centre of everything.

Take a few minutes to rest in a quiet gallery before you leave. This museum is large, and you do not need to see everything in one visit. Many people come back to the Guards' Room again and again, just to stand with the mourners. Each time, someone notices a new gesture—a hand on a sleeve, a face turned away. It is a good place for patience. The tombs were made to be looked at slowly.

When you step outside, turn and look back at the palace. The same stone you touched inside now catches the open sky. Around you, the square fills with people, cafés, and the sound of water from the fountains. Somewhere behind those walls, the small alabaster figures continue their silent walk. They have mourned the dukes for six centuries. Now, for a moment, you have walked with them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start in the Guards' Room before the crowds arrive.
Ask at the desk about guided climbs up the tower.
Split your visit across two days to avoid fatigue.
Bring a small notebook to sketch the mourners.
Leave time to walk the Place de la Libération afterward.
