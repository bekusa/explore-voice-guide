The guide

The story

Start with the water. A wide, still moat runs beside you, its surface green and heavy, broken only by lotus leaves and the odd ripple from a fish. Beyond it rises a long wall of red-brown brick. Every so often, a small tiered wooden spire sits on top of the wall, like a tiny temple roof. The traffic of Mandalay hums behind you. In front of you, everything feels slower, flatter, and very deliberate.

This is Mandalay Palace, the last royal palace of the Burmese kings. It was the heart of the Konbaung dynasty, the final ruling house of Burma before British rule. What you see is not one building but a whole walled city in miniature. The palace buildings sit at the exact centre of a vast square. The square, the walls, and the moat were all laid out as one design, at one time, by one king.

That king was Mindon. In the 1850s he decided to build a new royal capital at the foot of Mandalay Hill. Burmese kings often moved their capitals, and there was a religious reason for this one. According to a popular tradition, the Buddha once visited Mandalay Hill and foretold that a great city of the faith would rise below it. Mindon presented his new capital as the fulfilment of that prophecy. The city and its palace were founded in the late 1850s.

Here is the first surprise. Many of the palace buildings were not new at all. Mindon had the wooden halls of the old palace at Amarapura, the previous capital a short distance to the south, taken apart. They were carried here and put back together. Teak was the material of royalty in Burma. It was strong, it resisted insects, and it could be carved, lacquered, and covered in gold leaf.

Now look at the plan of the place. The walls form an almost perfect square, and each side runs for roughly two kilometres. Along the walls stand dozens of bastions, each crowned by one of those small spires. The Burmese word for this kind of tiered roof is pyatthat. It was a sign of royal or sacred status. Ordinary houses were never allowed to use it. So when you see that spire, you are looking at a symbol of power repeated again and again around the citadel.

The square was also a map of the universe. In traditional Burmese cosmology, the world has a sacred mountain at its centre, called Mount Meru. The king's throne room stood at the centre of the palace, and the palace stood at the centre of the city. The king, in other words, sat at the symbolic middle of the world. Gates were placed on all four sides, and each gate was linked to a sign of the zodiac.

Inside, the palace was a crowded world of halls, apartments, gardens, and offices. There were audience halls for receiving officials and envoys. There were private quarters for the king and his many queens. At the heart of it all stood the Great Audience Hall, with a soaring spire above the most important throne, the Lion Throne. That throne survived the events that followed. Today it is kept in the National Museum in Yangon, not here.

The palace had only a short royal life. Mindon died in the late 1870s. His son Thibaw became king, with Queen Supayalat at his side. In 1885, after the Third Anglo-Burmese War, British forces entered Mandalay. Thibaw and Supayalat were sent into exile in India. They left the palace in an ox cart, watched by crowds of their subjects. The Burmese monarchy ended here, inside these walls, in less than three decades.

The British turned the royal city into a military base. They renamed it Fort Dufferin, after the Viceroy of India at the time. Halls built for court ceremonies became clubs, offices, and barracks. Then came the Second World War. In 1945, during heavy fighting between Japanese and Allied forces for Mandalay, fire destroyed almost all of the wooden palace. Centuries of craft in teak and gold were lost in a matter of days.

So what are you looking at today? Most of the buildings inside are reconstructions, built mainly in the 1990s. They follow the old layout and shapes, but many use concrete, steel, and modern roofing instead of carved teak. Some visitors feel disappointed by this. Others find it moving. The buildings work like a full-size model, letting you understand the scale and rhythm of the court, even if the original materials are gone.

Here is the second surprise. One piece of the original palace still exists, but not here. After Mindon died, Thibaw had one of his father's apartments taken down and moved outside the walls. It was rebuilt as a monastery. Because it stood outside the citadel, it escaped the fire of 1945. Today it is known as Shwenandaw Monastery, and its dark carved teak gives you the best idea of how these halls once looked.

Inside the compound, look for the round watchtower near the palace buildings. A spiral path climbs it, and from the top you can see the rooftops laid out in their grid, with Mandalay Hill to the north. Much of the area inside the walls is still used by the military, so visitors usually enter through a single gate and stay on marked routes. A strong earthquake in 2025 also damaged parts of the city and the palace area, so some sections may be closed or under repair.

As you walk back toward the moat, notice how quiet the walls make the city feel. The red brick, the white-and-gold spires, the flat water, the hill on the horizon... all of it still follows the plan of one king who wanted his city to sit at the centre of the world. The wood has burned, the court is gone, but the square remains. Take a last look along the wall, and count the spires as they fade into the distance.
