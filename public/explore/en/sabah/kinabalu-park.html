The guide

The story

Breathe in first. The air here is cool and damp, and it smells of wet moss, leaf litter and something faintly sweet, like crushed ginger. Mist moves between the trees. Somewhere close, water drips from a fern onto a broad leaf. Then the cloud thins, just for a moment, and a wall of grey granite appears high above the forest. That is Mount Kinabalu, the tallest mountain in Malaysia, and you are standing in the park built around it.

Kinabalu Park lies in the state of Sabah, on the northern tip of the island of Borneo. It was established in 1964. In 2000 it became Malaysia's first UNESCO World Heritage Site. The park covers more than seven hundred square kilometres of mountain, ridge and forest. Most visitors arrive at the park headquarters, which sits at roughly 1,500 metres above sea level. Even here, far below the summit, the temperature feels very different from the humid coast you left behind.

The mountain itself rises to about 4,095 metres. Its highest point is called Low's Peak. The name comes from Hugh Low, a British colonial official and keen naturalist. In 1851 he made the first recorded climb onto the summit plateau. He did not reach the very top. That honour went to later climbers. But Low collected plants on the way, and his specimens helped show the scientific world how unusual this mountain really was.

Here is the first surprise. Kinabalu is a young mountain in geological terms. Its core is a huge body of granite that pushed up from deep underground over millions of years. During the last ice age, glaciers covered the summit. They scraped the rock smooth and carved the jagged spires you can see on clear days. A tropical island, almost on the equator, once carried ice. The bare, polished slabs near the top are the marks the glaciers left behind.

For the local people, the mountain has always meant more than rock. The Kadazan-Dusun, one of the main indigenous communities of Sabah, hold Kinabalu as sacred. In their tradition, the spirits of the dead travel to the mountain to rest. One popular explanation links the name to the phrase "Aki Nabalu", often translated as "the revered place of the dead". Other stories exist too, and scholars do not agree on a single origin. What is clear is the deep respect the mountain still receives.

Now look at the forest around you. As you climb this mountain, the vegetation changes in clear bands. Lowland rainforest gives way to oak and chestnut forest. Higher up, you enter cloud forest, where trees are short and twisted and every branch carries moss. Near the summit, only small shrubs and grasses survive between the rocks. In a single day of walking, you pass through plant zones that would cover thousands of kilometres if you travelled from the tropics towards the poles.

This variety is the reason for the World Heritage listing. Botanists count Kinabalu among the richest plant areas on Earth. The park holds thousands of plant species, and many grow nowhere else. There are hundreds of kinds of orchids alone. There are wild rhododendrons, mosses, ferns and tiny flowering plants that cling to wet rock. Some species are known only from a single ridge or valley inside the park. Scientists are still describing new ones.

The second surprise is a plant that eats meat. Kinabalu is famous for its pitcher plants. These plants grow leaves shaped like small jugs filled with liquid. Insects fall in and cannot climb out. The largest is Nepenthes rajah, named after the Brooke family, the British rajahs who once ruled nearby Sarawak. Its pitchers are big enough that researchers have found the remains of small animals inside. Even more strangely, tree shrews visit some pitchers to lick sweet nectar, and leave droppings that feed the plant.

Animals are here too, though most stay hidden. Listen for birds calling through the mist, and for the steady hum of insects. The park shelters many bird species found only on the mountains of Borneo. Squirrels run along the branches, and at night frogs call from the streams. You are more likely to hear the wildlife than to see it. A slow walk, with frequent stops, will show you much more than a fast one.

The mountain also carries a painful memory. In June 2015, a strong earthquake struck near the town of Ranau, close to the park. Rockfalls hit the summit trail while climbers were on the mountain, and eighteen people lost their lives, among them school pupils and mountain guides. Parts of the famous rock spires broke away. Some local people linked the disaster to disrespect shown on the summit shortly before. The trail was repaired and reopened, and the event is still remembered with great sadness.

If you plan to climb to the summit, you will need a permit and a licensed mountain guide. Most climbers take two days. They walk up to the rest huts at Laban Rata on the first day, sleep for a few hours, then start again long before dawn. The goal is to reach the summit as the sun rises over Borneo. The final section crosses bare granite in the dark, with ropes to hold, and the air is thin and very cold.

You do not need to reach the top to understand this place. Around the headquarters, short forest trails loop through moss and tree ferns. A botanical garden near the entrance introduces many of the mountain's plants, including orchids and pitcher plants, at a gentle pace. Further away, the Poring area of the park offers warm sulphur springs and a canopy walkway high in the trees. Each of these places shows a different face of the same mountain.

Standing here, it helps to remember the scale of time around you. The granite took millions of years to rise. The ice came and went. Plants arrived and changed into species found nowhere else. People came, gave the mountain a sacred meaning, and still guide climbers up its slopes today. The park protects all of these layers together. That is what makes it more than a mountain view.

Before you move on, stop for a moment and look up. The cloud is probably moving again, opening and closing over the summit. Notice the drops of water on the moss, and the cool air against your face. Listen to the birds and the water running somewhere below the path. As you walk away, keep watching the edges of the trail. A tiny orchid, or a small green pitcher, may be waiting just above the ground.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book the summit permit and licensed guide well in advance.
Arrive early, before afternoon cloud usually covers the peak.
Pack a warm layer and rain jacket, even on sunny mornings.
Walk the headquarters trails slowly to spot wildlife and orchids.
Combine your visit with Poring's hot springs and canopy walk.
