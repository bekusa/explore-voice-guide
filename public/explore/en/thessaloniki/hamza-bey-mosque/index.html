The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement of Egnatia Street for a moment and listen. Buses grind past, scooters weave between lanes, and a kiosk owner calls out to a regular. Then look at the low, heavy building of pale stone and brick right beside you. It sits slightly below the level of the modern street, as if the city has slowly risen around it. That small drop in height is your first clue. You are looking at a building far older than anything else on this corner.

This is the Hamza Bey Mosque, one of the oldest Ottoman buildings in Thessaloniki. To understand why it stands here, go back to 1430. In that year, Ottoman forces under Sultan Murad the Second captured the city after a long siege. For the next five centuries, Thessaloniki was an Ottoman city. It was a crowded port where Muslims, Christians and, later, a large Sephardic Jewish community lived side by side, each with their own places of worship.

In the first decades after the conquest, many of the city's new mosques were simply converted Byzantine churches. This building is different, and this is the surprise many visitors miss. It was built from the ground up as a mosque, in the 1460s. That makes it one of the earliest purpose-built mosques in the city. The person behind it was not a sultan or a general. It was a woman named Hafsa, the daughter of an Ottoman commander called Hamza Bey.

Pause on that detail. In the Ottoman world, wealthy women often paid for public buildings — mosques, fountains, schools, soup kitchens. These gifts were an act of piety and also a way to leave a lasting name. Hafsa chose to honour her father, and so the mosque carries his name rather than hers. Today, very few people walking past know that a woman's money and devotion raised these walls more than five hundred years ago.

The building did not stay exactly as Hafsa first built it. In the late sixteenth century it was rebuilt and enlarged, as the city's Muslim population grew. Look at the walls and you can see the typical Ottoman building method of the period. Courses of cut stone alternate with bands of thin red brick. This pattern made walls strong, but it also gave them a striped, almost woven appearance. Notice how the colours change in the sunlight, from honey to rose.

The mosque has an unusual layout for Thessaloniki. The prayer hall sits behind an enclosed courtyard, once lined with a covered arcade. In a traditional mosque, a courtyard like this gave worshippers a calm space before prayer. People would wash at a fountain, leave the noise of the market behind, and walk into the cool, quiet hall. Try to picture that silence now, only a few steps away from the traffic on Egnatia.

You may notice one thing that is missing. There is no minaret, the tall slender tower from which the call to prayer was sung five times a day. Thessaloniki once had many minarets rising over its rooftops. After the city became part of Greece in 1912, and especially after the population exchange between Greece and Turkey in the 1920s, almost all of them were taken down. The city's skyline changed completely within a generation.

That population exchange is the turning point in this building's story. The Muslim residents of Thessaloniki left for Turkey, and Greek Orthodox refugees arrived from Anatolia. Mosques across the city lost their congregations overnight. Some became warehouses, some museums, some were demolished. This one found a stranger second life. For much of the twentieth century it worked as a cinema, known to locals as the Alcazar. Many older residents of the city still use that name.

Imagine it for a second. Under the old Ottoman stonework, rows of seats faced a bright screen. Families came to watch films where people once came to pray. Shops also grew up around the building, pressing against its walls. Over time, the mosque almost disappeared behind the ordinary business of the street. For many years, passers-by saw shop signs and posters, not a fifteenth-century monument.

In more recent decades, the Greek archaeological services have worked to rescue the building. The later additions were cleared, the walls were studied and repaired, and the structure was protected as a historic monument. Restoration on a building this old is slow work. Every stone and every brick has to be understood before it is touched. The aim is not to make it look new, but to keep it standing and readable.

Here is a second thing worth knowing. The ground around you is full of history too. When workers built the Thessaloniki Metro, the station at Venizelou, just beside this corner, uncovered a long stretch of a Byzantine street with paving stones and the remains of buildings. Egnatia Street itself follows the line of the ancient Via Egnatia, a Roman road that once linked the Adriatic coast with Constantinople. You are standing on layers of Roman, Byzantine and Ottoman life.

This is what makes the corner so special in Thessaloniki. In one glance, you can see a modern Greek street, an Ottoman mosque, and, below your feet, a Roman and Byzantine road. The city did not replace its past. It built on top of it, layer after layer. The Hamza Bey Mosque is one of the few places where you can see that process clearly, because the old walls still sit below the new pavement.

Before you move on, take one more slow look. Follow a single band of red brick with your eyes as it runs along the wall. Notice the small difference in height between the street and the building's base. Listen again to the traffic and the voices of the market streets close by. When you walk away along Egnatia, you will be walking the same line that soldiers, merchants and pilgrims followed for two thousand years.
