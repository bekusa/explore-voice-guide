The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Scooters buzz along Egnatia Street. Students talk at café tables. Somewhere a bus brakes with a long sigh. And in the middle of all this ordinary noise stands a thick column of brick and grey marble, crowded with carved soldiers. Locals simply call it Kamara, the Arch. For many people in Thessaloniki it is not a monument at all. It is the place where you say, meet me at Kamara.

The arch was built around the year 300, at the end of the third century and the start of the fourth. It celebrated a Roman emperor named Galerius and his victory over the Sasanian Persians, the great rival empire to the east. Galerius was one of four men who shared power over the Roman world at that time. Historians call this system the Tetrarchy, from the Greek for rule by four. Galerius chose Thessaloniki as his base, and he rebuilt part of the city to match his ambitions.

What you see today is only a fragment. The original gateway was much larger. It stood on many pillars and carried a dome or vaulted roof above the crossing. Only part of that structure survives, a few of the massive piers and the brick arches above them. Try to picture the missing parts. Imagine a covered passage here, wide enough for processions, with the Roman road running straight through it.

That road matters. Egnatia Street follows the line of the Via Egnatia, one of the great Roman highways. It crossed the Balkans and connected the Adriatic coast with the east. So the arch did not stand on a quiet side lane. It stood on a main artery of the empire. Travellers, merchants and soldiers passed beneath it. Today the traffic is different, but the line of the road is still in use.

Now step closer to the marble panels on the lower piers. They are arranged in bands, one above another, like the pages of a stone comic strip. You can see horsemen, marching soldiers, captives and crowds. In some scenes the emperors sit enthroned, surrounded by figures who represent the order of the Roman world. The marble is worn and many faces are damaged. Still, the energy of the carving comes through. Look for the overlapping bodies and the sense of movement.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The arch was not a lonely monument. It formed part of a larger imperial complex. To the south, toward the sea, lay the palace of Galerius. Its ruins can still be seen around Navarinou Square, a short walk away. To the north, a ceremonial avenue led uphill to a great round building. Turn and look up the slope now. That building is still there.

This is the Rotunda. It is a huge cylinder of brick, crowned by a dome. It was built in the early fourth century, around the same time as the arch. Its walls are several metres thick. That thickness is the reason the dome has survived for so long. Scholars still debate its first purpose. Some believe Galerius meant it as his mausoleum. Others think it was a temple or a hall linked to the imperial cult.

If it was a tomb, it never received its owner. Galerius died around 311 and was buried far to the north, in what is now Serbia. The Rotunda stood waiting for a new role. Within a century or so, the Roman world had changed. Christianity became the official faith of the empire. The round building was converted into a church. Builders added a sanctuary on the eastern side and made other changes for Christian worship.

The church received some of the finest early Christian mosaics anywhere in the Mediterranean world. High in the dome, fragments still survive. Saints and martyrs stand with raised hands in front of golden backgrounds. Behind them rise fantastic buildings with columns, arches and curtains, almost like stage sets. Their colours include deep blue, green and shimmering gold. When light enters through the windows, the small pieces of glass catch it. Bring patience, because the mosaics are high and best seen slowly.

The next chapter came with the Ottomans. In 1590 the church was converted into a mosque. A slender minaret was added beside it, and that minaret is the second surprise of this visit. It still stands today. It is one of the very few minarets left in Thessaloniki. After the city became part of Greece in 1912, the building became a church again, dedicated to Saint George. Many locals still call it Agios Georgios.

So one structure carries three faiths and three empires: Roman, Byzantine and Ottoman. Few buildings in Europe show that layering so plainly. The Rotunda was damaged in the earthquake of 1978 and needed long restoration. In 1988 it became part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing. That listing covers the early Christian and Byzantine monuments of Thessaloniki. Today the building is used mostly as a monument and an exhibition space, with services held on special occasions.

As you walk between the two, notice how the modern city hugs them. Apartment blocks, bakeries and student bars press close. The area sits near the university, so the square is often full of young people. Pigeons gather on the paving. In the evening, floodlights warm the brick of the arch to an orange glow. Roman stone and everyday life share the same few square metres, and nobody here seems to find that strange.

Before you go, look once more at the arch. Find one small carved face that has survived seventeen centuries of weather, war and earthquakes. Then look up the street toward the dome and the thin white minaret. The ancient processional route still connects them, now as a pedestrian street. As you walk away along Egnatia, listen again to the traffic. It is the same road Romans used, still carrying people somewhere.

Key facts

Both monuments date to around the year 300.

Look closer

What to look for

Stacked bands of marble reliefs on the arch's lower piers.
Enthroned emperors carved among crowded figures on the panels.
The slender Ottoman minaret standing beside the Rotunda.
Gold-ground mosaic saints with raised hands in the dome.
Fantastic mosaic buildings with columns and curtains behind saints.
Before you go

Practical tips

Meet friends at Kamara, the locals' favourite meeting point.
Walk the pedestrian street from the arch uphill to the Rotunda.
Continue south to Navarinou Square to see the palace ruins.
Visit the arch after dark to see the warm floodlighting.
Bring binoculars or a zoom lens for the dome mosaics.
