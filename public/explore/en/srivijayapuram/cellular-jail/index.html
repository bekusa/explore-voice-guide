The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind comes off the sea. A gull calls somewhere below. Then you notice the walls. They are dark red brick, rough under the hand, and they run in long, straight lines toward a single tower. The corridors are quiet now. But the quiet here has a weight to it. This building was designed so that hundreds of men could live side by side and never once see each other's faces.

You are standing inside the Cellular Jail, in Srivijayapuram, the capital of India's Andaman and Nicobar Islands. Until 2024, this town was called Port Blair, and many maps and older signs still use that name. The jail sits on high ground facing the water. The British colonial government built it between 1896 and 1906. Its purpose was simple and harsh. It was meant to hold political prisoners from mainland India, far from home and far from any hope of escape.

To understand why it was built here, picture the distance. These islands lie in the Bay of Bengal, more than a thousand kilometres from the Indian mainland. For prisoners, being sent here meant crossing open sea to an unknown place. In India, people called this punishment Kala Pani. The phrase means 'black water'. For many Hindus of that era, crossing the ocean also carried a fear of losing caste and connection to family. So the sentence was a punishment of the mind, as well as the body.

Now look at the shape of the building. From the central tower, the wings spread outward like the spokes of a bicycle wheel. Originally there were seven of these wings. Each one was three storeys high. The design followed a prison model from Victorian Britain, sometimes called the separate system. The idea was isolation. Every prisoner had his own cell, and contact between prisoners was kept as close to zero as possible.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. Walk along a corridor and look at which way the cells face. The front of each cell does not look across at other cells. It faces the back wall of the next wing. So a prisoner, standing at his door, saw only blank brick. He could not see a neighbour. He could not signal with his hands. That is where the name comes from. This is a cellular jail — a prison built entirely of single cells.

Step inside one of the cells if you can. It is small and narrow, with a heavy iron door. High on the back wall there is one small ventilation opening, far above head height. A prisoner could feel the air move but could not look out. Stand there for a few seconds. Notice how quickly the space feels close. Then imagine years of this, with heat and humidity pressing in from the sea outside.

The work was as hard as the isolation. Prisoners were given forced labour, and some of it was deliberately exhausting. One task that appears often in prisoners' accounts is the oil mill. Men were made to turn a heavy press to extract oil from coconuts or mustard seeds, often with a daily quota. Others pounded coconut husks into fibre. Failure to meet the quota could bring further punishment. The jail's museum displays examples of these tools and records of this labour.

Many of the men held here were part of India's struggle for independence from British rule. Among the best known were Vinayak Damodar Savarkar, a writer and political activist, and his brother Ganesh. Prisoners resisted from inside these walls. In the 1930s, inmates went on hunger strikes to protest their conditions. Several men died during these protests, and their names are honoured in the memorial today. Their resistance helped draw public attention in India to what was happening here.

The jail's story did not end with British rule over the islands. During the Second World War, Japanese forces occupied the Andaman Islands for several years. The prison was used again in that period, and the islands' residents faced great hardship. Over the following decades, four of the seven wings were pulled down. Only three remain today. As you look out from the tower, you can trace where the missing wings once stood, and picture the full wheel.

Climb to the top of the central tower if it is open. This was the watch point. From here, guards could look down every corridor at once. Today the view runs the other way, out across the town and the water. You can see the harbour, the green hills, and on clear days other islands in the distance. It is a strange contrast. The most beautiful view in the building belonged to the people keeping watch.

In 1979, the Cellular Jail was declared a national memorial by the government of India. Inside, you will find a museum and photo galleries about the prisoners and the independence movement. Near the entrance burns a memorial flame. Many Indian visitors come here as a kind of pilgrimage. You may see families reading names on the plaques in silence, or older visitors touching the walls. It is a place of memory, and people treat it with respect.

In the evening, the jail hosts a light and sound show in the courtyard. It tells the story of the prison through narration, music and coloured light on the brick walls. It usually needs its own ticket, separate from daytime entry, and it can be popular. The show is a good way to hear the history in one sitting. Even if you skip it, the courtyard at dusk is worth seeing, as the red brick turns deep and dark.

As you walk back toward the entrance, notice the small things. The worn edges of the stone steps. The iron hinges on the cell doors. The narrow strip of sky above each corridor. Listen again to the wind from the sea. The men held here heard that same sound through a small opening high on the wall. They could hear the ocean. They could not see it. Take a moment before you leave... and look out at the water, freely.
