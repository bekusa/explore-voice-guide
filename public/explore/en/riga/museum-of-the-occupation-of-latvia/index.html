The guide

The story

Stand for a moment in front of this building and look at its skin. It is dark, almost black, made of metal panels that hold the grey Baltic light without returning it. Around you, the old town of Riga shows pastel facades, gilded weathercocks and carved gables. And here, in the middle of all that colour, sits a low, heavy box. The contrast is deliberate in its own way. This is a place built to hold difficult memory.

You are standing on Latvian Riflemen Square, at the edge of the medieval core, a short walk from the river Daugava. Behind you rises the reconstructed House of the Blackheads, with its stepped red gable. Nearby, the spire of Saint Peter's Church rises over the rooftops. A tall granite monument of three soldiers stands close by. They are the Latvian Riflemen, units formed during the First World War. Hold onto them. They matter to the story of this building.

Here is the first surprise. This dark structure was not built to tell the story it tells today. It went up in the early 1970s, during the Soviet period, as a museum honouring the Red Latvian Riflemen. Some of those riflemen had fought for the Bolsheviks after the Russian Revolution. So the Soviet state built a shrine to them. Decades later, the same walls were given a new purpose. Today they document what Soviet rule meant for ordinary Latvians.

The Museum of the Occupation of Latvia was founded in the early 1990s, soon after the country restored its independence. It was created by private initiative, with strong support from Latvians living abroad. Many of those exiles had fled the country during the Second World War. Others were the children of families who never came home. Their donations, letters and family objects helped fill the first exhibition. The museum still collects testimony from survivors and their descendants.

The story inside covers roughly half a century, from 1940 to 1991. It begins with a document signed far from Riga. In August 1939, Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union signed a non-aggression treaty, often called the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact. A secret protocol attached to it divided Eastern Europe into spheres of influence. Latvia fell into the Soviet sphere. Within a year, Soviet troops occupied the country, and Latvia was absorbed into the Soviet Union.

The first Soviet year ended with a night many Latvian families still name by its date. On the fourteenth of June 1941, thousands of people were arrested and deported to Siberia and other distant regions. Children, grandparents, teachers and farmers were loaded into freight wagons. Many never returned. A week later, Nazi Germany invaded the Soviet Union, and German forces soon occupied Latvia. One occupation replaced another — and brought a new kind of terror.

The German occupation brought the Holocaust to Latvia. The Jewish community of Riga was forced into a ghetto. In late 1941, tens of thousands of Jews were murdered in the Rumbula forest, just outside the city. Jews deported from Germany and other countries were also killed here. The museum presents this chapter directly, with photographs and documents. It is one of the hardest sections to walk through. Take your time, and pause if you need to.

When the war turned, the Soviet army returned in 1944. Many Latvians fled west by boat and on foot, becoming refugees in Germany and Sweden. Others stayed and resisted. Armed partisans, often called the forest brothers, hid in the woods for years after the war. Then came a second mass deportation, in March 1949. This time the main targets were rural families, especially farmers who resisted collective farms. Tens of thousands were sent east.

One of the most powerful objects inside is a reconstruction of a Gulag barrack. The Gulag was the Soviet network of forced labour camps. The barrack has rough wooden bunks, a small stove and very little light. Visitors often fall silent here. Nearby, you will find small objects made by prisoners and deportees. There are hand-sewn items, carved pieces of wood and letters written on scraps of cloth or birch bark. These objects are small enough to hold in one hand.

The later decades are quieter on the surface, but the museum shows their pressure too. Russian-speaking workers moved into Latvia in large numbers. The Latvian language lost ground in offices and factories. Then, in the late 1980s, people began to speak openly again. On the twenty-third of August 1989, around two million people joined hands across Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania. They formed a human chain hundreds of kilometres long. It became known as the Baltic Way.

Latvia declared the restoration of its independence in May 1990 and secured it in August 1991. In January 1991, people built barricades in the streets of Riga to protect key buildings from Soviet forces. Some of those barricades stood very close to where you are now. Several people lost their lives in those days. Today, many Latvians who stood on the barricades are still alive. Some bring their grandchildren to this museum.

The building itself has gone through a long transformation. For several years the exhibition moved to a temporary home while the old structure was renovated and extended. The plan for the extension was designed under the name House of the Future, and it caused heated public debate. Some people wanted the Soviet-era box removed entirely. Others argued that it should stay, as evidence in its own right. In the end, the dark building remained.

If you want to continue the story after your visit, there is another site in Riga. The former KGB headquarters, a corner building known locally as the Corner House, stands in the newer part of the city. The Soviet security services once questioned and held prisoners there. It is a separate visit, but many people pair the two. Together, they show both the national picture and the rooms where individual lives were changed.

When you step back outside, look again at the old town around you. The cafes, the church bells and the tour groups by the House of the Blackheads continue their day. Much of what you see was destroyed in the war and rebuilt later. The three granite riflemen still stand on their plinth. Behind them, this dark metal box holds names, wagons and letters. Walk slowly toward the river, and notice how ordinary the street feels now.
