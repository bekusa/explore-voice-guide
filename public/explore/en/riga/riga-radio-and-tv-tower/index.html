The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The wind moves across the Daugava River, low and steady, and above you it meets something that does not move at all. Grey concrete rises out of the island grass, smooth and enormous. Tilt your head back, then a little further. The tower keeps going, past the point where your neck says stop. Up at the top, thin antennas cut into the sky like the tip of a needle.

This is the Riga Radio and TV Tower. It stands on Zaķusala, a small island in the middle of the Daugava. The name means Hare Island in Latvian. It is a quiet name for such a large structure. The tower reaches roughly 368 metres into the sky. That makes it the tallest structure in all three Baltic states, taller than anything in Estonia or Lithuania. It is also one of the tallest towers in the whole of Europe.

Before we go into its story, look at its shape. Most television towers in the world are a single column. This one is different — it stands on three legs. Concrete supports lean in towards each other and join high above the ground. Engineers chose this tripod form partly for stability. Riga sits close to the Baltic Sea, and strong winds come in from the coast. Three legs spread the load and keep the tower steady in a storm.

The tower is a product of the Soviet period. At that time, Latvia was part of the Soviet Union. Construction began at the end of the 1970s and lasted into the mid-1980s. Planners in that era liked big, confident engineering. A tower like this sent a message about technology and progress. But it also had a very practical job. It had to carry radio and television signals across Latvia, clearly and over long distances.

That job has not changed. Here is a detail many visitors miss — the tower is not a monument first. It is working infrastructure. Every day, it sends out broadcast signals for television, radio and other communication services. The antennas at the top are the real reason the tower exists. When you look up, you are looking at machinery that reaches homes, cars and phones far beyond the city.

Why build it on an island? Height matters for broadcasting, and so does open space. Zaķusala gave the builders a wide, flat site away from the dense streets of the centre. The river around it keeps other tall buildings at a distance. From here, the signal has a clear path in many directions. The island location also gives the tower its strange, lonely look. It rises from the water almost like a lighthouse.

Now turn your attention to the island itself. Zaķusala is a calm place compared with the Old Town across the water. There are trees, grass and quiet paths near the river bank. Above part of the island runs the Island Bridge, a long road bridge that crosses the Daugava. Cars move over it with a low, constant hum. Underneath, the island feels slow and open. Many people in Riga see the tower every day but rarely walk here.

Part of the way up, high between the legs, there is an observation level. Here is a second surprise. For many years it was one of the best viewpoints in Riga. Visitors rode a lift up and looked out over the whole city. But in recent years, the tower closed to visitors for a long reconstruction. The work focused largely on safety and modern systems. Access can change, so check the current status before you plan a trip upward.

If you do get the chance to go up, the view explains Riga in a single look. The Daugava curves wide and grey below you. On the far bank, the Old Town gathers around its church spires. The tall spire of Saint Peter's Church is usually easy to find. Beyond the centre, you see Soviet-era housing blocks, green parks and the long flat land that runs towards the sea. On clear days, the horizon feels very far away.

Even from the ground, the tower changes with the light. In the morning, the concrete looks pale and cool. At sunset, the low sun turns one side of the legs warm and almost golden, while the other side falls into blue shadow. At night, lights mark the structure so that aircraft can see it. From the riverside in the Old Town, the tower then looks like a thin line of light standing over the water.

For many people in Riga, the tower carries mixed feelings. It comes from the Soviet years, a period that many Latvians remember with pain. Yet it is also simply part of the skyline now. Families grew up with it on the horizon. Children point at it from car windows. It has become a landmark people use to find their way. A building can hold both meanings at once — a reminder of the past, and a familiar piece of home.

Look closely at the concrete as you walk around the base. You can see the lines where builders poured the material in sections, one level after another. Weather has marked the surface over the decades. Rain leaves darker streaks, and the colour shifts from light grey to deeper tones. Up close, the legs feel less like architecture and more like a cliff. It is a good place to understand how much material went into this structure.

A good way to see the tower is to walk towards it slowly. Start on the Old Town side of the river and watch it grow as you approach. From the Island Bridge, you can see how the three legs meet and how the antennas sit on top. Then come down to the island and stand directly underneath. Each distance shows a different side — first a symbol on the skyline, then a piece of engineering, then a wall of stone.

Before you leave, stand at the base one more time and look straight up. Watch the clouds move behind the antennas. For a second it can seem as if the tower itself is moving. It is not. It is doing what it has done since the 1980s — standing in the wind and sending invisible signals across the country. As you walk back towards the river, listen to the bridge, the water and the quiet of Hare Island behind you.
