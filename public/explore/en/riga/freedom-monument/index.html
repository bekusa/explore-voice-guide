The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Tram bells, footsteps on paving stones, the rustle of the trees along the city canal. Then lift your eyes. High above the boulevard, a slim copper woman stands against the sky. Her arms are raised, and in her hands she holds three golden stars. The green of old copper meets the gold of the stars. Below her, the city keeps moving, but here, at the foot of this column, people tend to slow down.

This is the Freedom Monument, and it sits at a meeting point in Riga. Behind you, more or less, lies the Old Town, with its spires and narrow lanes. Ahead runs Freedom Boulevard, into the newer city of wide streets and Art Nouveau facades. Between them flows the old city canal, with the green slopes of Bastejkalns park beside it. The monument stands right on that line, between the old Riga and the modern one.

It was unveiled on 18 November 1935. That date was chosen with care. On 18 November 1918, Latvia declared its independence. The monument was made to honour the people who died in the War of Independence that followed. The design came from the sculptor Kārlis Zāle, working with the architect Ernests Štālbergs. And here is a detail many visitors miss. The money did not come mainly from the state. It came largely from public donations, from ordinary people across the country.

The spot itself carries an older story. Before the First World War, a statue of the Russian emperor Peter the Great stood near here. During the war it was removed and taken away by sea. The ship sank, and the statue went down with it. Years later, the young Latvian state chose this same ground for a monument to its own freedom. One emperor in bronze, replaced by a woman holding three stars.

Locals give that woman a name. They call her Milda. It is a traditional Latvian woman's name, and the nickname feels affectionate rather than official. The three stars in her hands are usually understood to stand for the historic regions of Latvia, often named as Kurzeme, Vidzeme and Latgale. Raised together over her head, they show the idea of a country joined as one. On a sunny day, the gold catches the light long before the rest of the figure does.

Now bring your eyes down to the base. Carved on the front, in large letters, are the words Tēvzemei un Brīvībai. In English, that means For Fatherland and Freedom. The phrase is short. It was meant to be read quickly by anyone passing on foot or on a tram. Many Latvians know these words by heart, and the inscription appears again and again in national ceremonies held here.

The monument is not only a column with a statue on top. Walk slowly around it, and you will find groups of carved figures set around the lower levels. They show scenes from Latvian life and history. Some groups show work, family and spiritual values. Others show soldiers and guardians of the homeland. Several reliefs show moments of struggle, including the revolution of 1905 and battles of the War of Independence. Each side of the base tells a slightly different part of the story.

Look for the figure of Lāčplēsis, the Bear-Slayer. He is the hero of a Latvian national epic, a strong young man who fights the enemies of his people. In folk tradition he became a symbol of courage and resistance. On the monument, you will also find figures in chains, straining to break free. For Latvian viewers in the 1930s, the meaning was clear. A small nation was breaking the chains of its past.

Then came 1940, and the Soviet occupation. Many national monuments in the region were destroyed. This one, surprisingly, was not. It stayed on the boulevard through the long Soviet decades. But it became a place to watch carefully. Laying flowers here could bring trouble with the authorities. People often passed by quietly, looking, but not stopping. The monument stood in silence, and so, for many years, did the people around it.

There is a popular tale from that time. According to one story often repeated in Riga, Soviet guides told visitors that the woman was Mother Russia, and the three stars were the three Baltic republics in her hands. Whether guides really said this, or whether it is a local joke, is hard to know. But Latvians enjoy telling it, with a dry smile. It shows how a single image can carry two very different meanings.

The silence broke in the late 1980s. On 14 June 1987, a group of activists gathered here to lay flowers for the victims of Soviet deportations. It was a small act, but a brave one, and it became one of the early public protests of the independence movement. More gatherings followed. Within a few years, Latvia restored its independence. The monument, which had waited so long, was once again at the centre of the nation's life.

Today, you may see an honour guard standing at the foot of the monument. Two soldiers stand completely still, facing the boulevard. At set times, a ceremonial change of the guard takes place, with slow, careful steps. Children often stop and stare. Tourists lift their phones. Locals sometimes walk past without looking, the way people do with something that is simply part of home. On national holidays, the base fills with flowers and candles.

Before you move on, take one more look up. The column is slender, and Milda seems small against the sky, yet she is easy to find from far down the boulevard. Notice the flowers at the base, perhaps fresh, perhaps left this morning by someone you will never meet. Then turn toward the canal or the Old Town, and listen again. The trams, the footsteps, the trees. The same sounds as before... but now you know what stands above them.

Practical tips

Walk a full circle to see every carved group.

Keep a respectful distance from the standing guards.
