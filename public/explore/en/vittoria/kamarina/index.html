The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through dry grass, and somewhere below you the sea breaks on soft sand. The ground under your feet is pale and loose, full of small stones. Some of those stones are not natural at all. They are pieces of walls, roof tiles and pottery, scattered across a low headland above the Mediterranean. This quiet stretch of coast near the fishing town of Scoglitti was once a Greek city. Its name was Kamarina.

The Greeks who came to Sicily did not arrive all at once. They came in waves, city by city, and many new settlements were founded by older ones. Kamarina was a daughter of Syracuse, the powerful city on the island's east coast. Ancient writers place its founding in the early sixth century before Christ. The settlers chose this spot with care. A low hill gave them a view of the sea, and rivers on either side gave them water and a natural boundary.

The river to the north was known to the Greeks as the Hipparis. Today it is called the Ippari, and it still reaches the sea close to the site. Between the rivers and the coast lay wetlands. Those marshes gave the city its most famous story. Before you hear it, look around you at the flat land and the line of the shore. Try to picture standing water, reeds and mist here instead of fields.

According to an ancient tale, the people of Kamarina wanted to drain the marsh. It brought fever, they said, and bad air. They asked an oracle for advice. The answer was short and strange: do not move Kamarina. The citizens ignored it and drained the water anyway. Legend says that enemies then crossed the dry ground and took the city. Whether it happened exactly like that, no one can say. But the phrase survived for centuries. People used it as a proverb, a warning against disturbing something best left alone.

The real history of the city is dramatic enough without legend. Kamarina was caught between larger powers, and it suffered for it. It rebelled against its mother city, Syracuse, and was destroyed. Later it was refounded under the tyrant of Gela, another Greek city further west along this coast. Then its people were moved away again by a ruler of Syracuse. After that, settlers from Gela returned and rebuilt it once more. Few places in Sicily were emptied and filled again so many times.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. This small coastal city appears in some of the finest poetry of ancient Greece. A citizen of Kamarina named Psaumis won a chariot race at the Olympic Games in the fifth century before Christ. The poet Pindar wrote odes in his honour. In those verses, Pindar mentions the river and the marshes of Kamarina. So the landscape you are standing in was sung aloud in Greece, more than two thousand years ago.

At its height, the city was laid out in a careful grid. Straight streets crossed at right angles, dividing the hill into regular blocks of houses. Archaeologists have uncovered the foundations of homes, and parts of the public square, the agora. The agora was the heart of a Greek city. It was a place for trade, debate and everyday meetings. As you walk the paths, notice how the low walls line up. That order was planned, not accidental.

The most important religious building here was a temple to Athena, goddess of wisdom and protector of cities. Its remains are part of the museum itself. Much later, a farmhouse was built on the same spot, and its walls rose on top of the ancient temple. Today that farm building houses the archaeological museum. Look carefully inside and you can see where old Greek stone meets later masonry. Two very different worlds sit one on top of the other.

One remarkable discovery at the temple was a large group of small lead sheets. Each one carried writing scratched into the soft metal, mostly personal names. Scholars think they were connected to the civic life of the city, perhaps lists of citizens belonging to different groups. For a historian, names like these are precious. They are not kings or generals. They are ordinary people of Kamarina, recorded in their own place.

Beyond the city walls lay the cemeteries. The Greeks buried their dead outside the town, and the necropolises of Kamarina have produced thousands of graves. From them came painted vases, small figures of clay, and simple everyday objects placed beside the dead. Many of these finds are now in the museum's cases. When you look at a cup or an oil flask there, remember it came from a person's grave, chosen by someone who loved them.

The sea has also kept its own record. The waters off this coast are shallow and sandy, and storms caught many ships here. Divers have found ancient wrecks and their cargo on the seabed nearby. Anchors, jars and other objects from the deep are shown in the museum too. They remind us that Kamarina faced the sea. Its people traded with the wider Mediterranean, and sailors passed this headland for centuries.

The Greek city did not last forever. During the long wars between Rome and Carthage in the third century before Christ, Kamarina was captured by the Romans. After that, the city never returned to its former strength. Life here became smaller, scattered and rural. Sand and soil slowly covered the streets. For a very long time, this was simply farmland by the sea.

Before you leave, stand for a moment at the edge of the headland. Look out at the water, then back at the low lines of stone in the grass. The marsh is gone now, and so are the temples and the crowded streets. But the rivers still run to the sea, and the wind still crosses this hill. As you walk back to the road, watch the ground near your feet. A curved fragment of red clay may be lying there... a small piece of Kamarina, still in its place.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early or late to avoid the strongest midday sun.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose sand and uneven paths.
Bring water; shade on the open headland is limited.
Start in the museum before walking the excavated city.
Combine your visit with a seafood lunch in nearby Scoglitti.
