The guide

The story

The wind gets to you first. There is nothing to stop it here — a flat plain in every direction, low mounds, dry grass, and dust that finds your teeth within a few minutes. Larks go up singing from the stubble. Under your feet the ground is pale, cracked clay, and it crunches. Two large shelters stand over the excavation areas, and inside them the light turns soft and grey. The temperature drops. The wind noise becomes a hum against the roof.

Look at the mound itself before you look at anything inside it. It rises perhaps twenty metres above the plain, a long low swell of earth. Nobody piled it up deliberately. It is made almost entirely of collapsed houses. People built with mud brick, lived in a house for a generation or two, filled it in, and built a new house directly on top. Do that for well over a thousand years and the town slowly lifts itself above the fields.

This settlement was occupied around nine thousand years ago, in the Neolithic — the late Stone Age, when farming was new. That timing matters. These people were among the earliest anywhere to live in a large permanent community, growing crops and keeping animals rather than following them. Estimates of the population vary, and honest archaeologists disagree, but thousands of people probably lived here at once. That was an extraordinary number of humans in one place at that date.

Now the strangest thing about the place. There are no streets. Look at the excavated area and you will see rooms packed against each other, sharing walls, with no gaps between them and no doors at ground level. To enter a house, you climbed onto the roof and went down a ladder through a hole. The roofs formed the surface people walked on. The town was effectively one continuous platform, with the doorways underneath your feet.

Think about what daily life looked like as a result. Work happened up on the roofs — grinding grain, making tools, cooking in the open, drying food. Movement across the settlement meant walking over other people's homes. The ladder hole served as both the door and the chimney, so a house had a hearth directly below it, and the walls above the fire were sooty. Excavators found the plaster near hearths blackened, then replastered, over and over.

The dead stayed in the house. Under the raised platforms where people slept, archaeologists found burials — adults and children, placed in the floor, sometimes many individuals under a single room across generations. Bodies were tightly folded. Some were buried with beads, tools or pigment. The living and the dead shared a floor. It seems unsettling now. To these people it was normal, and it may be one reason houses were rebuilt on exactly the same footprint each time.

Then there are the walls. Rooms were plastered smooth with white lime plaster and refreshed regularly. Some plaster surfaces carry painted designs in red, black and orange — geometric patterns, handprints, rows of human figures around large animals. Because plaster was reapplied so often, paintings ended up sealed in layers, one over another. Excavators work through them like pages. A room that looks blank can hold a painted surface a few millimetres deeper in.

Animals were built into the architecture in a literal way. Cattle horns were set into benches and plastered into walls, sometimes in rows, sometimes projecting into the room at head height. Modelled clay heads and animal shapes were attached to walls as well. These were not trophies hung on a hook. They were part of the structure, plastered in place. Whatever they meant, the people here wanted large horned animals physically present inside the rooms where they slept.

One painted panel became famous, and it is worth knowing the argument about it. It shows a pattern of rectangles below a shape with dots and lines above. Many people read it as a plan of the town with an erupting volcano behind — the twin-peaked mountain visible from here on a clear day. If they are right, it is one of the oldest known maps, and one of the oldest pictures of an eruption. Other specialists read it as a leopard skin and a decorative pattern. The debate is unresolved.

Something is missing here, and its absence is the real surprise. There is no palace. There is no obvious temple, no central plaza, no monumental building, no clear ruler's residence. Houses are broadly similar in size and equipment. Some had more burials or more decoration, but nothing suggests one family that controlled the rest. A settlement of thousands appears to have worked without visible kings, priests, or an administration. That challenges most assumptions about how early towns must have run.

The site was recognised late. A British archaeologist identified the mound in 1958 and excavated it in the early 1960s, and the finds changed how prehistory was written. Work stopped for decades. A new international project began digging again in 1993 with far more careful methods — sieving soil, analysing residues, sampling floors square by square. The site was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2012. A reconstructed house near the entrance shows what a single room looked like.

Before you leave, go outside the shelter and stand on top of the mound facing the plain. You are standing on nine thousand years of collapsed homes. Somewhere beneath your shoes, someone slept above their grandmother's grave, climbed a ladder each morning, and stepped out onto a roof to start work. The wheat fields around you now grow from the same soil they farmed. Feel the wind, look at the flat horizon, then take the path back down.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a hat and water; the plain offers no shade
Start at the reconstructed house to understand the layout
Visit the small site museum before entering the shelters
Come in spring or autumn to avoid extreme plateau heat
