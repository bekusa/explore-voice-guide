The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, water runs over stone. Two rivers meet at the foot of this rock, and their sound rises with the wind. Above you, three towers stand against the sky, grey and pale against the green slopes of the Pyrenean foothills. The limestone under your feet is smooth, polished by centuries of boots and hooves. This is the Château de Foix, and for most of the Middle Ages, it was the beating heart of a small but stubborn mountain state.

Look at where the castle sits. It stands on a steep outcrop of rock, right where the Arget river flows into the Ariège. Nature built most of the defence here. Cliffs drop away on several sides, and the rivers add a natural moat. Builders only had to finish what the landscape had started. From the walls, guards could watch the valley road in both directions — the road north toward Toulouse, and the road south toward the high mountains and Spain.

This was the seat of the Counts of Foix. They were a proud line of rulers who held this corner of southern France for centuries. They were not kings, but they acted with great independence. They made alliances, broke alliances, and fought their larger neighbours when it suited them. The castle was their symbol. When people in the region heard the word Foix, they thought of this rock and these towers.

Now, a story that shaped this whole region. In the early thirteenth century, a religious movement called Catharism spread across southern France. The Cathars were Christians who rejected the authority of the Catholic Church. They believed the material world was flawed, and they followed spiritual leaders called the perfect. The Church called them heretics. The Pope launched a crusade against them, known today as the Albigensian Crusade — a war of northern French knights against the south.

The Counts of Foix stood close to the Cathars. Count Raymond-Roger resisted the crusaders. His sister, Esclarmonde de Foix, became one of the most respected Cathar women of her time. She received the rite that made her one of the perfect, and she lived a life of preaching and charity. Her name still carries weight here. In Foix and across the Ariège, you will see it on streets, on schools, and in local legend.

Here is the first surprise. Simon de Montfort, the hard and feared leader of the crusade, came to Foix. He burned parts of the town below. But he could not take this castle by force. The rock was simply too steep, and the walls too strong. The castle earned a reputation that locals still repeat with pride — that no army ever stormed it. Later in the thirteenth century, a count did yield to the French king, but through surrender and negotiation, not a breach of the walls.

Let your eyes move across the three towers. They were not built at the same time. The two square towers are older. Their straight lines and solid mass belong to the classic medieval tradition of the region. The round tower came later, in the fifteenth century. Round towers handled attack better, because cannonballs and stones glanced off curved surfaces. So when you look at these three shapes together, you are really looking at different centuries standing side by side.

In the fourteenth century, Foix had its most famous count. He was Gaston the Third, better known by his nickname, Gaston Fébus. The name comes from Phoebus, the Greek sun god, and it suited his golden hair and his bright ambition. He ruled both Foix and Béarn, a territory further west. He was a skilled diplomat and a hard ruler. He also wrote a book about hunting, which became one of the most admired hunting manuals of the Middle Ages.

The second surprise involves a king. Over time, the lands of Foix passed through marriage and inheritance into the family of the kings of Navarre. One heir of that line was Henri, who became King Henri the Fourth of France at the end of the sixteenth century. With him, the county of Foix joined the French crown. So this small mountain power, which once resisted crusaders from the north, finally became part of France through a royal inheritance.

Once the counts were gone, the castle needed a new purpose. For a long period, it served as a prison. The thick walls that once kept enemies out now kept prisoners in. Imagine the cold in winter, the damp stone, the small windows. Graffiti carved by inmates can still be found in parts of the site, a quiet human trace left behind. The prison closed in the nineteenth century, and the castle slowly turned toward history and memory instead.

Today, the castle is a museum and a place for visitors to explore the Middle Ages. Inside, displays tell the story of the counts, of daily life in a medieval fortress, and of the Cathar period. There are also activities that show weapons, armour, and the skills of medieval life. Climb as high as you are allowed. The stairs are narrow and worn, so take them slowly. The reward at the top is the view.

From up there, the town of Foix spreads out below. You can see its old rooftops of red tile and slate, its narrow streets, and the bend of the river. Beyond the town, the hills rise in layers toward the Pyrenees. On a clear day, the mountain peaks stand sharp on the southern horizon. Many visitors say this is the moment when the castle makes sense. You understand why rulers chose this rock. They could see everything.

When you walk back down, take some time in the old town. The streets around the castle still hold timber-framed houses with overhanging upper floors. Small squares open up between them, often with a café table or a fountain. The large abbey church of Saint-Volusien stands near the river. The whole town feels connected to the rock above it. Wherever you turn, you can look up and find the towers watching over the rooftops.

Before you leave, stop for a moment and listen again. The rivers are still running at the foot of the rock, just as they did when Esclarmonde walked these paths, and when Gaston Fébus rode out to hunt. The stone around you is the same stone they touched. Look up once more at the three towers... two square, one round. Carry that shape with you as you walk on into the town.
