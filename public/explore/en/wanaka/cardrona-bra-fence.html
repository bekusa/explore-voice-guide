The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across the tussock grass. Somewhere behind you a car passes on the valley road, and then it is quiet again. In front of you is an ordinary rural fence, wire strung between wooden posts. It is hung with hundreds and hundreds of bras. Lace, cotton, sports bras, padded ones, some faded by the high country sun. They sway and flap together in the breeze, and the sound is a soft, steady rustle.

This is the Cardrona Bra Fence. It sits in the Cardrona Valley, on the road that climbs between Wānaka and the Crown Range toward Queenstown. The land around it is open and tawny, with dry hillsides and wide skies. There is no visitor centre here and no grand gate. The attraction is the fence itself, and the slightly absurd fact that you have come to look at it. Most people who stop do the same thing first. They laugh.

The story begins quietly, around the turn of 1999. The most common account says that over the Christmas and New Year holidays, a small number of bras appeared on the fence. Nobody announced who put them there. Locals have offered several versions of the tale. One popular story involves a group of women celebrating on a night out. Like much folklore, the exact details shift depending on who tells it.

What happened next is clearer. Word spread. Other people began to add their own bras. A handful became dozens, and dozens became hundreds. Travellers driving the valley road would pull over, unclip something from their luggage, and add it to the wire. The fence grew without a plan, a committee, or a single owner. It became a kind of public conversation, carried on one garment at a time.

Not everyone was amused. Over the years, the bras were reportedly removed on more than one occasion. Some neighbours saw the fence as untidy, or as a distraction for drivers on a narrow rural road. Each time the fence was cleared, it seems, new bras arrived. That cycle tells you something about this place. It was never really about underwear. It became about who gets to decide what a landscape is allowed to hold.

Here is the detail many visitors miss. The fence now has a serious purpose. Over time it became linked to fundraising for breast cancer awareness in New Zealand. Look for a donation box near the fence, where people leave money for the Breast Cancer Foundation. So the joke has a second layer. A roadside prank has turned into a small, steady source of support for a cause that touches many families.

That shift explains why the fence feels different up close. From the car it looks like pure comedy. On foot, you notice the small personal touches. Some bras carry handwritten names. Some have messages, dates, or tags tied to them. A few appear to be left in memory of someone. Read slowly, and the fence starts to feel like a public notice board for private stories. The laughter is still there, but it sits next to something gentler.

The setting matters too. The Cardrona Valley has a long human history before any bras arrived. In the nineteenth century, it was gold country. Miners came here during the Otago gold rushes of the 1860s, searching the river gravels for gold. Small settlements rose and faded. The old Cardrona Hotel, a short drive along this road, dates from that gold-rush era. It still welcomes travellers, and its weathered front is one of the most photographed buildings in the valley.

So this road has always carried people passing through. Miners, drovers, farmers, skiers heading to the slopes in winter, and now travellers with cameras. Each group left its mark. The miners left tailings and old water races in the hills. The farmers left fences like this one. And the travellers, in their own strange way, decorated those fences. It is all part of the same slow layering of human life on a high and open landscape.

Take a moment to look at the land beyond the wire. The hills here are covered in tussock, a native grass that grows in thick, golden clumps. In summer the colour turns pale and dry. In winter, snow can dust the slopes above you, and the air bites at your fingers. The light changes quickly in this valley. Clouds move fast over the ridges, and their shadows slide across the hills like dark water.

If you would like to add your own contribution, you will not be the first. Many people do. Bring something you are happy to leave behind, and fasten it securely, because the wind here is strong. Be careful near the road. Traffic moves quickly on this route, and the verge can be narrow. Park fully off the road, watch for passing cars, and keep children close while you take photographs.

For a good picture, step back rather than forward. A close-up shows colour and texture, but a wider frame shows the real surprise. A long line of bras runs along a plain farm fence, with empty hills and sky behind it. That contrast is the whole story in one image. Soft morning or late afternoon light warms the tussock and makes the fabric glow. In the middle of the day, the high country sun can flatten everything.

It is worth asking why a place like this lasts. Perhaps it is because it asks nothing of you. There is no ticket, no queue, no lecture. It is simply a fence that ordinary people decided to change. It mixes humour, protest, memory, and generosity, and nobody planned that mix. In a world of carefully designed attractions, there is something honest about a landmark that was built by accident.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and listen again. The wind is still moving through the grass. The fabric is still rustling on the wire. Look along the fence and find one bra that catches your eye, maybe a bright colour, maybe a faded name. Somebody stood exactly where you are standing and left it there. As you drive on through the valley, watch the other fences you pass. Most of them are just wire. This one carries a crowd.
