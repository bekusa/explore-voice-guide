The guide

The story

Listen first to the gravel. It crunches softly under your feet as you walk toward a pale, low house with tall windows and grey slate roofs. The trees around you are old. In late spring, the air near the gardens can carry the sweet smell of roses. This is not a grand royal palace. It feels more like a country home, and that is exactly what it was meant to be.

You are standing at the Château de Malmaison, on the western edge of Paris. For a few remarkable years, this modest house sat at the centre of French power. It belonged to Joséphine, the woman who became Empress of the French. It was also a home for her husband, Napoleon Bonaparte. Here, the private life of the couple and the public life of a nation ran side by side.

First, a word about the name. Malmaison sounds a little unsettling in French — it can be read as something like "bad house". Locals have long told a story that the name goes back to Viking raiders who once camped here, centuries before the château existed. Treat that as legend rather than history. Either way, the name stuck, and Joséphine clearly did not mind it at all.

The house itself is older than its famous owners. A manor stood here by the seventeenth century, and it passed through several hands. Then, in 1799, Joséphine bought the estate. Napoleon was away on his military campaign in Egypt at the time. The purchase was expensive, and the house needed a great deal of work. When Napoleon came home, he was not pleased about the debts. But he soon came to love this place.

Only months later, Napoleon seized power and became First Consul of France. During those years, Malmaison was far more than a weekend retreat. Ministers came out from Paris. Meetings took place in these rooms. Decisions that shaped modern France were discussed a short carriage ride from the capital. Picture messengers on horseback arriving on this same approach, dust on their boots, papers in their bags.

To make the house fit for this new role, the couple turned to two architects, Charles Percier and Pierre Fontaine. They became leading figures of the Empire style — a look inspired by ancient Rome, Greece and Egypt, full of columns, laurel wreaths and military symbols. Malmaison is one of the best places anywhere to see that style in its early, fresh form, before it spread across Europe.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look for the Council Room. Its walls and ceiling are covered in striped fabric, gathered and draped so the whole room looks like a soldier's campaign tent. Painted spears and trophies of arms complete the effect. Napoleon, the general, could sit in his country house and still feel he was in the field. It is theatre, but very clever theatre.

Nearby is the library, one of the most admired rooms in the house. Dark wood, slender columns and painted ceilings create a calm, scholarly space. Napoleon worked here, and you can imagine him pacing between the shelves. The room is surprisingly intimate. That is the quiet surprise of Malmaison as a whole — the rooms are human in scale. You can almost hear the conversations that once filled them.

Now turn your thoughts to Joséphine herself. She was born on the island of Martinique, in the Caribbean, and she grew up surrounded by tropical plants. At Malmaison, she poured her energy into the gardens. She collected rare plants from around the world and built heated glasshouses to protect them. Her rose collection became famous. The artist Pierre-Joseph Redouté painted many of her flowers, and his delicate rose pictures are still reproduced today.

The gardens held more than plants. Joséphine kept unusual animals here, including black swans from Australia. Accounts also mention kangaroos and emus on the estate. For Parisians of the time, these creatures were astonishing. Today the grounds are much smaller than in Joséphine's day. Much of the original estate was sold off and built over. But the lawns and trees still give a sense of the open, romantic landscape she loved.

The story of this house also holds sadness. Joséphine and Napoleon did not have children together, and he wanted an heir. In 1809, he divorced her. Joséphine kept Malmaison and lived here for the rest of her life. She died here in 1814, the same year Napoleon was first sent into exile. She is buried in the church in the centre of Rueil-Malmaison, not far from where you stand.

There is one final chapter. In 1815, after his defeat at Waterloo, Napoleon came back to Malmaison. Joséphine was already gone. He stayed only a few days, in the house they had shared, before he left for his final exile. It is easy to imagine him walking these paths one last time. Later, after many changes of ownership, the château became a national museum in the early twentieth century.

As you move through the rooms, notice how much is original to the period — furniture, paintings, porcelain, even items of clothing. It is a collection built around two people, not around an idea of empire. Then step back outside. Look at the plain, honest front of the house. Listen again to the gravel. Somewhere in the garden, roses still grow, and the house still feels lived in — a home first, and a palace second.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in late spring to see the roses in bloom.
Walk the gardens before entering the house.
Visit Joséphine's tomb at the church in central Rueil.
Allow extra time for the library and Council Room.
Wear comfortable shoes for the gravel garden paths.
