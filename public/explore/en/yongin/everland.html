The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere to your left, a carousel organ is playing. Somewhere behind you, a crowd screams as one, then falls silent as a coaster drops out of sight. Underfoot, the paths are smooth and swept, and the air carries popcorn, cut grass, and, depending on the season, a faint sweetness of flowers. Around the whole scene rise the forested hills of Gyeonggi Province. Welcome to Everland, in the city of Yongin, south of Seoul.

The first thing worth knowing is that this place did not begin as a theme park. It opened in 1976 as Yongin Farmland, a nature park on hillside land in the Pogok area of Yongin. The emphasis then was on gardens, animals, and open space. Over the following decades, rides and themed zones grew around that green core. In 1996 the park took the name Everland, and the modern resort took shape.

That origin story explains a lot about how the park feels today. Many large theme parks sit on flat ground. Everland climbs. Paths curve along slopes, terraces step down the hills, and the trees around the edges are real forest, not decoration. As you walk, you will notice how often the view opens suddenly. You come over a rise, and a whole zone spreads out below you. The hills shape the experience as much as any ride does.

The park is run by a company within the Samsung group, and that connection is part of the local story. For many South Koreans, a trip to Everland is a shared memory. It may be a school excursion, a first date, or a family day out in spring. You will likely see all three groups around you right now. Students in matching jackets, couples in coordinated outfits, grandparents pushing strollers. The park is a kind of national meeting place.

Everland is divided into themed zones, and each one has its own architecture and mood. Near the entrance is Global Fair, with buildings borrowing from many countries. Beyond that you will find American Adventure, Magic Land, European Adventure, and Zootopia, the animal zone. The names tell you the idea. Each area borrows a style from somewhere else, then gives it a Korean twist in the food stalls, the signage, and the music playing from hidden speakers.

Now, the ride that most thrill-seekers come for. T Express opened in 2008. It is a wooden roller coaster, and that alone surprises many visitors. Wooden coasters feel different from steel ones. They rattle, they sway, and they sound like a storm in a timber barn. The first drop on T Express is extremely steep, at around seventy-seven degrees, which places it among the steepest wooden coasters ever built. Watch the riders' faces at the top. Most of them stop talking.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. Because T Express is built into the hillside, the terrain adds to the drama. The track uses the natural slope, so the ground falls away beneath the cars in ways that a flat site could not offer. If you stand near the lower part of the structure, look up through the lattice of timber beams. It is a remarkable piece of carpentry and engineering, and it looks quite different from below than it does from the queue.

For many people, though, Everland means animals more than rides. Zootopia brings together several experiences. Safari World takes visitors by vehicle through enclosures with big cats and bears. Lost Valley follows a similar idea with grazing animals in a landscape of rocks and water. The vehicles move slowly, and the drivers often narrate in Korean with real enthusiasm. Even if you do not understand every word, the tone carries clearly. Laughter in the vehicle usually means an animal is doing something unexpected.

Then there are the pandas. In 2016, two giant pandas named Ai Bao and Le Bao arrived from China. In 2020 they had a cub named Fu Bao, the first giant panda born in South Korea. She became a national celebrity. Crowds queued for hours, and her keepers became famous too. In 2024 Fu Bao moved to China, as agreements for these animals require. Many fans travelled to say goodbye. Meanwhile, twin sisters had been born at Everland in 2023, named Rui Bao and Hui Bao.

The second surprise is how deep the attachment to these animals runs. Panda World is not just an enclosure. It is a place where visitors stand quietly, phones raised, watching a bear eat bamboo for ten minutes at a time. Look at the merchandise shops nearby and you will see panda faces on almost everything. For a country with no native pandas, this family of bears has become part of popular culture, followed online in daily videos and news stories.

The gardens are a reminder of the old farmland roots. Everland is known for seasonal flower festivals, especially tulips in spring and roses in early summer. The rose garden here has been tended for decades, and it remains one of the park's quieter corners. Even outside the festival seasons, you will see careful planting along the paths. Notice how the beds change colour from zone to zone, and how the staff tend them during opening hours, in full view of visitors.

Everland also sits within a larger resort. Next door is Caribbean Bay, a water park that is busiest in the hot months. A short distance away, in the same valley, is the Hoam Museum of Art, linked to the Samsung family's art collection, with traditional Korean gardens around it. Few theme park visitors know it is there. If you want a contrast to the rides and noise, that quiet museum, with its pond and pine trees, is a very different afternoon.

Before you move on, pause wherever you are standing. Look past the rides and the painted facades to the ridgeline beyond. Those hills were here long before the first visitors arrived in 1976. Listen again to the mix of sounds. The organ music, the screams from the coaster, a child laughing at a passing parade. As you walk to your next stop, notice the trees that frame every view. This park began as a garden, and in many quiet corners, it still is one.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride T Express early before the queue grows long.
Check panda viewing arrangements soon after you arrive.
Wear comfortable shoes for the park's hilly paths.
Visit in spring for the tulip festival gardens.
Pair your visit with the nearby Hoam Museum of Art.
