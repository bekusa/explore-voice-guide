The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, the silence is not empty. Wind runs low across the water. Ice knocks against ice somewhere you cannot see. A gull calls once, and the sound travels much further than seems right. The air has almost no smell at all, only cold and salt. This is the Russian Arctic National Park, one of the most northerly protected areas on Earth. It sits far above the mainland of Arkhangelsk Oblast, but it is still governed from that old port city on the White Sea.

What you are standing in, or sailing through, is not one place but two. The park was created in 2009 on the northern part of Novaya Zemlya, the long, curved archipelago that separates the Barents Sea from the Kara Sea. In 2016, the protected area grew to take in Franz Josef Land, a scatter of more than a hundred islands even further north. Together they form a wilderness of glaciers, bare rock, and frozen sea. Very few people ever see it with their own eyes.

Look at the land itself. Much of it is ice. On Novaya Zemlya, a great ice cap covers the spine of the northern island, and its glaciers flow down to the sea. Where they meet the water, they end in tall blue walls. Pieces break away with a sound like distant thunder. On Franz Josef Land, nearly every island wears a dome of ice. Between the glaciers, the ground is dark basalt, a volcanic rock, often stepped into flat terraces like a giant staircase.

Now, a surprise most visitors miss. In summer, this frozen place is not grey. Look closely at the ground near the shore. Small cushions of moss grow there, along with lichens in orange, yellow and pale green. In sheltered spots you may find tiny flowers, among them the Arctic poppy, with its pale yellow petals. Everything stays close to the ground, out of the wind. A plant here may grow for many years and still fit inside your palm.

The true owners of this park are the animals. The polar bear is the one everyone hopes to see. Bears hunt seals from the sea ice and walk long distances across it. Walrus haul out in heavy, crowded groups on gravel beaches, grunting and shifting their tusked heads. In the cold waters, there are beluga whales, and with luck, bowhead whales and narwhals, the whale with a single long tusk. On the cliffs above, seabirds nest in their thousands.

One of those birds deserves a moment. The ivory gull is pure white, with dark eyes and black legs. It lives almost entirely in the high Arctic, close to the ice. It is rare and difficult to see anywhere else in the world. Franz Josef Land is one of its important breeding areas. If a small white gull circles your ship and seems to follow it with interest, look again. You may be looking at one of the park's most treasured residents.

Humans came here late, and they came in hardship. In the winter of 1596, the Dutch navigator Willem Barentsz and his crew were trapped by ice off the northeast coast of Novaya Zemlya. They built a wooden shelter from their ship's timbers and survived the long polar night inside it. Barentsz himself died the following summer, during the escape by open boat. The remains of that winter house were found centuries later. The sea that washes these shores now carries his name.

Franz Josef Land has its own story of accident. In 1873, an Austro-Hungarian expedition was drifting helplessly, its ship locked in the pack ice. Then, through the mist, the crew saw land that no one had ever charted. They named it after their emperor, Franz Joseph. That is why a group of Russian Arctic islands carries an Austrian name. Many of the capes and channels here still carry the names of European explorers and patrons from that era.

The most moving meeting in Arctic history happened here too. The Norwegian explorer Fridtjof Nansen had left his ship and tried to reach the North Pole on skis with one companion, Hjalmar Johansen. They failed, turned south, and spent a winter in a stone hut on Franz Josef Land, living on walrus and bear. In 1896, near Cape Flora, Nansen heard a dog bark. Moments later, he met the English explorer Frederick Jackson. It was pure chance, and it saved them both.

Later history is more complex. In the Soviet period, polar stations were built on these islands to record weather and ice. One famous station stood in Tikhaya Bay, on Hooker Island. Its old wooden buildings still stand, and nearby rises a dark cliff covered in nesting birds. Novaya Zemlya also carries a heavier memory. During the Cold War, the Soviet Union used the archipelago for nuclear weapons tests. Today the park protects the northern wilderness, but that past is part of the landscape too.

A second surprise waits on Champ Island in Franz Josef Land. On some of its slopes lie stone spheres, almost perfectly round. Some are as small as a marble. Others are taller than a person. They are not carved by humans. Geologists explain them as natural formations that grew inside softer rock over a very long time, and were later exposed by erosion. Standing among them, the scene looks almost unreal, like something left behind on purpose.

If you are here, you most likely came by expedition ship, with small rubber boats for landings. That is how almost everyone visits. The season is short, only the few summer weeks when the sea ice opens. Rangers and guides go ashore first, often armed against bears, and they decide where it is safe to land. Weather changes everything. A planned landing can disappear in an hour of fog. Here, patience is not a virtue. It is simply the way things work.

Before you leave, stop for a moment. Look at the edge of the ice, where white meets dark water. Notice how the light never quite fades in summer. The sun circles low and does not set. Listen again for that silence, the wind, the birds, the slow movement of ice. Few people will ever stand where you are standing now. Take one last long look... and carry it home with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring binoculars to spot walrus and bears from the ship deck.
Dress in waterproof layers for wet rubber-boat landings.
Always stay close to armed rangers during every shore landing.
Expect weather to cancel landings and keep plans flexible.
Carry spare camera batteries, since cold drains them quickly.
