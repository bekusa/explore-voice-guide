The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, the forest reaches you as sound. Insects hum in a steady layer. A branch cracks somewhere you cannot see. Then a bird calls, sharp and clear, and the green walls around you swallow the echo. The air is warm and heavy, and it smells of wet leaves, mud, and something sweet from fruit fallen to the forest floor. This is Odzala-Kokoua National Park, in the northwest of the Republic of the Congo.

This is one of the oldest protected areas in Africa. Odzala was first set aside in the 1930s, during the colonial era, long before most of the continent's well-known parks existed. For decades it was a remote name on a map. Few roads reached it, and few outsiders came. Later the protected area grew far beyond its original borders, and it now covers more than thirteen thousand square kilometres of forest, river and savanna.

That size matters. Odzala-Kokoua sits inside the Congo Basin, the second-largest tropical rainforest on Earth after the Amazon. The forest here is not one single green mass. It is a patchwork. Dense rainforest gives way to open grassland, then to river forest, then to swamp. Scientists call this a forest-savanna mosaic. For the animals, it means many different homes in one landscape, and for you it means the view changes again and again as you travel.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Some of the most important places in the park are not in the deep forest at all. They are clearings called bais. A bai is an open, marshy gap in the trees, often fed by springs. The soil and water in these clearings hold minerals that animals cannot easily find elsewhere. So they come out of the forest to drink, to dig, and to eat the mineral-rich mud.

If you sit quietly at the edge of a bai, the forest slowly empties into it. Forest elephants are the most famous visitors. They are smaller than the elephants of the East African savanna, with rounder ears and straighter tusks that point downwards. They move with surprising quiet for such large animals. You may also see forest buffalo, which are reddish-brown and much smaller than their savanna cousins, standing knee-deep in the water.

But for many people, the reason to come here has a different shape. Odzala-Kokoua is home to western lowland gorillas. These are the gorillas of the central African rainforest, quite different from the mountain gorillas of Rwanda and Uganda. They live in family groups, led by a large adult male called a silverback. The name comes from the band of grey-white hair across his back, which appears as he matures.

The story of the gorillas here carries a hard chapter. In the early 2000s, outbreaks of the Ebola virus moved through parts of this region. The disease killed large numbers of gorillas and chimpanzees, and some groups in and around the park were lost almost entirely. It was one of the worst wildlife disasters recorded in central Africa. The gorillas you may see today belong to a population that survived, and that recovery is still watched closely.

That history shapes how gorilla visits work. Groups of visitors are kept small. Time with the gorillas is limited, and people keep a set distance. Guides may ask you to wear a face mask. This is not only a rule for show. Gorillas share much of our biology, and they can catch human illnesses. A cold that means little to you could be dangerous to a young gorilla. Standing back is a way of protecting them.

The forest holds much more than its large animals. Chimpanzees live here too, though they are harder to find. Monkeys move through the high branches, and you often hear them before you see them. Red river hogs, with bright orange-red coats, root through the leaves. On the forest floor, look for the tracks of small antelope called duikers. Overhead, hundreds of bird species fill the canopy, from parrots to the heavy, honking hornbills.

Water is the other road through this park. Rivers wind through the forest, and in many places a boat is the easiest way to move. On the water, the light changes completely. The walls of trees open up, and the sky appears above the river. Kingfishers flash past at the banks. If you go out in the late afternoon, the forest edges turn gold, and the sounds of evening begin to rise around you.

The people who live around the park are part of its story too. For many generations, communities in this region have depended on the forest for food, building materials and medicine. Today the park is managed by the Congolese government together with African Parks, a conservation organisation. Many of the rangers, guides and camp staff come from nearby villages. When a guide shows you a track or names a plant, that knowledge often comes from home.

Coming here takes effort. The park is far from the capital, Brazzaville, and the journey often includes a long drive or a small charter flight. Inside the park, camps are few and spread out. This distance is part of what you feel when you arrive. There are no crowds, no traffic, and very little noise from the human world. The rhythm of the day follows the light, the heat and the movement of the animals.

So take a moment, wherever you are standing now. Notice the layers of green, the damp ground under your feet, the constant hum in the air. Somewhere close, an animal is probably aware of you before you are aware of it. As you walk on, listen for the change when the trees open into a clearing. That quiet, bright edge... is where the forest shows itself.

Before you go

Practical tips

Sit quietly at a bai edge and wait.
Wear muted greens or browns in the forest.
Expect to wear a face mask near gorillas.
Pack light rain gear for sudden showers.
Take a late-afternoon boat trip for softer light.
