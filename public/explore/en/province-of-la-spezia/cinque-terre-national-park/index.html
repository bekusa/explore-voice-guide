The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Somewhere below, the sea pulls at the rocks with a slow hiss. Closer by, a lizard rustles through dry leaves on a stone wall. The air smells of salt, warm rosemary, and the faint sweetness of vines. Look up the slope and you see narrow green steps climbing toward the sky, held in place by stone. This is the Cinque Terre, and almost everything you can see here was shaped by human hands.

The name means "Five Lands." It refers to five villages strung along this rugged stretch of the Ligurian coast, in the province of La Spezia. From north to south they are Monterosso al Mare, Vernazza, Corniglia, Manarola, and Riomaggiore. Each one sits in a fold of the cliffs, where a small stream once cut down to the water. For centuries, the sea and steep mule paths were the main ways in and out. That isolation shaped everything here, from the dialect to the food.

The national park was created in 1999. A few years earlier, in 1997, UNESCO listed this coastline as a World Heritage Site, together with nearby Porto Venere and three small offshore islands. Here is the first surprise. UNESCO did not list it mainly for its beauty. It listed it as a cultural landscape. That means a place where people and nature built something together over a very long time. What was protected is the work of generations of farmers.

Look again at those terraces. Each step is held up by a dry-stone wall, built without mortar. Stones are fitted so carefully that their own weight locks them together. Water drains through the gaps instead of pushing the walls over. Estimates of their total length run to thousands of kilometres across these hills. Nobody planned this as one project. Families added wall after wall, carrying stone on their backs, turning a cliff into farmland.

What did they grow? Mostly grapes, along with olives and lemons. The vines here produce crisp white wines from local varieties. There is also a rare dessert wine called Sciacchetrà. Its grapes are left to dry after the harvest, so their sugars concentrate before pressing. A small glass is golden, thick, and sweet, with notes of honey and apricot. If you see it on a menu, it is worth tasting. You are drinking the result of very steep, very patient work.

Here is the second surprise. Look for thin metal rails running straight up the slopes. They look like a toy railway. These are small monorails used by growers to carry grapes, tools, and supplies up and down the terraces. Before them, everything moved by hand, in baskets carried along narrow paths. The monorails are a quiet sign that this is still a working landscape, not a museum. Someone still prunes these vines every winter.

The terraces also carry a weakness. When farmers leave, nobody repairs the walls. Rain gets in, stones shift, and slopes can slide. During the twentieth century, many families moved away for easier work, and some terraces were abandoned. In October 2011, a violent storm sent mud and debris pouring into Vernazza and Monterosso. The villages were badly damaged. Volunteers and residents dug them out, and the disaster made clear how much these walls protect the people below.

Each village has its own character. Monterosso is the largest, with the longest sandy beach. At one end, a giant figure carved into the rock watches over the shore. Locals simply call him the Giant. Vernazza curves around a small natural harbour, overlooked by an old watchtower and a church right at the water's edge. Many people find its little square, full of boats and café tables, the heart of the whole coast.

Corniglia is the odd one out. It is the only village that does not touch the sea directly. Instead, it sits high on a rocky ridge, surrounded by vineyards. If you arrive by train, a long brick stairway of several hundred steps zigzags up from the station. There is also a shuttle bus. From the top, the view runs along the coast in both directions, and the village feels quieter than its neighbours.

Manarola tumbles down a narrow ravine toward a tiny harbour. Its houses are painted in warm shades of pink, ochre, and terracotta. Locals say the bright colours once helped fishermen pick out their homes from the water. That is a popular tale, not documented history, but it is easy to believe from a boat. Around the winter holidays, a local man's large illuminated nativity scene lights up the hillside above the village.

Riomaggiore, the southernmost village, climbs steeply on both sides of a stream that now runs under its main street. Tall, narrow houses stack against each other like books on a shelf. Between Riomaggiore and Manarola runs a famous seaside path called the Via dell'Amore, the Way of Love. A rockslide closed it for many years. It has reopened in recent years after major engineering work, and it is now a popular short walk.

The railway changed life here. In the nineteenth century, a line was cut along this coast, largely through tunnels. Suddenly the villages were connected to each other and to the wider world. Today the train is still the easiest way to move between them. Paths link them too. The coastal route known as the Blue Trail is the best known, while higher trails climb past sanctuaries and through chestnut woods. Many of these paths are very old.

Now, wherever you are standing, take one more look around. Notice the stones under your feet, worn smooth by centuries of footsteps. Notice the walls beside you, each stone placed by someone whose name nobody remembers. Listen to the sea and the trains moving through the tunnels. As you walk on, remember that this hillside only stays green because people keep working on it. Every vineyard you pass is still being cared for.

Before you go

Practical tips

Use the regional train to hop between the five villages.
Wear sturdy shoes with grip for the coastal trails.
Check trail closures before hiking after heavy rain.
Buy the park card before walking the main coastal trail.
Visit early morning to enjoy the villages before crowds arrive.
