Istanbul in 2 Days: A Self-Guided Route Through the Old City

Last verified October 20269 min readLokali Journal
Short answer

Two days covers Istanbul's historic core on foot. Day one: Hagia Sophia, the Blue Mosque, the Basilica Cistern and Topkapi Palace, all within Sultanahmet. Day two: the Grand Bazaar, Spice Bazaar, Suleymaniye Mosque and a Bosphorus ferry. Dress modestly for mosques and check prayer times.

Istanbul is enormous, but the part that holds most of what you came to see is not. Sultanahmet - the old city on the European side - puts a Byzantine cathedral, an Ottoman imperial mosque, an underground Roman cistern and a sultan's palace within one square kilometre of each other. Two days is tight but it works, provided you spend day one in that square kilometre and day two moving outward.

One thing to understand before you arrive: several of the major monuments are working mosques, not museums. That affects when you can go in and what you wear, and it is the single most common way visitors waste an hour.

Mosque etiquette, briefly

  • Shoulders and knees covered for everyone. Women also cover their hair - scarves are usually available free at the entrance, but bringing your own is easier.
  • Shoes off before the carpet. There are shelves or a plastic bag at the door. Socks are strongly recommended.
  • Visitor access pauses around the five daily prayers, and Friday midday is the busiest prayer of the week. Check the day's times and plan around them rather than queueing through one.
  • Photograph the architecture, not the people praying. Keep the phone down and silent inside the prayer hall.

Day 1 - Sultanahmet

  1. 8:30
    Hagia Sophia
    Nearly 1,500 years old, built as a cathedral, later an imperial mosque, a museum through the twentieth century, and a working mosque again since 2020 with a separate visitor route. Go at opening. Allow an hour and a half.
  2. 10:30
    Blue Mosque
    Two minutes' walk across the square, and still an active neighbourhood mosque with no entry charge. The name comes from the Iznik tilework inside. Allow forty-five minutes.
  3. 11:30
    Sultanahmet Square
    You are standing on the Hippodrome, the Byzantine chariot-racing track. The Egyptian obelisk and the Serpent Column are the original track decorations, still in place.
  4. 12:30
    Basilica Cistern
    A sixth-century underground water reservoir, 336 columns, cool and dim. The right place to be in the middle of a hot day, and the best single hour in Sultanahmet for anyone travelling with teenagers.
  5. 14:30
    Topkapi Palace
    Four centuries of Ottoman sultans, arranged as a sequence of courtyards rather than a single building. The Harem is a separate ticket and worth it. Allow two and a half hours and do not try to see everything.
  6. 17:30
    Gulhane Park
    The palace's old outer garden, now a public park that drops down towards the water. Walk it at dusk - this is where the city goes to sit.

Day 2 - Bazaars, the Bosphorus and Suleymaniye

Day two moves. The logic is to do the covered markets early while they are functional rather than crushed, climb to Suleymaniye for the view, then get on the water in the afternoon when the light is best.

  1. 9:00
    Grand Bazaar
    Four thousand shops, sixty covered streets, in business since the fifteenth century. Closed on Sundays - check before you plan around it. Go early: by midday the main lanes are solid.
  2. 11:00
    Suleymaniye Mosque
    Sinan's masterpiece and, for many visitors, a better experience than the Blue Mosque - the same scale, a fraction of the crowd, and a terrace looking out over the Golden Horn.
  3. 12:30
    Spice Bazaar
    Downhill towards the water. Smaller and more chaotic than the Grand Bazaar, better for actually buying something edible. The streets around it are where locals shop.
  4. 14:30
    Bosphorus Strait
    Take a public ferry rather than a tour boat - same water, same views, a fraction of the price, and you can get off where you like. The strait is the formal boundary between Europe and Asia.
  5. 17:00
    Kadikoy
    Cross to the Asian side for the evening. No monuments, which is the point: this is where Istanbul eats and drinks without reference to visitors. Ferries run back late.

If you have a third day

Spend it on the Byzantine city rather than more of the Ottoman one. The Chora Church holds the finest late-Byzantine mosaics and frescoes anywhere, and sits in a quiet residential district most visitors never reach. Combine it with a walk through Fener and Balat, the old Greek and Jewish quarters along the Golden Horn - steep painted streets, antique shops and the best coffee stops in the city. Or go out to the Princes' Islands, which are car-free and about ninety minutes out by ferry.

Practical notes

  • Get a transport card on arrival and load it up. It works on ferries, trams, metro and buses, and the ferries alone justify it.
  • The tram through Sultanahmet is the single most useful line for visitors and it is also where pickpockets work. Bag in front of you.
  • Istanbul is hilly in a way photographs hide. The walk from the Spice Bazaar up to Suleymaniye is short and steep.
  • Carry a scarf and wear socks you do not mind taking off six times.
  • Check mosque prayer times each morning. They move through the year with sunrise and sunset.

Frequently asked

Is two days enough for Istanbul?

Two days is enough for the historic core and nothing more, which for a first visit is a reasonable trade. The itinerary above covers Sultanahmet's four major monuments, both bazaars, one imperial mosque beyond the famous pair and an hour on the Bosphorus. What it leaves out is substantial: the Byzantine churches, the Asian side beyond one evening, Dolmabahce, the modern art scene in Karakoy and Beyoglu, and any of the islands. Istanbul rewards a longer stay more than most cities, because the thing that makes it remarkable is how ordinary the layers are once you stop monument-hunting.

What should I wear to visit Hagia Sophia and the Blue Mosque?

Both are working mosques, so the same rules apply at each. Everyone covers shoulders and knees - no vest tops, no shorts above the knee. Women also cover their hair; scarves are generally offered free at the door, but your own is quicker and cleaner. Everyone removes their shoes before stepping onto the carpet, so wear socks and shoes you can slip off without sitting down. Visitor entry pauses for each of the five daily prayers and is busiest around Friday midday. None of this requires special clothing - a scarf and long trousers cover every mosque in the city.

Do I need to book Istanbul's museums in advance?

For Topkapi Palace and the Basilica Cistern, booking ahead is worthwhile in high season, and Hagia Sophia's visitor route can involve a queue at peak hours. The Blue Mosque and Suleymaniye are active mosques with free entry and no reservation. Buy from the official sites; Sultanahmet has a long-standing problem with resellers charging several times face value for standard tickets, including people approaching tourists in the square itself. Check current hours the week you travel - Ramadan, public holidays and restoration work all shift them, and Topkapi has a weekly closing day. The Grand Bazaar closes on Sundays, which catches out more two-day visitors than anything else on this list, so check which day you are landing on before you fix the order of these two days.

Is the Bosphorus cruise worth it, or should I take a public ferry?

Take the public ferry. The commercial cruises and the municipal ferries use the same water and give you the same view of the palaces, the fortresses and the bridges; the ferry costs a fraction as much, runs far more often, and lets you get off at a village up the strait and have lunch rather than staying on a boat for a fixed loop. Use your transport card. The one argument for a cruise is commentary, and if that is what you want, run an audio guide on your own phone with headphones while the ferry does the moving.

Which side of Istanbul should I stay on?

For a two-day first visit, stay on the European side and ideally within walking distance of Sultanahmet - it removes a commute from both mornings, and the early starts in this itinerary are what keep you ahead of the crowds. The trade-off is that Sultanahmet empties of locals in the evening and the dining is noticeably tourist-oriented. Karakoy and Beyoglu are a good compromise: fifteen minutes from the monuments by tram, much better food and bars. Stay on the Asian side only if you have more time and want a residential base, since the ferry crossing stops being charming around the fourth time.
