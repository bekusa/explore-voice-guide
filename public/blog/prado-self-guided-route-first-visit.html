A Prado Self Guided Route for Your First Visit

6 min readPublished 2026-11-21
In short

Plan a Prado self guided route that moves from Bosch to Velázquez, Goya, and the Black Paintings, with time to look rather than rushing.

The Prado can make you feel late before you have seen a single painting. You enter with a crowd, find several centuries of European art behind the next doorway, and notice people moving quickly toward Las Meninas. A Prado self guided route gives the museum a shape: begin with strange religious vision, move into royal power, then finish with Goya’s distrust of both.

Do not try to see everything. The collection is too large, and the reward for rushing is a sore back and a camera roll full of paintings you did not really look at. Give this route around two and a half hours. It follows a story rather than every room: fear and temptation, courtly display, war, and private darkness.

Before you enter

Use the museum map as a way to find the sections named below, rather than relying on room numbers from an old blog post. Galleries move, rooms close for conservation work, and the Prado has several routes through its main building. The Central Gallery is your useful landmark. It is the long, high spine of the museum, where paintings once made for kings face one another across the light.

Choose one entrance, collect a paper map if one is available, and decide where you will meet anyone traveling with you. Then put your phone away for the first ten minutes. The Prado is easier to understand once your eyes adjust to its scale.

If you want narration without renting an on-site device, download a guide before arriving. The quiet work of looking is part of the visit.

The Prado self guided route in four movements

Start with Bosch and a world on edge

Look first for Hieronymus Bosch’s The Garden of Earthly Delights. It is a triptych, designed to close like shutters, and it pulls you in from a distance with its pale blue horizon and impossible pink structures. Up close, the central panel becomes crowded with naked figures, oversized fruit, birds, musical instruments, and creatures that seem invented after a bad dream.

Do not spend your time trying to identify every symbol. Bosch does not offer a tidy puzzle with one correct answer. Notice the movement instead. The left panel begins with a calm creation scene. The center is bright, busy, and unstable. The right panel drops into a cold, mechanical hell, where bodies and objects have lost their normal purpose.

Stay for ten minutes, perhaps fifteen. Then look at one quieter Northern European painting nearby. The contrast matters. Bosch is not simply eccentric. He belongs to an age that sees daily life, pleasure, punishment, and salvation pressed tightly together.

Move toward Venice, then the Spanish court

As you approach the great Italian and Spanish paintings, the color changes. Venetian art brings warm flesh, heavy fabric, deep red, and skies built from layers of paint. Titian’s portraits are useful preparation for the Prado’s central subject: what power looks like when it wants to be admired.

Then find Diego Velázquez. Start with a royal portrait before going directly to Las Meninas. In portraits of Philip IV and his court, Velázquez makes status visible through restrained details: black silk, a pale hand, a dog, a sash, the empty space around a figure. These are not friendly likenesses. They are carefully controlled public images.

When you reach Las Meninas, stand back first. The canvas is large, but it does not behave like a grand historical scene. A young infanta stands in the foreground. Her attendants pause around her. A dog lies near the floor. Velázquez includes himself at the easel, while a mirror at the back suggests the king and queen standing where you stand.

The painting turns looking into its subject. Who is posing? Who is watching? Who is being painted? The crowd around it can be tiring, so do not fight for the closest position immediately. Watch from the edge, then move forward when there is room. Two or three careful minutes are more useful than twenty minutes of jostling.

The Central Gallery also gives you Rubens. His paintings are full of movement: pale bodies, horses rearing, flags and clouds lifting through the frame. If Velázquez feels cool and watchful, Rubens feels like a room has suddenly filled with music. Choose one large painting and follow a single line of motion through it rather than trying to take in every figure at once.

Let Goya change the temperature

Francisco de Goya is where this route becomes less ceremonial. His early works can be light and sociable, with fashionable clothes, games, and open air. His later paintings record a harsher Spain: violence, fear, old age, greed, and faces that seem worn down by what they have seen.

Find The Third of May 1808. The white-shirted man at its center raises his arms before a firing squad. His pose echoes religious painting, but there is no rescue coming. The soldiers are shown almost without faces, as a dark machine of uniforms and rifles. On the ground, blood catches the light.

This is a painting to view slowly, but not sentimentally. Goya gives the condemned men individual expressions and gestures. The soldiers become a single force. That choice is the painting’s force.

Nearby, look for Saturn Devouring His Son, one of the Black Paintings. It began as wall decoration in Goya’s house, not as a polite museum work. The god’s eyes stare out of the darkness as he tears into a body. The image is difficult, and it is meant to be. If you are visiting with children, you may prefer to skip this room or make it brief.

The Black Paintings are not a neat ending to Goya’s life or to Spanish history. They are private, uncertain, and frightening. That is why they stay with you after the orderly grandeur of the royal portraits.

What to leave out

A first visit does not need every famous name. El Greco, Fra Angelico, Raphael, Murillo, and the museum’s sculpture can each justify an hour of their own. Adding them all to a two-and-a-half-hour route turns the Prado into a checklist.

If you care most about Italian Renaissance painting, shorten Bosch and spend more time with Titian and Raphael. If Spanish art is your reason for coming, begin with Velázquez and give Goya the longest stretch. If you have only one hour, choose The Garden of Earthly Delights, Las Meninas, one Velázquez portrait, The Third of May 1808, and one Black Painting. It is not a complete Prado, but it is a coherent one.

Avoid planning your visit around a photograph of yourself in front of a painting. The busiest rooms are often the least satisfying places to stop. A smaller portrait in a side gallery may give you the stillness that the headline works cannot.

When a human guide is worth paying for

A self-guided visit is better when you want to set your own pace, sit when your feet hurt, return to a painting, or skip a room without explaining yourself. It also works well if you have read a little beforehand and want a clear route rather than a lecture.

A paid human guide can be worth it if you are visiting with a group that has very different interests, if you want sustained discussion, or if you are studying art history and need help comparing technique across paintings. A good guide can point out brushwork, restoration history, and visual jokes that an audio track may pass over. The trade-off is fixed timing and less freedom to stay with the one work that catches you.

Leave a little time at the end for one unscripted choice. Walk back through the Central Gallery, turn into the room that looks quieter, and stop where the color changes your pace. The Prado is most memorable when one painting, not the whole museum, follows you back onto the street.
