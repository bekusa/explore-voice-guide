Free Audio Guide Apps: What You Actually Get Without Paying

Last verified October 20267 min readLokali Journal
Short answer

Most free audio guide apps fall into four groups: museum-issued apps, city tourism apps, AI-generated guides and podcast-style tours. Museum apps are the most accurate but cover one building. City apps are free and shallow. AI guides cover almost anywhere in many languages. Podcast tours are well-made but English-only and few.

Paid audio guides at a major museum cost roughly as much as the entry ticket itself, and the rental desk is usually the longest queue in the building. So the question most travellers actually have is narrow: what can I get for nothing, and what am I giving up?

Here is the honest version, category by category. Full disclosure: we make one of these apps, which is named in the fourth category below.

1. The museum's own app

Most large institutions now publish a free app or a browser guide for their own collection. This is almost always the most accurate audio you will find anywhere - it is written by the curators who catalogued the objects, and it is updated when a room is rehung or a work goes out on loan.

The limits are equally clear. It covers one building and nothing outside it, so a week in a city means downloading six separate apps. Language coverage is typically five to eight languages, which is generous for European visitors and useless if you read Georgian, Thai or Filipino. And many of them expect you to be on the museum's wifi, which means the audio stops working the moment you step into the garden.

Use it when: you are spending half a day or more inside one serious collection, and your language is on their list.

2. The city or national tourism app

Tourism boards fund these, so they are genuinely free and usually ad-free. They tend to be strongest on logistics - transport passes, event calendars, a map of public toilets - and weakest on the thing you actually wanted, which is someone telling you what you are looking at.

A typical entry runs two or three paragraphs of promotional prose with no audio at all. When there is audio, it is often a single city-wide introduction rather than a stop-by-stop guide. Treat these as a planning tool you use the night before, not as a guide you listen to on site.

Use it when: you need opening times, transport and a decent offline map in one place.

3. AI-generated audio guides

This is the newest category and the one with the widest coverage. Instead of a recording studio producing a fixed set of tours, the guide is generated for whatever place you are standing in front of, in whatever language you set. That changes the economics: a small team can cover a village church in Kakheti as easily as the Louvre, and cover both in forty languages.

The trade-off is accuracy, and it is a real one. A generated guide can be confidently wrong about a date, a name or an attribution in a way that a curator-written script is not. The apps worth using in this category are the ones that ground their text in a verifiable source - Wikipedia, Wikidata, museum catalogues - rather than generating from scratch, and that are open about doing so.

This is the category our own app, Lokali, sits in. It is free, it works in the language you set rather than the language the building happens to offer, and its written companion pages are public: you can read the guide for the Colosseum, Hagia Sophia or Narikala Fortress in your browser before you decide whether to install anything. We would rather you check the writing first.

Use it when: you are travelling somewhere without a strong guide industry, or you want one app for a multi-country trip, or your language is not one of the big eight.

4. Podcast and narrated walking tours

A handful of independent producers make genuinely excellent narrated walks - scripted, scored, performed by someone who can hold a story for an hour. At their best they are better company than any museum handset, and many release a free sample tour per city.

There are two catches. The catalogue is small, because each tour is a real production: a city either has one or it does not. And they are overwhelmingly English-only, occasionally Spanish or German. Download before you travel, because they are large files.

Use it when: your city is on their list, you read English comfortably, and you have a free evening to walk.

Where the paywall usually sits

Across all four categories, the free tier almost always breaks at the same three points. First, offline downloads - streaming is free, keeping the audio is not, which matters most precisely when you are abroad without data. Second, language: the free tier ships two or three languages and charges for the rest. Third, route length - the first two or three stops are free, the remaining twelve are a purchase.

Before you install anything, check those three. An app that is free for the first three stops is not a free audio guide; it is a sample.

A practical shortlist

  • Check the museum's own site first - if your language is listed, that audio will be the most accurate you can get for that building.
  • Download anything you need offline while you still have wifi. Audio guides are the single most common thing travellers discover they cannot load.
  • Test one stop before you commit to an app for a whole trip. Read or listen to a single guide and see whether the writing is specific or generic.
  • Be sceptical of any guide quoting prices and opening hours. Those change constantly and are the first thing to go stale - check the official site.
  • Bring wired headphones if you can. Bluetooth pairing in a crowded gallery is a reliable waste of five minutes.

If you want to see what a generated guide reads like before installing anything, every attraction page on this site is the written version of the same guide the app reads aloud. Start with a city you already know well - that is the fastest way to judge whether a guide is any good.

Frequently asked

Are free audio guide apps actually any good?

The good ones are genuinely good, but they are good at different things. A museum's own free app will usually beat any paid third-party guide for that specific building, because curators wrote it. A free AI guide will cover far more places in far more languages, with a real accuracy trade-off you should know about. A free city tourism app is best for logistics and weakest for commentary. The failure mode to watch for is not low quality but narrow scope: an app that is excellent for three stops and empty for the rest of your trip. Test one guide in a place you already know before trusting an app with a city you do not.

Do I need an internet connection to use an audio guide app?

For streaming, yes. For listening offline, it depends on the app, and this is the single most common thing travellers get caught by. Many free tiers let you stream audio over wifi or data but reserve downloads for a paid plan, which means the guide stops working exactly where you need it - inside a thick-walled basilica, underground in a cistern, or abroad without roaming. Before you travel, open the app on your home wifi, download the places you plan to visit, then switch your phone to airplane mode and check that the audio still plays. Two minutes of testing at home saves an afternoon of frustration.

Why do museums charge for audio guides when free apps exist?

The rental fee covers the handset, the staff at the desk, cleaning and replacement, and the original script and recording - usually in eight or more languages with professional voice talent. It is a real cost, not pure margin. What has changed is that most museums now also publish the same or similar content free through their own app or mobile site, because they would rather you use their commentary than a stranger's. It is worth checking the official site before you queue: the free version is frequently the same script, and sometimes a more current one.

Is it rude to listen to an audio guide inside a church or mosque?

Not at all, provided you use headphones and keep the volume where only you can hear it. What is genuinely disruptive is a phone speaker, a loud pause-and-discuss conversation, or standing in a doorway while you catch up with the narration. In active places of worship, stop the audio entirely during prayer and step aside from the prayer area - in many mosques, including Hagia Sophia, visitor access pauses for prayer times and the dress code applies regardless of what you are listening to. One wired earbud is the most considerate option: you hear the guide and still hear the room.
